Part 3 · Selling

Order to cash: five documents, each with its effect

Order to cash. Five documents. Click each one. A quotation and a sales order change neither stock nor the books; the order records what the customer is owed. The delivery note takes the goods out of stock and, with perpetual inventory, posts their cost: debit cost of goods sold, credit stock in hand. The sales invoice creates the receivable: debit debtors, credit sales. The payment entry clears it against the bank. These are the same documents WenQuest Ops reads to compute on-time delivery and receivables.

Part 3 · Buying

Procure to pay: from a request to a paid supplier

Procure to pay is the mirror image. A material request, then a purchase order with a promised date. The purchase receipt brings the goods into stock: debit stock in hand, credit stock received but not billed, a holding account until the supplier's invoice arrives. The purchase invoice moves that to accounts payable, and the payment entry clears it. The receipt is also where our incoming inspection starts, and where we measure whether the supplier was on time.

Part 3 · Accounting · post the transactions in order

Every document posts balanced entries

Accounting, live. Post the six transactions in order: receive the bolts, the supplier's invoice, pay the supplier, ship to the customer, invoice the customer, and the customer pays. Watch the accounts. Every posting has equal debits and credits, so the trial balance at the bottom always balances. Stock in hand goes up on receipt and down on delivery. The holding account for goods received but not billed clears when the invoice arrives. At the end the bank shows the margin on this one sale. Next page: the statements built from these same entries.

Part 3 · Inventory

Stock is a ledger: every movement, quantity and value

Valuation:
In ERPNext, stock is a ledger. Each receipt and each delivery is a line with a quantity, a rate and the running value, item by item and warehouse by warehouse. Receive 100 bolts at 40 cents, then 100 at 60 cents, then ship 120. Switch between FIFO and moving average: the quantity is the same, but the cost of the 120 shipped, and the value left on the shelf, differ. WenQuest Ops sums this ledger to get stock on hand and inventory value. It never estimates them.

Part 3 · Inventory · change the numbers

FIFO or moving average: the same shipment, two costs

Change the numbers. FIFO costs the shipment at the oldest receipts first. Moving average costs it at the average of everything on hand. When prices rise, FIFO shows lower cost of goods and higher stock value; moving average smooths it. The method is set per item or company in ERPNext and your accountant decides it; WenQuest Ops simply reads the values ERPNext computed. If you try to ship more than you have, it says so instead of inventing a cost.

Part 4 · The hub · toggle the orders

ON-TIME DELIVERY

Orders fully shipped in the period whose last delivery date is on or before the promised date.

DAYS ON HAND

Inventory value ÷ (cost of goods shipped in the last 90 days ÷ 90).

Every number on the dashboard has a published formula you can check by hand. On-time delivery: of the orders fully shipped in the period, how many shipped on or before the promised date. Part 10 · A vision · 1 of 4 · Vision

The future factory, at a glance

This is a factory of the future. An order comes in, and the whole plant starts moving on its own. Materials leave the automated warehouse, every tote scanned by barcode and QR code, and driverless carts carry them to the line. Robot arms take over: loading, fastening, assembling, one after another. Every product passes a machine-vision station: image, measure, decide, in the blink of an eye. Rejects are pushed off the line; good parts get an automatically generated FDA UDI label, a 2D code plus human-readable text, then a verifying scan, and they ship. Data from every machine flows, live, into one data hub.

Part 10 · A vision · 2 of 4 · Vision

Machine vision: automatic inspection

Zoom in on the inspection station. Camera above, ring light on, parts flowing underneath. It first reads the 2D code on the part to know its lot, then turns the image into dimensions: hole size, spacing, scratches, each checked against the drawing. All in tolerance: green light, pass. One out: a pusher sends it into the quarantine bin. Every reading goes onto a control chart. When the trend drifts, it alarms before a single bad part. AI drafts the nonconformance report, but the disposition takes effect only when an engineer approves.

Part 10 · A vision · 3 of 4 · Vision

Every gauge, collected automatically

The inspection station is more than a camera. A coordinate measuring machine; a 3D scanner on five axes, X, Y and Z plus a tilting head and a rotary table, moving together to cover the whole part; and a roughness tester. Their results come back automatically. No copying by hand. Calipers, micrometers and custom gauges send wirelessly: one press, and the reading lands in the record, tied to the part and the feature. Every reading records which gauge took it. If that gauge is past its calibration date, the reading is refused and the gauge is flagged for recalibration. All readings come together in one table, judged automatically, plotted on control charts, and sent on to the data hub.

Part 10 · A vision · 4 of 4 · Vision

The data hub: a closed loop

Finally, the hub: orders, inventory, machines and quality, in one connected web of data. A digital twin draws the whole plant live on screen: where it's slow, where it's jammed, at a glance. When something's off, AI suggests a fix: change the tool, slow down, restock. A person approves at the key step, robots carry it out, and the results flow back to the hub. A closed loop. Fully integrated, fully automated. People are freed from hauling and watching, to focus on judgment.
