The main thread of this chapter can be summed up in one sentence: **any rotation is a turn through some angle about some axis**; everything else revolves around this sentence, expressing, computing and using it with different mathematical tools.

We began with rotations in the plane and saw that a rotation can be carried out by a matrix, that the columns of the matrix are the turned coordinate axes, and that the inverse of the matrix is its transpose. In space, the matrices satisfying $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ and $\det R = 1$ form the group SO(3). It is closed under multiplication but not commutative: the order of rotations in space cannot be exchanged at will. Rotations about fixed axes premultiply, rotations about body axes postmultiply; this rule runs through all the chapters that follow.

Euler's theorem asserts that the axis of rotation always exists; algebraically, 1 must be an eigenvalue of a rotation matrix, and the angle is given by the trace. Rodrigues' formula goes the other way, writing down the rotation matrix from the axis and the angle. Its second derivation, obtaining the matrix exponential from the differential equation of rotation, links rotations with skew-symmetric matrices; exponential coordinates $\hat\omega\theta$ thereby become the "common language" for describing rotations, and the starting point of the screw theory of Chapter 6.

Euler angles describe an orientation by three elementary rotations. They are intuitive, but they depend on a convention and lose a degree of freedom at gimbal lock. Every representation with only three numbers has a singularity of some kind. Quaternions use one number more and gain in return freedom from singularities everywhere, fast composition and easy normalization; the price is the double cover: q and −q represent the same rotation.

In applications, the best transition between two orientations is a uniform turn about one axis along the geodesic, which written with quaternions is spherical linear interpolation; the difference between two orientations is best measured by the error angle, with the error vector given by the logarithm map; and after long cumulative computations the matrix must be orthonormalized at regular intervals, with the singular value decomposition giving the nearest rotation matrix.

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| Rotation matrix | Its columns are the turned coordinate axes; its inverse is its transpose |
| SO(3) | The group of all rotation matrices in space; multiplication does not commute |
| Euler's rotation theorem | Any rotation is a turn through some angle about some axis |
| Exponential coordinates | A three-dimensional vector whose direction is the axis and whose length is the angle |
| Gimbal lock | Euler angles lose a degree of freedom at particular orientations: Cardan angles (such as ZYX) when the middle angle is ±90°, proper Euler angles (such as ZYZ) when the middle angle is 0 or 180° |
| Double cover | q and −q represent the same rotation |
| Geodesic | The shortest path between two orientations, a uniform turn about one axis |

### Historical notes

| Year | Person | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| 1775 | Leonhard Euler | Proved that every motion of a rigid body about a fixed point has a fixed axis |
| 1830 | Michel Chasles | Extended Euler's theorem to general rigid-body motion (Chapter 6) |
| 1840 | Olinde Rodrigues | A concise geometric form of the axis–angle formula; the four-parameter rule for composing rotations |
| 1843 | William Rowan Hamilton | Discovered the quaternions |
| 1858 | Arthur Cayley | Set out matrices and their operations systematically |
| 1985 | Ken Shoemake | Brought spherical linear interpolation into computer animation |
