### 4.6.1 A representation with four numbers

The representations introduced in the previous sections each have strengths and weaknesses. The rotation matrix has no singularities, but it uses 9 numbers for 3 degrees of freedom; Euler angles and exponential coordinates use only 3 numbers, but they become singular at gimbal lock and at an angle of π respectively. Section 4.5.5 pointed out that this is not a flaw of one particular method: with only 3 numbers it is impossible to describe all spatial rotations without a singularity somewhere.

What, then, if we use one number more? That is exactly what the quaternions of this section do: they describe a rotation with 4 numbers, among which there is only one constraint, so the redundancy is small, and there is no singularity anywhere. Composing two rotations takes a single quaternion product, less work than a matrix product. Because of these advantages, today's robot controllers, drone flight controllers, phone attitude sensors and 3D game engines almost all store and compute orientations internally as quaternions.

### 4.6.2 From complex numbers to quaternions

The story of quaternions begins with complex numbers. A complex number $a + b\,i$ can be regarded as the point (a, b) of the plane. Multiplying it by $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ turns this point through exactly θ about the origin. So rotations in the plane can be carried out by multiplying complex numbers.

The nineteenth-century Irish mathematician W. R. Hamilton wanted to extend complex numbers to three dimensions, looking for "triplets" that could also be multiplied and divided; he searched for years without success. On 16 October 1843, as he was walking along a canal in Dublin, the idea suddenly came to him: three components were not enough; he needed three "imaginary units" plus a real part, four components in all, and he had to give up the commutative law of multiplication. On the spot he carved the basic formula into the stone of Brougham Bridge, which he was passing:

$$
i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1 . \tag{4.6.1}
$$

Numbers of this kind came to be called **quaternions**.

It is worth mentioning that Rodrigues, in a paper of 1840, had already described rotations by four parameters and given the rule by which these four parameters combine when two rotations are composed; that rule is exactly the later quaternion product, except that he did not regard the parameters as a kind of "number". Today these four parameters are called the **Euler–Rodrigues parameters**, because Euler, in a paper of 1770, had also found a way of representing orthogonal matrices by four parameters. Representing a rotation by "sandwiching" a vector between quaternions was published by Cayley (1845) and by Hamilton in turn. We shall see in this section that the Euler–Rodrigues parameters and the unit quaternions are one and the same thing.

### 4.6.3 Operations on quaternions

**Definition 4.6.1 (Quaternion)** A number of the form

$$
q = q_0 + q_1 i + q_2 j + q_3 k \tag{4.6.2}
$$

is called a quaternion, where $q_0, q_1, q_2, q_3$ are real numbers and i, j, k satisfy Eq. (4.6.1). $q_0$ is called the real part and $(q_1, q_2, q_3)$ the imaginary part. By the notation conventions we write $q = (q_0, q_1, q_2, q_3)$, with the real part first.

From Eq. (4.6.1) we can derive the rules for multiplying the three imaginary units. Take ij first: multiplying both sides of ijk = −1 on the right by k and using $k^2 = -1$ gives $-ij = -k$, that is, ij = k. Next ik: multiplying both sides of ij = k on the left by i and using $i^2 = -1$ gives $-j = ik$, that is, ik = −j. Next kj: multiplying both sides of ij = k on the right by j and using $j^2 = -1$ gives $-i = kj$, that is, kj = −i. The rest can be obtained in the same way (Exercise 4.6.1). The result is

$$
ij = k,\quad jk = i,\quad ki = j; \qquad ji = -k,\quad kj = -i,\quad ik = -j . \tag{4.6.3}
$$

In the first three, taken in the cyclic order i→j→k→i, the product is positive; reversing the order changes the sign. The reader will notice that this corresponds exactly to the cross products of the unit vectors of the three coordinate axes, $\hat x \times \hat y = \hat z$ and so on. This is no coincidence, as we shall see at once.

Write a quaternion in the form "real part + vector part", $q = (q_0, \vec q)$, where $\vec q = (q_1, q_2, q_3)$ is a three-dimensional vector. Multiplying two quaternions, expanding by the distributive law and tidying up with Eq. (4.6.3), we obtain (Exercise 4.6.1)

$$
(a_0, \vec a)(b_0, \vec b) = \big(a_0 b_0 - \vec a \cdot \vec b,\ \ a_0 \vec b + b_0 \vec a + \vec a \times \vec b\big) . \tag{4.6.4}
$$

This formula turns the quaternion product into the familiar dot and cross products, and all the derivations that follow rest on it. Since the cross product does not commute, $\vec a \times \vec b = -\vec b \times \vec a$, the quaternion product does not commute in general either.

Three more notions are needed:

- **Conjugate**: $q^* = (q_0, -\vec q)$, that is, the imaginary part changes sign;
- **Norm**: $|q| = \sqrt{q_0^2 + q_1^2 + q_2^2 + q_3^2}$; from Eq. (4.6.4) one can check that $q\,q^* = (|q|^2, \vec 0)$;
- **Unit quaternion**: a quaternion of norm 1. The inverse of a unit quaternion is its conjugate, $q^{-1} = q^*$, just as the inverse of a rotation matrix is its transpose.

### 4.6.4 Unit quaternions represent rotations

Regard a three-dimensional vector v as the quaternion $(0, v)$ with zero real part; this is called a pure quaternion.

**Theorem 4.6.1** Let q be the unit quaternion

$$
q = \Big(\cos\frac{\theta}{2},\ \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,\hat\omega\Big) , \tag{4.6.5}
$$

where $\hat\omega$ is a unit vector. Then for every vector v, the v′ given by

$$
(0, v') = q\,(0, v)\,q^* \tag{4.6.6}
$$

is exactly v turned through θ about $\hat\omega$.

*Proof*　Write $q = (q_0, \vec q)$. First compute, by Eq. (4.6.4), $(0, v)\,q^* = (0, v)(q_0, -\vec q) = (v \cdot \vec q,\ q_0 v - v \times \vec q)$. Then multiply on the left by q, again by Eq. (4.6.4). The real part is

$$
q_0 (v \cdot \vec q) - \vec q \cdot (q_0 v - v \times \vec q) = 0 ,
$$

because $\vec q \cdot (v \times \vec q) = 0$. So the result is again a pure quaternion. The vector part is

$$
q_0 (q_0 v - v \times \vec q) + (v\cdot\vec q)\,\vec q + \vec q \times (q_0 v - v \times \vec q) .
$$

Using $-v \times \vec q = \vec q \times v$ and the vector triple product formula $\vec q \times (\vec q \times v) = \vec q\,(\vec q \cdot v) - v\,|\vec q|^2$, this tidies up to

$$
v' = (q_0^2 - |\vec q|^2)\, v + 2 q_0\, (\vec q \times v) + 2\, (\vec q \cdot v)\, \vec q .
$$

Finally substitute Eq. (4.6.5): $q_0^2 - |\vec q|^2 = \cos^2\frac{\theta}{2} - \sin^2\frac{\theta}{2} = \cos\theta$, $2q_0\vec q = 2\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,\hat\omega = \sin\theta\,\hat\omega$, $2(\vec q\cdot v)\vec q = 2\sin^2\frac{\theta}{2}(\hat\omega\cdot v)\hat\omega = (1 - \cos\theta)(\hat\omega\cdot v)\hat\omega$. Hence

$$
v' = v\cos\theta + (\hat\omega \times v)\sin\theta + \hat\omega\,(\hat\omega \cdot v)(1 - \cos\theta) ,
$$

which is exactly the vector form (4.4.2) of Rodrigues' formula.　∎

The four components $(q_0, q_1, q_2, q_3)$ of Eq. (4.6.5) are the Euler–Rodrigues parameters.

Why the **half angle** θ/2? Because q appears twice in Eq. (4.6.6), and v′ is quadratic in q. The proof shows that once cos(θ/2) and sin(θ/2) have been multiplied in pairs, the double-angle formulas turn them into cos θ and sin θ; for the final angle of rotation to be θ, q can only contain the half angle. The half angle has an important consequence:

**Double cover.** q and −q give the same rotation, because in Eq. (4.6.6) the two minus signs cancel. From Eq. (4.6.5), when the angle θ increases by 2π, cos(θ/2) and sin(θ/2) both change sign and q becomes −q; only when the angle increases by 4π does q return to its original value. In other words, each rotation corresponds to exactly two unit quaternions, negatives of each other (Figure 4.6.1). This is an essential difference between the unit quaternions and SO(3), and it must be handled with care in interpolation and control (Section 4.7).

### 4.6.5 Composing rotations is multiplying quaternions

To avoid confusion with the components $q_0, q_1, \ldots$, let p and q denote two different unit quaternions. First carry out the rotation p, then the rotation q. By Eq. (4.6.6),

$$
q\,\big(p\,(0, v)\,p^*\big)\,q^* = (q p)\,(0, v)\,(q p)^* ,
$$

where two properties are used: quaternion multiplication is associative, so the brackets can be dropped freely (this can be checked term by term from Eq. (4.6.4) together with the scalar and vector triple product formulas, Exercise 4.6.6); and $(qp)^* = p^* q^*$ (Exercise 4.6.2). So the composite rotation corresponds to the quaternion $qp$. As with rotation matrices, the later rotation is written on the left.

**Example 4.6.1** Redo method A of Example 4.2.2 with quaternions: first turn 90° about the x axis, then 90° about the z axis. By Eq. (4.6.5),

$$
q_x = {{ex4_6.qx}}, \qquad q_z = {{ex4_6.qz}} .
$$

Computing $q_z q_x$ by Eq. (4.6.4) gives

$$
q = q_z\,q_x = {{ex4_6.q}} .
$$

The real part is $\cos(\theta/2) = 0.5$, so θ/2 = 60° and θ = 120°; the imaginary parts are all equal, so the axis is $(1, 1, 1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt 3$. This agrees completely with the conclusion of Example 4.4.1. If the order is reversed, $q_x q_z = {{ex4_6.q_rev}}$: the axis has changed, confirming once more that rotations in space do not commute.

To compose two rotations, a quaternion product needs {{ex4_6.mul_quat}} multiplications and {{ex4_6.add_quat}} additions, while a 3×3 matrix product needs {{ex4_6.mul_matrix}} multiplications and {{ex4_6.add_matrix}} additions. In a controller that computes thousands upon thousands of times a second, the difference is real. But to turn a single vector, a rotation matrix times a vector needs only 9 multiplications, which is actually less work than the two quaternion products of Eq. (4.6.6); so practical programs often "compose with quaternions, apply with matrices".

### 4.6.6 Converting between quaternions and rotation matrices

**The rotation matrix from the quaternion.** Write the result $v' = (q_0^2 - |\vec q|^2) v + 2q_0(\vec q \times v) + 2(\vec q\cdot v)\vec q$ obtained in the proof of Theorem 4.6.1 as a matrix times v. Take the top-left entry as an example: it is the x component of v′ when $v = (1, 0, 0)^{\mathsf T}$, which equals $q_0^2 - |\vec q|^2 + 2q_1^2 = q_0^2 + q_1^2 - q_2^2 - q_3^2$, and using $q_0^2 + |\vec q|^2 = 1$ this becomes $1 - 2(q_2^2 + q_3^2)$. The other entries follow in the same way, giving

$$
R = \begin{pmatrix}
1 - 2(q_2^2 + q_3^2) & 2(q_1q_2 - q_0q_3) & 2(q_1q_3 + q_0q_2) \\
2(q_1q_2 + q_0q_3) & 1 - 2(q_1^2 + q_3^2) & 2(q_2q_3 - q_0q_1) \\
2(q_1q_3 - q_0q_2) & 2(q_2q_3 + q_0q_1) & 1 - 2(q_1^2 + q_2^2)
\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.6.7}
$$

Note that every entry of the matrix is quadratic in q, so q and −q give the same R, in agreement with the double cover.

**The quaternion from the rotation matrix.** From Eq. (4.6.7), $\mathrm{tr}\,R = 3 - 4|\vec q|^2 = 4q_0^2 - 1$, so $q_0 = \frac12\sqrt{1 + \mathrm{tr}\,R}$ (taking the plus sign, that is, choosing from q and −q the one with non-negative real part). Then from the skew-symmetric part of R,

$$
q_1 = \frac{r_{32} - r_{23}}{4q_0}, \qquad q_2 = \frac{r_{13} - r_{31}}{4q_0}, \qquad q_3 = \frac{r_{21} - r_{12}}{4q_0} . \tag{4.6.8}
$$

When $q_0$ is close to zero, that is, when the angle is close to 180°, these formulas divide by a very small number and magnify the error. A safer approach uses the other relations contained in Eq. (4.6.7). From the diagonal entries,

$$
4q_0^2 = 1 + \mathrm{tr}\,R,\quad 4q_1^2 = 1 + 2r_{11} - \mathrm{tr}\,R,\quad 4q_2^2 = 1 + 2r_{22} - \mathrm{tr}\,R,\quad 4q_3^2 = 1 + 2r_{33} - \mathrm{tr}\,R ; \tag{4.6.9}
$$

and from the sums and differences of the off-diagonal entries,

$$
\begin{aligned}
4q_0q_1 &= r_{32} - r_{23}, & 4q_0q_2 &= r_{13} - r_{31}, & 4q_0q_3 &= r_{21} - r_{12}, \\
4q_1q_2 &= r_{12} + r_{21}, & 4q_1q_3 &= r_{13} + r_{31}, & 4q_2q_3 &= r_{23} + r_{32} .
\end{aligned} \tag{4.6.10}
$$

First compute the four squares by Eq. (4.6.9) and take the square root of the largest of them, which gives that component; then use the three formulas of Eq. (4.6.10) that contain it, dividing by 4 times this component, to find the other three components. Since the divisor is the largest of the four components, it is at least 1/2, and the error is not magnified. Program 4.6.1 uses this approach and tests all four cases.

**Example 4.6.2** Find the unit quaternion corresponding to the rotation matrix of Example 4.3.1,

$$
R = {{ex4_6.R}} .
$$

The method above gives

$$
q = {{ex4_6.qR}} .
$$

From $q_0 = \cos(\theta/2) \approx {{ex4_6.q0R}}$ we get θ ≈ {{ex4_6.theta_deg:.2f}}°, and the axis is $\vec q / \sin(\theta/2) = {{ex4_6.w}}$, in agreement with the results of Sections 4.3 and 4.4.

### 4.6.7 Strengths of quaternions, and points to watch

The strengths of quaternions come down to four points: only 1 number more than 3, so the redundancy is small; no singularity anywhere; little computation for composition; and when numerical error pulls a quaternion away from unit length, dividing it by its own norm is enough to restore it, much simpler than orthonormalizing a rotation matrix. The next section will show a fifth strength: quaternions are the best tool for smooth interpolation between two orientations.

There are also three points to watch. First, the four numbers have no intuitive meaning and are unsuitable for user interfaces. Second, the double cover: to compare whether two quaternions represent the same orientation, both q and −q must be considered. Third, the conventions are not uniform: this book and most mathematics books write the real part first, while the message format of the Robot Operating System ROS stores them in the order (x, y, z, w), with the real part w last. When data are exchanged, getting the order wrong is a common source of errors.

**Engineer's notes**

- Before reading a quaternion, make sure of three things: whether the real part is first or last; whether it represents $R_{sb}$ or $R_{bs}$; and whether it has been normalized.
- When updating a quaternion by integrating the angular velocity, normalize it once after every step; the cost is tiny, and it prevents long-term drift.
- To judge the difference between the orientations represented by two quaternions p and q, use $|p \cdot q|$ (the absolute value of the four-dimensional dot product); do not compare the components directly.
