### 4.5.1 Describing an orientation by three elementary rotations

A rotation matrix has nine numbers and exponential coordinates have three, but neither fits human intuition well: given a set of exponential coordinates, it is hard to picture at once which way the body is facing. Pilots, ship captains and robot operators are used to another way of putting it: how many degrees the nose has been raised, how many degrees the body is banked to the right, how many degrees the heading has turned. This way of speaking breaks an arbitrary rotation into three rotations, about three coordinate axes in turn, each described by a single angle. The three angles are called **Euler angles**, because Euler was the first to use them systematically in his study of the rotation of rigid bodies.

On the teach pendant of an industrial robot, the orientation of the end-effector is almost always displayed and entered as three angles. An engineer must therefore be thoroughly at home with Euler angles: how they are defined, how they are converted to and from rotation matrices, and when they break down.

### 4.5.2 The conventions for Euler angles

There are many ways to choose the three axes of the three rotations. Two consecutive rotations may not be about the same axis, or the two would merge into one and only two degrees of freedom would remain. Under this restriction there are three axes to choose from each time, with consecutive axes different, giving 3 × 2 × 2 = 12 orders; and each order can be carried out about fixed axes or about body axes.

The 12 orders fall into two classes. In the first, the first and last rotations are about the same axis, as in z-y-z and z-x-z; these are called **proper Euler angles**. In the second, the three rotations are about three different axes, as in z-y-x and x-y-z; these are called **Cardan angles**, also Tait–Bryan angles. In aviation and robotics the most common choice is the z-y-x order from the second class.

**ZYZ Euler angles.**　First turn through α about $\hat z$, then through β about the new $\hat y$, and finally through γ about the new $\hat z$; all three are about body axes. By Theorem 4.2.2, rotations about body axes postmultiply in turn:

$$
R = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \alpha)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat y, \beta)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \gamma) . \tag{4.5.1}
$$

The angles Euler used in his study of rigid-body rotation were exactly of this class, with the same axis first and last (physics books often take the z-x-z order); the three angles correspond to the precession, nutation and spin of a top. In robotics, the three joint axes of the wrist of many six-axis arms (the so-called spherical wrist) are arranged like "z-y-z"; once the zero offsets and the positive directions of the joints are allowed for, the three wrist joint angles correspond to a set of ZYZ Euler angles. Some manufacturers' manuals call such a wrist "roll-pitch-roll".

**Roll–pitch–yaw**.　Let the body frame {b} start out coincident with {s}. First turn through φ about the fixed axis $\hat x_s$; this is called **roll**. Then turn through θ about the fixed axis $\hat y_s$; this is called **pitch**. Finally turn through ψ about the fixed axis $\hat z_s$; this is called **yaw**. Together they are called the roll–pitch–yaw angles, RPY angles for short, from the initials of roll, pitch and yaw. By the notation conventions, in this section and wherever Euler angles are involved, θ means the pitch angle; when this book writes ZYX Euler angles, it always does so in the order (ψ, θ, φ), that is, yaw, pitch, roll. By Theorem 4.2.2, rotations about fixed axes premultiply in turn:

$$
R = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \psi)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat y, \theta)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, \varphi) . \tag{4.5.2}
$$

Eq. (4.5.2) can also be read another way. Read from left to right, it says "first turn through ψ about the body z axis, then through θ about the body y axis, and finally through φ about the body x axis". The two readings give the same matrix; this is just the result of Exercise 4.2.3, which we state here as a theorem.

**Theorem 4.5.1** Turning through φ, θ, ψ about the fixed axes x, y, z in turn gives the same result as turning through ψ, θ, φ about the body axes z, y, x in turn; both equal Eq. (4.5.2).

*Proof*　By Theorem 4.2.2, the three rotations about fixed axes premultiply in turn, and the product is $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \psi)\mathrm{Rot}(\hat y, \theta)\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, \varphi)$; the three rotations about body axes postmultiply in turn, and the product is $I\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \psi)\mathrm{Rot}(\hat y, \theta)\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, \varphi)$. The two are the same.　∎

This is why RPY angles are also often called "ZYX Euler angles". Some books and software describe them in terms of fixed axes, others in terms of body axes; the words differ, the matrix is the same.

### 4.5.3 The rotation matrix from the Euler angles

Multiplying the three elementary rotation matrices in turn, and writing $c_\psi = \cos\psi$, $s_\psi = \sin\psi$ and so on, we get

$$
R = \begin{pmatrix}
c_\psi c_\theta & c_\psi s_\theta s_\varphi - s_\psi c_\varphi & c_\psi s_\theta c_\varphi + s_\psi s_\varphi \\
s_\psi c_\theta & s_\psi s_\theta s_\varphi + c_\psi c_\varphi & s_\psi s_\theta c_\varphi - c_\psi s_\varphi \\
-s_\theta & c_\theta s_\varphi & c_\theta c_\varphi
\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.5.3}
$$

The reader is advised to carry out the multiplication by hand once. There is no need to memorize this matrix, but one should be able to derive it and should notice its structure: the first column contains only ψ and θ, the third row contains only θ and φ, and the bottom-left entry contains only θ. The inverse computation below relies on exactly this structure.

### 4.5.4 The Euler angles from the rotation matrix

Suppose the entries $r_{ij}$ of R (row i, column j) are known and ψ, θ, φ are wanted. Comparing with Eq. (4.5.3):

- the bottom-left entry $r_{31} = -\sin\theta$ fixes the pitch angle θ;
- the first two entries of the first column, $r_{11} = \cos\psi\cos\theta$ and $r_{21} = \sin\psi\cos\theta$, give ψ through their ratio;
- the last two entries of the third row, $r_{32} = \cos\theta\sin\varphi$ and $r_{33} = \cos\theta\cos\varphi$, give φ through their ratio.

Taking the arctangent of a ratio hides a trap. $\arctan(y/x)$ can only give angles between −90° and 90°, and it cannot tell (x, y) from (−x, −y); 45° and −135°, for instance, have the same tangent. For this reason programming languages all provide a two-argument function $\mathrm{atan2}(y, x)$: it decides the quadrant from the signs of x and y separately and returns the one correct angle between −180° and 180°.

In addition, the sign of cos θ cannot be determined from $r_{31}$. If we agree that $\cos\theta \ge 0$, that is, θ ∈ [−90°, 90°], then $\cos\theta = \sqrt{r_{11}^2 + r_{21}^2}$. Hence

$$
\theta = \mathrm{atan2}\Big(-r_{31},\ \sqrt{r_{11}^2 + r_{21}^2}\Big), \quad
\psi = \mathrm{atan2}(r_{21},\ r_{11}), \quad
\varphi = \mathrm{atan2}(r_{32},\ r_{33}) . \tag{4.5.4}
$$

If we agree instead that cos θ < 0, we obtain another solution, $(\psi + 180^\circ,\ 180^\circ - \theta,\ \varphi + 180^\circ)$, which gives the same rotation matrix. So even in the normal case the Euler angles have two solutions.

At first sight this result is surprising: R was obtained by turning 30° about the z axis and then 45° about the fixed x axis, yet its RPY angles are not (ψ, θ, φ) = (30°, 0°, 45°). The reason is the order. Eq. (4.5.2) prescribes fixed axes in the order x, then y, then z, while Example 4.2.3 used fixed axes in the order z, then x; the two orders differ, so naturally the three angles differ as well. In fact, (ψ, θ, φ) = (30°, 0°, 45°) gives exactly the "body axes" result of Example 4.2.3. This reminds us once again: **the three numbers of a set of Euler angles mean nothing apart from the agreed order.**

### 4.5.5 Gimbal lock

Euler angles have a fundamental flaw. When the pitch angle is θ = ±90°, Eq. (4.5.4) breaks down: then cos θ = 0, $r_{11}$, $r_{21}$, $r_{32}$, $r_{33}$ are all zero, and atan2(0, 0) is undefined.

This is not merely a problem with the formula. Take θ = 90°, so that $\sin\theta = 1$ and $\cos\theta = 0$; substituting into Eq. (4.5.3) and simplifying with the angle-sum and angle-difference formulas gives

$$
R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\sin(\psi - \varphi) & \cos(\psi - \varphi) \\ 0 & \cos(\psi - \varphi) & \sin(\psi - \varphi) \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.5.5}
$$

ψ and φ appear only through their difference ψ − φ. For example, (ψ, φ) = (40°, 10°) and (70°, 40°) give the same matrix

$$
{{ex4_5.L}} .
$$

The three angles determine only two independent quantities, and the rotation has lost one degree of freedom. Geometrically, in the reading "about the body axes z, y, x", after a pitch of 90° the body x axis about which the third rotation turns has been brought onto the line of the z axis of the first rotation, but pointing the opposite way. The two rotations are in fact about the same line, only in opposite senses, so only the difference of the two angles counts. Algebraically, this is $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat y, 90^\circ)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, \varphi) = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, -\varphi)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat y, 90^\circ)$ (Exercise 4.5.5).

This phenomenon is called **gimbal lock**. The name comes from the gimbal mounts of gyroscopes and inertial navigation platforms: three nested rings, each turning about one axis, so that the platform inside keeps its direction however the vehicle turns. When the middle ring has turned through 90°, the axes of the outer and inner rings coincide, and the platform loses the freedom to turn in one direction (Figure 4.5.1). The inertial platform of the Apollo spacecraft used a three-ring gimbal; the astronauts had to avoid orientations close to gimbal lock when manoeuvring the spacecraft, and the guidance computer warned them as they approached one.

The harm of gimbal lock lies not only in the point θ = 90° itself but even more in its neighbourhood. When θ is close to 90°, a tiny change of orientation makes the Euler angles jump enormously.

Gimbal lock is not a flaw of one particular convention for Euler angles. It can be proved that any three-parameter representation covering all of SO(3) must be singular at some orientations (the proof is beyond the scope of this book; see the paper by Stuelpnagel in the chapter references). This is decided by the topology of SO(3) itself and has nothing to do with which order is chosen. Exponential coordinates have a similar problem at an angle of π (Section 4.4.6). The "wrist singularity" of a robot arm, which occurs when the axes of the first and third wrist joints line up, is physically the very same phenomenon; Chapter 16 discusses it in detail.

### 4.5.6 Using Euler angles well

Given this flaw, why are Euler angles still so widely used in engineering? Because they are intuitive: for people entering, displaying and understanding orientations, three angles with a clear physical meaning are the most convenient. The sensible approach is a division of labour:

- **Euler angles for dealing with people**: teach-pendant displays, parameter settings, descriptions of orientation in reports;
- **Rotation matrices or quaternions inside the computation**: composition, interpolation and control of orientation, avoiding gimbal lock;
- **State the convention when converting between the two**: which order, fixed or body axes, and the ranges of the angles.

Euler angles are intuitive but have singularities; rotation matrices have no singularities but are redundant. Is there a representation with no singularities and very little redundancy? The quaternions introduced in the next section achieve this with four numbers.

**Engineer's notes**

- Different makes of robot use different conventions for orientation angles: the orders differ, fixed or body axes differ, and so do the units and ranges. When passing an orientation between different systems, the safest thing is to pass a rotation matrix or a quaternion; when Euler angles must be passed, run a round-trip test with a known orientation.
- When planning the path of the end-effector, keep the pitch angle from passing through ±90°; when it must, switch to quaternion interpolation internally.
- Use atan2, not arctan; use `math.hypot(r11, r21)`, not `sqrt(1 - r31**2)`; the latter loses accuracy when θ is close to ±90°.
