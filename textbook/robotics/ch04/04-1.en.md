### 4.1.1 The problem

A robot arm holds a rectangular part with a vacuum suction cup placed at the middle of the part. To set the part into a tilted tray, the wrist must first turn through 30° about the axis perpendicular to the part. One corner P of the part lies 0.2 m from the centre of the cup along the long side of the part and 0.1 m along the short side. Where is this corner after the turn?

The question is not hard in itself, but it stands for the most basic computation in robot kinematics: where the points of a rigid body go when the body turns about a fixed point. Every time the arm changes the orientation of its end, the controller makes this computation for a great many points on the workpiece, the tool and the obstacles nearby. So what we want is not just this one answer but a way of writing it that applies at once to any angle and any point. In this section we shall see that this way of writing it is the rotation matrix.

### 4.1.2 Frames and the sign of a rotation

Take the centre of the cup as the origin O and set up a Cartesian frame {a} in the plane of the part: the x axis along the long side, the y axis along the short side. Following the conventions of Chapter 3, the position vector of corner P is written **p**, and its components in {a} are written $p_a$:

$$
p_a = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.2 \\ 0.1 \end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{m}. \tag{4.1.1}
$$

We take an angle θ as positive when it turns the x axis towards the y axis (anticlockwise), and we measure it in radians unless degrees are stated. After the turn, P has moved to a new position P′, whose components in the same frame {a} are written $p_a' = (x', y')^{\mathsf T}$.

Figure 4.1.1 shows the part and its corner before and after the turn. Here the frame {a} stays fixed and the part turns. This point of view is called an **active rotation**. The opposite case, a **passive rotation**, in which the body stays still and the frame turns, is discussed in Section 4.1.6.

### 4.1.3 The rotation formula from polar coordinates

When a rigid body turns about a fixed point, the distance from any of its points to the centre of rotation stays the same; only the direction changes. This suggests that polar coordinates are the natural way to describe a rotation. Let the polar coordinates of P be (r, φ) (see Figure 4.1.1), that is,

$$
x = r\cos\varphi, \qquad y = r\sin\varphi, \tag{4.1.2}
$$

where $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ is the distance from P to O and φ is the angle between the position vector and the x axis. In our example,

$$
r = \sqrt{0.2^2 + 0.1^2} = \sqrt{ {{ex4_1_1.r_sq:.2f}} } \approx {{ex4_1_1.r}}\ \mathrm{m}, \qquad \varphi = \arctan\frac{0.1}{0.2} \approx {{ex4_1_1.phi_deg:.2f}}^\circ .
$$

A turn through θ leaves the distance unchanged and adds θ to the direction angle, so

$$
x' = r\cos(\varphi+\theta), \qquad y' = r\sin(\varphi+\theta). \tag{4.1.3}
$$

Eq. (4.1.3) already answers the question, but it makes us convert Cartesian coordinates into polar ones and, after the turn, convert them back. We would like a direct relation between (x′, y′) and (x, y). Using the addition formulas

$$
\cos(\varphi+\theta) = \cos\varphi\cos\theta - \sin\varphi\sin\theta, \qquad
\sin(\varphi+\theta) = \sin\varphi\cos\theta + \cos\varphi\sin\theta ,
$$

substituting them into Eq. (4.1.3), and noting from Eq. (4.1.2) that $r\cos\varphi = x$ and $r\sin\varphi = y$, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
x' &= x\cos\theta - y\sin\theta, \\
y' &= x\sin\theta + y\cos\theta .
\end{aligned} \tag{4.1.4}
$$

The polar coordinates r and φ have dropped out of the derivation altogether. Eq. (4.1.4) contains only the original coordinates and the angle, and it holds for every point of the plane and every angle.

**Example 4.1.1** With θ = 30°, $\cos 30^\circ \approx {{ex4_1_1.c30}}$ and $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x' &= 0.2 \times {{ex4_1_1.c30}} - 0.1 \times 0.5 = {{ex4_1_1.term_xc}} - {{ex4_1_1.term_ys:.2f}} = {{ex4_1_1.x_p}}\ \mathrm{m}, \\
y' &= 0.2 \times 0.5 + 0.1 \times {{ex4_1_1.c30}} = {{ex4_1_1.term_xs:.1f}} + {{ex4_1_1.term_yc:.5f}} = {{ex4_1_1.y_p}}\ \mathrm{m}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Going through polar coordinates with Eq. (4.1.3), that is, computing with φ + θ ≈ {{ex4_1_1.phi_theta_deg:.2f}}°, gives the same result.

### 4.1.4 The rotation matrix

Eq. (4.1.4) has an important feature: x′ and y′ are both linear combinations of x and y, and the coefficients depend only on the angle θ, not on the point being turned. In other words, the operation "turn through θ" is completely determined by four numbers, cos θ, −sin θ, sin θ and cos θ. Arranging them in a matrix, we can write Eq. (4.1.4) as

$$
p_a' = R(\theta)\,p_a, \qquad
R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.1.5}
$$

**Definition 4.1.1 (Plane rotation matrix)** A 2×2 matrix R(θ) of the form (4.1.5) is called the plane **rotation matrix** of angle θ.

The geometric operation of turning has thus become the multiplication of a column vector by a matrix. Representing linear transformations by matrices was set out systematically by Arthur Cayley in his *Memoir on the Theory of Matrices* of 1858, and the plane rotation is one of the most intuitive examples of it. For Example 4.1.1,

$$
R(30^\circ) = \begin{pmatrix} {{ex4_1_1.c30}} & -0.5 \\ 0.5 & {{ex4_1_1.c30}} \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
R(30^\circ)\begin{pmatrix} 0.2 \\ 0.1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} {{ex4_1_1.x_p}} \\ {{ex4_1_1.y_p}} \end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

**What the columns of a rotation matrix mean.** Substituting the unit vector of the x axis, $(1, 0)^{\mathsf T}$, into Eq. (4.1.5) gives $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)^{\mathsf T}$, the first column of R(θ); substituting $(0, 1)^{\mathsf T}$ gives $(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)^{\mathsf T}$, the second column (Figure 4.1.2). So column i of a rotation matrix is the unit vector of coordinate axis i after the turn, expressed in the original frame. This view carries over unchanged to three dimensions and runs through the whole book: to read a rotation matrix is to read where the turned axes point.

### 4.1.5 Basic properties of the rotation matrix

**Theorem 4.1.1** A plane rotation matrix R = R(θ) satisfies:

1. $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$, that is, the two columns of R are unit vectors perpendicular to each other;
2. $\det R = 1$;
3. $R^{-1} = R^{\mathsf T} = R(-\theta)$;
4. $|Rp| = |p|$ for every p, that is, a rotation preserves length.

*Proof*　(1) By direct computation:

$$
R^{\mathsf T}R =
\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix} \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta & 0 \\ 0 & \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta \end{pmatrix}
= I .
$$

The (i, j) entry of the product is the dot product of column i with column j, so $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ says exactly that the columns are unit vectors and mutually perpendicular.

(2) $\det R = \cos^2\theta - (-\sin\theta)(\sin\theta) = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$.

(3) By (1), $R^{\mathsf T}$ satisfies the definition of the inverse. And since cosine is an even function and sine an odd one, R(−θ) is exactly the transpose of R(θ).

(4) $|Rp|^2 = (Rp)^{\mathsf T}(Rp) = p^{\mathsf T}R^{\mathsf T}Rp = p^{\mathsf T}p = |p|^2$.　∎

The geometric meaning of property (3) is plain: turning back to where we started is turning through −θ. Its computational meaning matters just as much. Inverting a general matrix takes a fair amount of work and may introduce numerical error; the inverse of a rotation matrix is just its transpose, exact and almost free. "Turn there and turn back" occurs everywhere in robot programs, and this property is used again and again. Property (4) can be checked on the example: $|p_a| = {{ex4_1_1.norm_p}}$ m, and $|p_a'| = \sqrt{ {{ex4_1_1.x_p}}^2 + {{ex4_1_1.y_p}}^2 } \approx {{ex4_1_1.norm_pp}}$ m.

Properties (1) and (2) together characterize the rotation matrices: the 2×2 matrices with $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ and $\det R = 1$ are exactly the plane rotations. The converse is not hard to see. Let a 2×2 matrix A satisfy both conditions. By (1) the first column of A is a unit vector, and a unit vector can always be written as $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)^{\mathsf T}$. The second column is also a unit vector, perpendicular to the first, and in the plane there are only two unit vectors perpendicular to $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)^{\mathsf T}$, namely $\pm(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)^{\mathsf T}$; since $\det A = 1$, only the plus sign is possible. So A is R(θ). The set of these matrices is written SO(2); where the name comes from is explained in Section 4.2, together with its three-dimensional counterpart SO(3). If $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ but $\det R = -1$, the matrix represents a **reflection**, not a rotation. For example, diag(1, −1) mirrors the plane in the x axis; no continuous turning can make a part into its mirror image.

### 4.1.6 Active and passive rotations

So far the part has turned and the frame has stayed still. In practice the opposite case is just as common. Suppose a camera has been mounted turned through 30° about its optical axis: the part is at rest, while the camera's frame {b} is turned through 30° relative to {a}. Now the question is what the components of the same point P are in {b}.

The vector **p** itself has not changed; only the axes we measure it against have. As shown in Chapter 3, its components in {b} are its projections onto the two axes of {b}. The components of those two axes in {a} are exactly the two columns of R(30°); following the notation conventions we write this matrix $R_{ab}$, read "the orientation of {b} relative to {a}". Hence

$$
p_b = R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}\, p_a = R_{ba}\, p_a , \qquad R_{ab} = R(30^\circ). \tag{4.1.6}
$$

In Eq. (4.1.6) two equal subscripts standing side by side can be thought of as "cancelling": in $R_{ba}\,p_a$ the two a's cancel and b is left, and the result is indeed the components in {b}. This subscript-cancellation rule is stated as a theorem in Chapter 5 and used throughout the book.

An active rotation through θ and a passive rotation through θ differ by a transpose. Mixing them up is the most common mistake of beginners, and on the shop floor it is a common reason why a robot "always goes the wrong way".

### 4.1.7 Two familiar examples

The minute hand of a clock turns through 6° each minute, clockwise, so θ = −6°. Where the tip of the hand is at any moment is the rotation matrix times its starting position.

When a phone goes from portrait to landscape, the picture turns through 90°. Then

$$
R(90^\circ) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
$$

which takes (x, y) to (−y, x). The matrix contains only 0 and ±1, which is why picture software needs no trigonometry at all to turn an image through 90°.

A rotation in the plane is fixed completely by one angle. Rotations in space are different: the axis itself can point in any direction. The next section extends the rotation matrix to three dimensions and shows that the two conclusions of this section, that the columns are the turned axes and that the inverse is the transpose, still hold there.

**Engineer's notes**

- Trigonometric functions in program libraries take radians. 30° must first be converted to π/6 ≈ {{ex4_1_1.theta_rad:.4f}} rad. Passing degrees straight to `cos()` is one of the most common mistakes in the field.
- A "rotation matrix" obtained from calibration or from a sensor usually fails $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ slightly because of measurement error. Orthogonalize it before use, or the error builds up as matrices are multiplied together (the method is in Section 4.8).
- To check whether a matrix is a valid rotation matrix, look at three things: every column has length 1, the columns are mutually perpendicular, and the determinant is +1.
