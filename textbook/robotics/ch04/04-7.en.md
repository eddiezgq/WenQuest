### 4.7.1 Converting between the four representations

The previous five sections introduced four ways of describing a rotation. They describe the same thing and can be converted into one another. Table 4.7.1 collects the conversion formulas for reference.

::: Table 4.7.1
caption: The four representations of rotation and the conversions between them
:::

| From ↓ to → | Rotation matrix R | Axis–angle / exponential coordinates $\hat\omega\theta$ | Unit quaternion q | ZYX Euler angles (ψ, θ, φ) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rotation matrix R | — | Angle from the trace, Eq. (4.3.2); axis from the skew-symmetric part, Eq. (4.4.11); for θ = π use Eq. (4.4.12) | Eq. (4.6.8); for a stable algorithm use Eqs. (4.6.9) and (4.6.10) | Eq. (4.5.4); singular at θ = ±90° |
| Axis–angle | Rodrigues' formula (4.4.5) | — | Eq. (4.6.5) | First find R, then use Eq. (4.5.4) |
| Quaternion | Eq. (4.6.7) | $\theta = 2\arccos q_0$, $\hat\omega = \vec q/\sin(\theta/2)$ | — | First find R, then use Eq. (4.5.4) |
| Euler angles | Eq. (4.5.3) | First find R, then the axis–angle | Multiply the quaternions of the three elementary rotations | — |

| Representation | Numbers | Constraints | Singularity | Composition | Main uses |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rotation matrix | 9 | 6 | None | Matrix product | Coordinate transformation, theoretical derivations |
| Exponential coordinates | 3 | None | At θ = π the direction of the axis is not unique | Cannot be composed directly | Angular velocity, errors, small rotations |
| Euler angles | 3 | None | Gimbal lock | Cannot be composed directly | User interfaces, display |
| Unit quaternion | 4 | 1 | None (but q and −q are equivalent) | Quaternion product | Internal storage in controllers, interpolation |

"Cannot be composed directly" in the table means that the exponential coordinates or Euler angles of two rotations cannot simply be added to give the exponential coordinates or Euler angles of the composite rotation (Exercise 4.4.3); they must first be converted to matrices or quaternions and multiplied, and then converted back.

### 4.7.2 The problem of orientation interpolation

The conversion formulas solve a static problem. Robot motion also raises a dynamic one: given a starting orientation $R_0$ and a final orientation $R_1$, the end-effector is to turn smoothly from the one to the other in a given time; what orientation should it have at each moment in between? This is called **orientation interpolation**. Using a parameter t ∈ [0, 1] for progress, with t = 0 the start and t = 1 the end, we want to construct a curve R(t).

A good interpolating curve naturally has to meet three requirements:

1. at every moment R(t) is a rotation matrix;
2. the path is shortest: no roundabout way;
3. uniform speed: the magnitude of the angular velocity stays constant, so the robot does not alternately speed up and slow down, and the torques are smooth.

Below we test several intuitive approaches one by one on a concrete example.

**Example 4.7.1** The end-effector turns from $R_0 = I$ to

$$
R_1 = {{ex4_7.R1}} ,
$$

whose ZYX Euler angles are yaw 90°, pitch 45° and roll 90°. By Eq. (4.3.2), the angle between the two orientations is {{ex4_7.theta_total:.2f}}°; this is the angle that the shortest way of turning has to cover.

**Method 1: interpolating the matrix entries directly.** Let $R(t) = (1-t)R_0 + tR_1$. At t = 0.5 the first column of the matrix has length only {{ex4_7.col_norm:.4f}} and the determinant is {{ex4_7.det_mid:.4f}}; it is not a rotation matrix at all. Geometrically, this amounts to "squashing" the body. This approach fails the first requirement and should be abandoned.

**Method 2: interpolating the Euler angles.** Let the three angles yaw, pitch and roll all change in proportion to t from 0 to their final values, that is, 90°t, 45°t and 90°t at time t, and find R(t) by Eq. (4.5.3). Then every moment gives a rotation matrix, and the first requirement is met. But summing the angles turned step by step, the whole motion turns through about {{ex4_7.len_euler:.1f}}°, over 10% more than the shortest {{ex4_7.theta_total:.1f}}°. Taking the average angular velocity of the shortest way of turning as 1 (that is, {{ex4_7.theta_total:.1f}}° per unit time), its angular velocity peaks at {{ex4_7.sp_e_max:.2f}} and falls to only {{ex4_7.sp_e_min:.2f}}. At the midpoint t = 0.5 it differs from the orientation on the shortest path by about {{ex4_7.mid_gap:.0f}}°. Worse still, a different convention for the Euler angles gives a different path, so the result depends on an arbitrary choice that has nothing to do with the physics. If the path passes near gimbal lock, the angular velocity also rises sharply (Section 4.5.5).

**Method 3: linear interpolation of quaternions, then normalization.** Let the quaternions of the start and end orientations be $q_A$ and $q_B$ (to avoid confusion with the components $q_0, q_1, \ldots$, the subscripts A and B distinguish the two quaternions). Let $q(t) = (1-t)q_A + tq_B$, and divide it by its norm. This approach is called normalized linear interpolation. It follows exactly the shortest path, and the total angle turned is exactly {{ex4_7.theta_total:.1f}}°; but the angular velocity is not uniform: again taking the average angular velocity of the shortest way of turning as 1, it peaks at {{ex4_7.sp_n_max:.2f}} and falls to {{ex4_7.sp_n_min:.2f}}. The reason is explained in Section 4.7.4.

### 4.7.3 The shortest path: turning uniformly about one axis

How can we both follow the shortest path and keep the speed uniform? Euler's theorem gives the answer. By Theorem 4.3.1, the relative rotation $R_0^{\mathsf T}R_1$ from $R_0$ to $R_1$ is a single rotation through some angle θ about some axis $\hat\omega$. The most natural path is then to turn about this axis, at constant angular velocity, from 0 to θ. In the notation of Section 4.4,

$$
R(t) = R_0\, e^{[\hat\omega]\theta t}, \qquad \hat\omega\theta = \log\big(R_0^{\mathsf T} R_1\big) . \tag{4.7.1}
$$

Here log is the logarithm map of Section 4.4.6, and postmultiplication means turning about the body's own axis in {b} (Theorem 4.2.2). This curve meets all three requirements: at every moment it is a rotation matrix; the total angle turned is exactly θ, the shortest; and the angular velocity is constant, equal to θ (per unit time), that is, uniform. In SO(3) such a curve is called a **geodesic**, like the shortest arc along a great circle between two points on a sphere.

For Example 4.7.1, $R_0 = I$, the axis is $\hat\omega = {{ex4_7.w}}$, and the angle is {{ex4_7.theta_total:.2f}}°. Figure 4.7.1 shows the paths traced on the unit sphere by the direction of the z axis of the end-effector under Euler-angle interpolation and along the geodesic; the path of normalized linear interpolation coincides with the geodesic, only it is traversed unevenly, so it is not drawn separately.

### 4.7.4 Spherical linear interpolation

Eq. (4.7.1) requires computing a matrix exponential and a logarithm. With quaternions it can be written more simply.

A unit quaternion has four components and norm 1, so it can be regarded as a point on the unit sphere in four-dimensional space. By Eq. (4.6.5), the quaternion of a rotation through θ makes an angle of θ/2 in four-dimensional space with the quaternion (1, 0, 0, 0) of "no rotation". The uniform rotation of Eq. (4.7.1) corresponds to the quaternion moving uniformly along a great-circle arc of the four-dimensional unit sphere. The reason is as follows. By Section 4.6.5, the quaternion corresponding to Eq. (4.7.1) is $q(t) = q_A\,(\cos\frac{\theta t}{2},\ \sin\frac{\theta t}{2}\hat\omega)$. The second factor on the right moves uniformly with t along a great circle through (1, 0, 0, 0), because only two of its four components change and their sum of squares is always 1. Multiplying on the left by the unit quaternion $q_A$ changes neither the lengths of four-dimensional vectors nor the angles between them (Exercise 4.7.5), so it "carries" this great circle as a whole onto a great circle through $q_A$, and the motion is still uniform.

In the plane, if a vector turns uniformly along the unit circle from a to b (which make an angle Ω), each intermediate vector can be written as a combination of a and b. Let $p(t) = \alpha\,a + \beta\,b$, and require that p(t) make an angle tΩ with a and an angle (1 − t)Ω with b. The law of sines gives $\alpha = \sin((1-t)\Omega)/\sin\Omega$ and $\beta = \sin(t\Omega)/\sin\Omega$ (Exercise 4.7.2). This conclusion does not depend on the dimension and applies equally to quaternions on the four-dimensional unit sphere.

**Definition 4.7.1 (Spherical linear interpolation)** Let $q_A$ and $q_B$ be unit quaternions with $\cos\Omega = q_A \cdot q_B$ (the four pairs of corresponding components multiplied and added). Then

$$
\mathrm{slerp}(q_A, q_B; t) = \frac{\sin\big((1-t)\Omega\big)}{\sin\Omega}\, q_A + \frac{\sin(t\Omega)}{\sin\Omega}\, q_B , \qquad t \in [0, 1] . \tag{4.7.2}
$$

This formula was introduced into computer animation by K. Shoemake in 1985 and is called **spherical linear interpolation**, or Slerp for short. It gives exactly the same path as Eq. (4.7.1) (Program 4.7.1 checks this point by point), but uses only a few multiplications and trigonometric functions, so the computation is much lighter.

Now the trouble with Method 3 can be explained. $(1-t)q_A + tq_B$ is a point on the chord joining $q_A$ and $q_B$; normalizing projects it onto the sphere, where it does land on the great-circle arc, so the path is right. But points equally spaced along the chord are not equally spaced when projected onto the arc: the points near the two ends crowd together and those in the middle are farther apart, so the turning is fast in the middle and slow at the ends. The sine coefficients in Slerp are there precisely to correct this.

**Handling the shortest path.** Because of the double cover, $q_B$ and $-q_B$ represent the same orientation. If $q_A \cdot q_B < 0$, the angle between them on the four-dimensional sphere is greater than 90°, the corresponding rotation is greater than 180°, and this is the "long way round". In that case $q_B$ should be replaced by $-q_B$ before interpolating. Forget this step and the robot turns most of the way round between two orientations that are very close; this type of accident has really happened in engineering practice. In Example 4.7.1, $q_A \cdot q_B = {{ex4_7.dot01:.4f}} > 0$, so no change of sign is needed.

**Engineer's notes**

- In robot controllers, straight-line motion commands for the orientation of the end-effector (such as "move in a straight line to a given pose") are almost always carried out internally by Slerp or the equivalent axis–angle interpolation, with the position interpolated linearly on its own.
- When Ω is very small, $\sin\Omega$ in Slerp is close to zero and the formula becomes 0/0. Practical programs switch to normalized linear interpolation when Ω is very small, where the difference between the two is negligible.
- Smooth transitions through several orientations (joining several Slerp segments) produce sudden changes of angular velocity at the joins and need higher-order interpolation methods, discussed in Chapter 22.
