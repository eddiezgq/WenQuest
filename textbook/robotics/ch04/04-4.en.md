### 4.4.1 Writing the rotation matrix from the axis and the angle

Euler's theorem tells us that every rotation is determined by a rotation axis $\hat\omega$ and an angle θ. Robot programs often need exactly the reverse: given the axis and the angle, find the rotation matrix. For example, a teach pendant has an instruction "turn the tool 15° about some tilted direction"; or a force sensor measures a torque on the end-effector, and the controller decides to turn the end-effector through a small angle in the direction of that torque. The axis is then usually not a coordinate axis, and neither Eq. (4.2.4) nor Eq. (4.2.5) can be used.

Euler had already obtained, in his 1775 paper, a formula for the coordinates after a rotation computed from the axis and the angle; the French mathematician Olinde Rodrigues gave it a neat geometric form in 1840 and also solved the problem of how two rotations combine, and today the formula usually bears his name (on the question of attribution, see the article by Cheng and Gupta in the chapter references). There were no matrices at that time; the matrix notation below came later. This section first derives the formula geometrically and then reaches the same result from another angle, the matrix exponential. The second derivation may look like a detour, but it reveals a deep structure of rotation, and it will be the starting point of the screw theory of Chapter 6.

### 4.4.2 Geometric derivation: splitting the vector into two parts

Let the unit vector $\hat\omega$ be the axis of rotation, and let the vector v turn through θ about it to give v′. We want to express v′ in terms of $\hat\omega$, θ and v.

From this section on, where no confusion can arise, we no longer distinguish between a vector itself and its components: v and v′ are both understood as components (column vectors) in the same frame, so that vector operations and matrix operations can be written together directly.

The key step is to split v into two parts: the part $v_\parallel$ along the axis and the part $v_\perp$ perpendicular to the axis (Figure 4.4.1). By the projection formula of Chapter 3,

$$
v_\parallel = (\hat\omega \cdot v)\,\hat\omega, \qquad v_\perp = v - (\hat\omega \cdot v)\,\hat\omega . \tag{4.4.1}
$$

The two parts fare quite differently under the rotation. The part along the axis lies on the axis, and the rotation does not change it: $v_\parallel' = v_\parallel$. The perpendicular part turns through θ in the plane perpendicular to the axis, which is just the plane rotation of Section 4.1.

To write down a plane rotation we need two directions in this plane that are perpendicular to each other and of the same length. The first is $v_\perp$ itself; for the second we take $\hat\omega \times v$. By the geometric meaning of the cross product, $\hat\omega \times v$ is perpendicular to $\hat\omega$, so it lies in this plane; it is also perpendicular to v, and hence to $v_\perp$; and its length is $|v|\sin\alpha$ (α being the angle between v and the axis), which is exactly $|v_\perp|$. By the right-hand rule, turning $v_\perp$ through 90° brings it to $\hat\omega \times v$. So these two directions play the roles of the "x axis" and the "y axis" of a plane rotation. By Section 4.1,

$$
v_\perp' = \cos\theta\; v_\perp + \sin\theta\; (\hat\omega \times v) .
$$

Adding the two parts:

$$
v' = v_\parallel + \cos\theta\,(v - v_\parallel) + \sin\theta\,(\hat\omega \times v) ,
$$

and substituting $v_\parallel = (\hat\omega \cdot v)\hat\omega$ and collecting the two terms in $v_\parallel$, we obtain

$$
v' = v\cos\theta + (\hat\omega \times v)\sin\theta + \hat\omega\,(\hat\omega \cdot v)(1 - \cos\theta) . \tag{4.4.2}
$$

This is the vector form of Rodrigues' formula. Each of the three terms has a clear meaning: the first shrinks the original vector by the factor cos θ; the second pushes it out "in the direction of the turn"; the third puts back the part along the axis. The reader can check it in the two cases θ = 0 and θ = 90°.

### 4.4.3 Matrix form

Every term in Eq. (4.4.2) is a linear function of v, so it can be written as some matrix times v. For this we need two tools from Chapter 3.

The first is the matrix representation of the cross product: for $\hat\omega = (\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3)^{\mathsf T}$, define the skew-symmetric matrix

$$
[\hat\omega] = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\omega_3 & \omega_2 \\ \omega_3 & 0 & -\omega_1 \\ -\omega_2 & \omega_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad [\hat\omega]\, v = \hat\omega \times v . \tag{4.4.3}
$$

The second is the square of $[\hat\omega]$. We have $[\hat\omega]^2 v = \hat\omega \times (\hat\omega \times v)$; by the vector triple product formula $a \times (b \times c) = b(a\cdot c) - c(a \cdot b)$, and noting $\hat\omega \cdot \hat\omega = 1$,

$$
[\hat\omega]^2 v = \hat\omega\,(\hat\omega \cdot v) - v , \qquad \text{that is,}\quad [\hat\omega]^2 = \hat\omega\hat\omega^{\mathsf T} - I . \tag{4.4.4}
$$

Substitute these two into Eq. (4.4.2): the second term is $\sin\theta\,[\hat\omega]v$; the first and third terms together are $v\cos\theta + (1-\cos\theta)(v + [\hat\omega]^2 v) = v + (1 - \cos\theta)[\hat\omega]^2 v$. This gives the following theorem.

**Theorem 4.4.1 (Rodrigues' formula)** The rotation matrix for a turn through θ about the unit vector $\hat\omega$ is

$$
\mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega, \theta) = I + \sin\theta\,[\hat\omega] + (1 - \cos\theta)\,[\hat\omega]^2 . \tag{4.4.5}
$$

Check with $\hat\omega = \hat z = (0, 0, 1)^{\mathsf T}$: $[\hat z]$ has only −1 and 1 in its upper left corner, $[\hat z]^2 = \mathrm{diag}(-1, -1, 0)$, and substituting into Eq. (4.4.5) gives exactly $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \theta)$ of Eq. (4.2.4).

**Example 4.4.1** Find the rotation matrix for a turn of 120° about the diagonal direction $\hat\omega = (1, 1, 1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt 3$.

Here $\sin 120^\circ = \sqrt3/2$ and $1 - \cos 120^\circ = 3/2$. Substituting into Eq. (4.4.5), the $\sqrt 3$ in each term cancels out, and we get

$$
\mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega, 120^\circ) = {{ex4_4.R1}} .
$$

This matrix sends the x axis to the y axis, the y axis to the z axis and the z axis to the x axis: the three coordinate axes are permuted cyclically once. Standing a cube on its diagonal and turning it through 120° is exactly this case. More interesting still, it is exactly the result of method A in Example 4.2.2. By Euler's theorem, turning the book "first 90° about the x axis, then 90° about the z axis" is equivalent to a single turn of 120° about the direction $(1, 1, 1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt 3$, which makes equal angles with the three coordinate axes. The reader can pick up the book and check.

### 4.4.4 The matrix exponential: the differential equation of rotation

Now look at rotation from another angle. Let a body turn about $\hat\omega$ at a constant unit angular velocity, and let p(t) be the position of a point of the body. By Chapter 3, for a rigid body turning about a fixed axis, the velocity of a point is the angular velocity vector crossed with the position vector, so

$$
\dot p(t) = \hat\omega \times p(t) = [\hat\omega]\, p(t) . \tag{4.4.6}
$$

This is a system of linear ordinary differential equations. Its form recalls the simplest scalar equation $\dot x = a x$, whose solution is $x(t) = e^{at}x(0)$. The same can be done for matrices, once we have defined "the exponential of a matrix".

**Definition 4.4.1 (Matrix exponential)** For a square matrix A, define

$$
e^{A} = I + A + \frac{A^2}{2!} + \frac{A^3}{3!} + \cdots = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{A^k}{k!} . \tag{4.4.7}
$$

It can be proved that this series converges for every square matrix. Replace A by At and differentiate term by term with respect to t: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\frac{(At)^k}{k!} = A\frac{(At)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}$, so the derivative of the whole series is A times the original series, that is, $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}e^{At} = A\,e^{At}$; and at t = 0, $e^{A\cdot 0} = I$. So the solution of Eq. (4.4.6) is $p(t) = e^{[\hat\omega]t}p(0)$. The time taken to turn through θ is exactly θ, so **the rotation matrix for a turn through θ about $\hat\omega$ equals $e^{[\hat\omega]\theta}$**.

Does this agree with Rodrigues' formula? Expand the series and use, again and again, a property of $[\hat\omega]$: multiplying both sides of Eq. (4.4.4) by $[\hat\omega]$ once more, and noting $[\hat\omega]\hat\omega = \hat\omega\times\hat\omega = 0$, we get

$$
[\hat\omega]^3 = -[\hat\omega] . \tag{4.4.8}
$$

So the higher powers of $[\hat\omega]$ are of only two kinds: the odd powers are all $\pm[\hat\omega]$ and the even powers are all $\pm[\hat\omega]^2$. Collecting the terms of the series (4.4.7) into these two classes:

$$
e^{[\hat\omega]\theta} = I + \Big(\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \cdots\Big)[\hat\omega] + \Big(\frac{\theta^2}{2!} - \frac{\theta^4}{4!} + \frac{\theta^6}{6!} - \cdots\Big)[\hat\omega]^2 .
$$

From calculus, sine and cosine can be written as infinite series (Taylor series):

$$
\sin\theta = \theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \cdots, \qquad \cos\theta = 1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2!} + \frac{\theta^4}{4!} - \cdots .
$$

Comparing, we see that the first bracket is exactly sin θ and the second is exactly 1 − cos θ. This gives

$$
e^{[\hat\omega]\theta} = I + \sin\theta\,[\hat\omega] + (1 - \cos\theta)\,[\hat\omega]^2 = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega, \theta) . \tag{4.4.9}
$$

Two entirely different roads, one by geometry and one by a differential equation, have led to the same formula. That an infinite series can be reduced to a finite expression is due entirely to Eq. (4.4.8).

Computing directly with the series on a computer requires taking enough terms. For Example 4.4.1, the largest error with the first 5 terms is about {{ex4_4.err5:.2f}}, with the first 10 terms about $ {{ex4_4.err10:sci1}} $, and {{ex4_4.n_needed}} terms are needed before the error falls below $10^{-12}$ (Figure 4.4.2). ### 4.4.5 Exponential coordinates

In Eq. (4.4.9) the axis and the angle always appear as the product $\hat\omega\theta$. Combine them into one three-dimensional vector:

**Definition 4.4.2 (Exponential coordinates of a rotation)** For a turn through θ about $\hat\omega$, the three-dimensional vector $\hat\omega\theta$ is called its **exponential coordinates**. Its direction is the axis and its length is the angle.

Exponential coordinates describe a rotation with exactly 3 numbers, matching the number of degrees of freedom of a rotation; unlike the 9 numbers of a rotation matrix, they carry no redundancy. The set of all 3×3 skew-symmetric matrices of the form $[\hat\omega]\theta$ is written so(3). Eq. (4.4.9) says that the matrix exponential maps elements of so(3) to elements of SO(3). In the terms of Chapter 6, so(3) is the Lie algebra of the Lie group SO(3), and the matrix exponential is the bridge between the two. There is no need to worry about these names now; just remember: **the exponential coordinates form an ordinary three-dimensional vector, and taking the matrix exponential gives the rotation matrix**.

### 4.4.6 The logarithm map: exponential coordinates from the rotation matrix

Going the other way, given R, finding the exponential coordinates $\hat\omega\theta$ is called the **logarithm map**, because it is the inverse of the matrix exponential.

The angle is already given by Eq. (4.3.2). To find the axis, write Eq. (4.4.5) as R and subtract its transpose. Since $[\hat\omega]$ is skew-symmetric, $[\hat\omega]^{\mathsf T} = -[\hat\omega]$; while $[\hat\omega]^2$ and I are both symmetric and vanish in the subtraction. Hence

$$
R - R^{\mathsf T} = 2\sin\theta\,[\hat\omega] . \tag{4.4.10}
$$

When $0 < \theta < \pi$, $\sin\theta > 0$, and so

$$
[\hat\omega] = \frac{R - R^{\mathsf T}}{2\sin\theta} , \tag{4.4.11}
$$

and the three components of $\hat\omega$ are then read off the skew-symmetric matrix according to Eq. (4.4.3). This method uses only subtraction and division, which is much simpler than finding an eigenvector as in Section 4.3, and it also fixes the positive direction of the axis automatically.

**Example 4.4.2** Apply the logarithm map to the matrix R of Example 4.3.1. The angle has already been found: $\theta \approx {{ex4_4.t2_deg:.2f}}^\circ$, $\sin\theta \approx {{ex4_4.sin_t2}}$. Compute

$$
\frac{R - R^{\mathsf T}}{2} = {{ex4_4.W2}} ,
$$

divide by sin θ and read off the components, which gives $\hat\omega = {{ex4_4.w2}}$, the same as the result found with an eigenvector in Section 4.3. The exponential coordinates are $\hat\omega\theta = {{ex4_4.expc2}}$.

Eq. (4.4.11) fails in two cases, the two special cases mentioned in Section 4.3.5.

**θ = 0.** Then R = I, there is no rotation, the exponential coordinates are the zero vector, and the axis is undefined.

**θ = π.** Then sin θ = 0, and by Eq. (4.4.10) $R - R^{\mathsf T} = 0$, so Eq. (4.4.11) gives no information at all. In this case go back to Eq. (4.4.5): at θ = π, $R = I + 2[\hat\omega]^2 = 2\hat\omega\hat\omega^{\mathsf T} - I$, so

$$
\hat\omega\hat\omega^{\mathsf T} = \frac{R + I}{2} . \tag{4.4.12}
$$

The right-hand side is known. Every column of $\hat\omega\hat\omega^{\mathsf T}$ is a multiple of $\hat\omega$; take the column with the largest diagonal entry (to avoid dividing by a very small number) and normalize it, which gives $\hat\omega$. Here $\hat\omega$ and $-\hat\omega$ are both correct answers.

**Example 4.4.3** Let

$$
R = {{ex4_4.R3}} ,
$$

whose trace is −1, so θ = π by Eq. (4.3.2). By Eq. (4.4.12),

$$
\frac{R + I}{2} = {{ex4_4.half_RI}} .
$$

The diagonal entry of the second column, 0.64, is the largest; dividing the second column $(0.48, 0.64, 0)^{\mathsf T}$ by $\sqrt{0.64} = 0.8$ gives $\hat\omega = (0.6, 0.8, 0)^{\mathsf T}$.

Rodrigues' formula gives a way to represent a rotation by 3 numbers (the exponential coordinates). Engineering also makes wide use of another 3-number representation, which splits a rotation into three turns about coordinate axes: Euler angles. The next section discusses them and explains why "gimbal lock" occurs.

**Engineer's notes**

- Rodrigues' formula is numerically stable even for very small angles, which makes it suitable for adding up one small rotation each cycle in a controller: $R_{k+1} = R_k\,e^{[\hat\omega_b]\Delta\theta}$ (about a body axis, postmultiplication).
- In the logarithm map, when θ is close to π the denominator of Eq. (4.4.11) is small and errors are amplified. Well-established libraries switch automatically to Eq. (4.4.12) when θ is close to π; when writing your own program, be sure to handle both cases, and test with an example at θ = π.
- When the angular velocity is roughly constant during one control cycle, the angular velocity times the time interval is exactly the exponential coordinates of the rotation over that interval. Gyroscope integration and the error computation in orientation control both use exponential coordinates as a "common language".
