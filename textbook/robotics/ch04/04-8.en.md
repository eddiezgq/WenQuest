### 4.8.1 Two engineering problems

The chapter ends with two problems met every day on the shop floor.

First, how far apart are two orientations? The controller requires the end-effector to be at orientation $R_d$, and the sensors measure that it is actually at orientation R. How far apart are they? "How far" needs a number to measure it, so that we can judge whether the result is acceptable; it also needs a direction, so that we can tell the controller which way to correct.

Second, what do we do when the matrix goes "out of true"? In every cycle the controller updates the orientation by multiplying the current orientation by a small rotation, a thousand times a second. Every multiplication has a tiny rounding error, and as these build up the matrix gradually stops satisfying $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$: the three "coordinate axes" are no longer of equal length, nor mutually perpendicular. A matrix obtained by calibrating a sensor carries measurement error from the very start. Such a matrix is no longer a rotation matrix, and computing with it carries the error into every step that follows.

### 4.8.2 Orientation error

The most natural description of the difference between two orientations is how many degrees one must turn to get from one to the other. The rotation of the actual orientation relative to the desired one is

$$
R_{\mathrm{err}} = R_d^{\mathsf T} R , \tag{4.8.1}
$$

which, by the subscript-cancellation rule, is the deviation as seen in the desired orientation's own frame. By Euler's theorem, $R_{\mathrm{err}}$ is a turn through some angle about some axis, and this angle is the difference between the two orientations.

**Definition 4.8.1 (Orientation error angle)** The **orientation error angle** between two orientations $R_1$ and $R_2$ is

$$
d(R_1, R_2) = \theta\big(R_1^{\mathsf T} R_2\big) = \arccos\frac{\mathrm{tr}(R_1^{\mathsf T}R_2) - 1}{2} \in [0, \pi] . \tag{4.8.2}
$$

It has three good properties: it is zero when the two orientations are the same and positive otherwise; it does not change when $R_1$ and $R_2$ are exchanged (because $R_2^{\mathsf T}R_1$ is the inverse of $R_1^{\mathsf T}R_2$, with the same angle); and it satisfies the triangle inequality, that is, the total angle turned going from $R_1$ to $R_3$ by way of $R_2$ is never less than the angle turned in going there directly. A quantity with these three properties is called in mathematics a **metric**, a reasonable kind of "distance".

Moreover, it does not depend on the choice of frame. Let Q be any rotation matrix; then

$$
d(QR_1, QR_2) = \theta(R_1^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}QR_2) = d(R_1, R_2) ,
$$

and for postmultiplication by Q the same conclusion follows from the trace property $\mathrm{tr}(Q^{\mathsf T}AQ) = \mathrm{tr}\,A$. In other words, whatever frame we observe in and wherever the tool frame is defined, the error angle computed is the same. This matters for engineering judgement: whether a result is acceptable should not depend on an arbitrarily chosen frame.

There are two more, equivalent, ways of computing the orientation error angle:

- **Using quaternions**: if the unit quaternions of the two orientations are p and q, then $d = 2\arccos|p \cdot q|$, where $p\cdot q$ is the sum of the products of the four pairs of corresponding components. The absolute value is taken because of the double cover.
- **Using the difference of the matrices**: the square root of the sum of the squares of the differences of the matrix entries, written $\|R_1 - R_2\|_F$, is called the **Frobenius norm**. It can be proved (Exercise 4.8.2) that

$$
\|R_1 - R_2\|_F = 2\sqrt 2\,\sin\frac{d}{2} . \tag{4.8.3}
$$

It corresponds one-to-one with the error angle, and for small errors it is approximately $\sqrt 2\,d$.

**The error vector.** The controller needs not only the size of the error but also its direction. Applying the logarithm map (Section 4.4.6) to $R_{\mathrm{err}}$ gives the exponential coordinates

$$
e = \log(R_{\mathrm{err}}) \in \mathbb{R}^3 . \tag{4.8.4}
$$

Its direction is the axis about which the deviation turns, and its length is the error angle; its components are expressed in the frame of the desired orientation. To correct, turn the opposite way about this axis, that is, set $R \leftarrow R\,e^{-[e]}$. The error vector is the error quantity most commonly used in orientation control (Chapter 44). When the error is small, Eq. (4.4.5) gives $R_{\mathrm{err}} \approx I + [e]$, so it is enough to take the skew-symmetric part of $R_{\mathrm{err}}$:

$$
[e] \approx \frac{R_{\mathrm{err}} - R_{\mathrm{err}}^{\mathsf T}}{2} . \tag{4.8.5}
$$

This approximation uses only subtraction, is extremely fast, and is accurate enough when the error is below a few degrees.

$$
R_d = {{ex4_8.R_d}} ,
$$

the orientation used again and again in this chapter. The measured orientation is 2° off from it, about the body axis ${{ex4_8.err_axis}}$. ### 4.8.3 Numerical drift

Now for the second problem. Suppose the end-effector turns at constant angular velocity, with components $\omega_b = (0.3, -0.5, 0.8)^{\mathsf T}$ rad/s in the end-effector's own frame, and the controller updates the orientation every Δt = 1 ms. The simplest update postmultiplies the current orientation by the first-order approximation of a small rotation:

$$
R_{k+1} = R_k\,(I + [\omega_b]\Delta t) . \tag{4.8.6}
$$

The $I + [\omega]\Delta t$ in this formula is only the first two terms of the rotation matrix $e^{[\omega]\Delta t}$, and is not itself strictly a rotation matrix. Program 4.8.1 simulates {{ex4_8.steps}} steps, that is, 20 seconds of updates: in the end the largest deviation of $R^{\mathsf T}R$ from I reaches {{ex4_8.drift1:.4f}}, and the determinant has become {{ex4_8.det1:.4f}}. In other words, the "coordinate axes" have been stretched by about one per cent. If instead each small rotation is computed exactly by Rodrigues' formula (4.4.5), the deviation is only $ {{ex4_8.drift2:sci1}} $, which comes from the computer's rounding error; but it too grows slowly with time (Figure 4.8.1).

The conclusion is that in a system that runs for a long time, however exact the update formula, the matrix should be pulled back onto SO(3) at regular intervals; this operation is called **orthonormalization**.

### 4.8.4 Methods of orthonormalization

Given a 3×3 matrix M that is close to a rotation matrix but not strictly orthogonal, we want a true rotation matrix to replace it. Three methods are in common use.

**The Gram–Schmidt process.** Chapter 2 introduced this method of turning a set of vectors into orthogonal unit vectors: normalize the first column of M and take it as the new x axis; subtract from the second column its projection on the x axis and normalize the result to get the y axis; take the z axis as the cross product of x and y, which guarantees a right-handed frame. The method is simple and intuitive but not symmetric: the first column keeps its original direction entirely, and all the error is pushed onto the other two columns.

**The singular value decomposition.** The singular value decomposition of Chapter 2 writes M as $M = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}$, where U and V are orthogonal matrices and Σ is a diagonal matrix. When M is close to a rotation matrix, the diagonal entries of Σ are all close to 1. Replacing Σ by the identity matrix gives an orthogonal matrix $UV^{\mathsf T}$. To make sure the determinant is +1 and not −1, a correction is inserted:

$$
R = U\,\mathrm{diag}\big(1, 1, \det(UV^{\mathsf T})\big)\,V^{\mathsf T} . \tag{4.8.7}
$$

**Theorem 4.8.1** Let the singular values of M be arranged in decreasing order, $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \sigma_3 \ge 0$. Of all rotation matrices, the R given by Eq. (4.8.7) is the one closest to M, that is, it minimizes $\|R - M\|_F$.

*Proof (outline)*　$\|R - M\|_F^2 = \mathrm{tr}(R^{\mathsf T}R) - 2\,\mathrm{tr}(R^{\mathsf T}M) + \mathrm{tr}(M^{\mathsf T}M) = 3 - 2\,\mathrm{tr}(R^{\mathsf T}M) + \mathrm{tr}(M^{\mathsf T}M)$, so the problem reduces to maximizing $\mathrm{tr}(R^{\mathsf T}M) = \mathrm{tr}(R^{\mathsf T}U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}) = \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma\,V^{\mathsf T}R^{\mathsf T}U)$. Write $Z = V^{\mathsf T}R^{\mathsf T}U$; it is an orthogonal matrix whose entries are at most 1 in absolute value, so $\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma Z) = \sum_i \sigma_i z_{ii} \le \sum_i \sigma_i$, with equality when Z = I, that is, when $R = UV^{\mathsf T}$. This proves the case $\det(UV^{\mathsf T}) = 1$, which always holds when M is close to a rotation matrix and $\det M > 0$. The case $\det(UV^{\mathsf T}) = -1$ requires Z to be restricted to orthogonal matrices of determinant −1; the conclusion is that the correction belongs in the position of the smallest singular value. For a rigorous proof see the papers by Kabsch and by Umeyama in the chapter references, and also Exercise 4.8.4.　∎

In statistics this problem is called the orthogonal Procrustes problem, and in spacecraft attitude determination it is called Wahba's problem (1965); in robotics it also arises in hand-eye calibration (Chapter 50) and point cloud registration (Chapter 54).

**Normalizing the quaternion.** If the orientation is stored as a quaternion, orthonormalization takes a single step: divide by the norm of the quaternion. This is one of the reasons quaternions are especially popular for integration over long periods.

**Example 4.8.2** An orientation matrix obtained by calibration carries measurement error:

$$
M = {{ex4_8.M}} ,
$$

and the largest deviation of $M^{\mathsf T}M$ from I is {{ex4_8.orth_err_M:.4f}}. The Gram–Schmidt process gives

$$
R_{\mathrm{GS}} = {{ex4_8.G}} , \qquad \|R_{\mathrm{GS}} - M\|_F \approx {{ex4_8.dist_G:.4f}} ;
$$

the singular value decomposition gives

$$
R_{\mathrm{SVD}} = {{ex4_8.S}} , \qquad \|R_{\mathrm{SVD}} - M\|_F \approx {{ex4_8.dist_S:.4f}} .
$$

Both are rotation matrices, and the result of the singular value decomposition is closer to M, as Theorem 4.8.1 says. Figure 4.8.2 sketches the geometric meaning: all rotation matrices form a "surface" SO(3) in nine-dimensional space, the matrix M with its error lies off it, and the singular value decomposition finds the point of the surface closest to M.

**Engineer's notes**

- When analysing and comparing orientation errors, the error angle (Eq. (4.8.2)) is the most sensible measure; the differences of the three Euler angles taken separately depend on the convention and mean nothing near gimbal lock. For acceptance testing, however, report according to the standard agreed in the contract (such as ISO 9283), which has its own component definitions for orientation deviation; record the two separately and do not mix them up.
- For the error in orientation control, use the logarithm map of Eq. (4.8.4) or the small-angle approximation (4.8.5), not differences of Euler angles.
- In embedded controllers the orientation is often stored as a quaternion and normalized once every cycle; when a rotation matrix is needed, it is converted by Eq. (4.6.7).
