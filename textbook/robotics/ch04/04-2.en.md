### 4.2.1 From the plane to space

The part in the previous section turned only within one plane, and a single angle was enough to describe its orientation. The end-effector of a robot arm, however, moves in space. Take the UR5e collaborative arm as an example: its end-effector can face any direction, bending down to pick a part off a conveyor, leaning sideways to push a part into a fixture, or tilting up so that a camera looks at the operator. To say which way the end-effector is "facing", one angle is no longer enough.

This section answers three questions: how an orientation in space is described by numbers; what conditions these numbers satisfy; and how two rotations combine into one. We shall see that the answer is still a rotation matrix, only now 3×3 instead of 2×2, and that the two conclusions of the previous section, that the columns are the turned axes and that the inverse is the transpose, carry over unchanged.

### 4.2.2 Describing orientation by coordinate axes

Fix a Cartesian frame {s} to the base of the robot, called the space frame, and another Cartesian frame {b} to the end-effector, called the body frame. Both frames are right-handed: when the x axis turns towards the y axis, the thumb points along the z axis. However the end-effector moves, {b} moves with it, so **the way {b} is turned relative to {s} is exactly the way the end-effector is turned**. We call this the **orientation** of the end-effector.

The most direct way to describe the orientation of {b} is to say where each of its three coordinate axes points. Let the unit vectors along the three axes of {b} be $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b, \hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_b, \hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_b$; their components in {s} are three column vectors of length three. Placing these three column vectors side by side in a matrix, we write

$$
R_{sb} = \begin{pmatrix} \hat x_b & \hat y_b & \hat z_b \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix}
\hat x_b \cdot \hat x_s & \hat y_b \cdot \hat x_s & \hat z_b \cdot \hat x_s \\
\hat x_b \cdot \hat y_s & \hat y_b \cdot \hat y_s & \hat z_b \cdot \hat y_s \\
\hat x_b \cdot \hat z_s & \hat y_b \cdot \hat z_s & \hat z_b \cdot \hat z_s
\end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.2.1}
$$

The second equality uses a result of Chapter 3: the components of a vector in a frame are its dot products with the unit vectors of the axes of that frame. So every entry of $R_{sb}$ is the cosine of the angle between two axes; these are the direction cosines of Chapter 3. The subscript sb is read "{b} relative to {s}".

These nine numbers are not independent of one another. The three axes are unit vectors, which gives 3 conditions; the three axes are mutually perpendicular, which gives 3 more. Nine numbers bound by 6 conditions leave only 3 that can be chosen freely. This is exactly the number of degrees of freedom of a rotation in space: describing the orientation of a rigid body takes 3 independent numbers, no more and no fewer. The nine numbers form a "redundant" description, whose advantage is that computing with it is simple, as we shall soon see.

The six conditions can be written as one matrix equation. That the three axes are unit vectors and mutually perpendicular is equivalent to

$$
R_{sb}^{\mathsf T} R_{sb} = I , \tag{4.2.2}
$$

for the same reason as in Theorem 4.1.1: the (i, j) entry of the product is the dot product of column i with column j. Moreover, {b} is right-handed, that is, $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b \times \hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_b = \hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_b$. By the scalar triple product formula of Chapter 3, the determinant of a matrix equals the triple product of its three columns, so

$$
\det R_{sb} = (\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b \times \hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_b) \cdot \hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_b = \hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_b \cdot \hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_b = 1 . \tag{4.2.3}
$$

If {b} were left-handed, the determinant would be −1. A left-handed frame cannot be obtained from a right-handed one by turning, only by a reflection, so a matrix with det = −1 does not represent a rotation.

**Definition 4.2.1 (Special orthogonal group)** The set of all real 3×3 matrices with $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ and $\det R = 1$ is called the three-dimensional **special orthogonal group** and is written SO(3). Its matrices are called spatial rotation matrices.

In the name, "orthogonal" refers to $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$; a matrix satisfying it is called an **orthogonal matrix**. "Special" refers to the determinant being +1, which rules out reflections. What "group" means is explained in Section 4.2.5. The SO(2) of the previous section is the same idea in the plane.

### 4.2.3 Elementary rotations about the coordinate axes

The simplest rotation in space is a turn through some angle about one of the coordinate axes. Let {b} start out coinciding with {s} and then turn through θ about the $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_s$ axis. The z axis itself does not move, so $\hat z_b = (0, 0, 1)^{\mathsf T}$; the x and y axes turn through θ in the xy plane, which is just the plane rotation of the previous section, and Figure 4.1.2 shows that $\hat x_b = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)^{\mathsf T}$ and $\hat y_b = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)^{\mathsf T}$. Arranging them in a matrix as in Eq. (4.2.1):

$$
\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.2.4}
$$

The same reasoning applies to rotations about the x axis and the y axis; we need only rename the axes in turn. In a rotation about the x axis, y turns towards z; in a rotation about the y axis, z turns towards x. By the right-hand rule, in all three cases "which axis turns towards which" follows the cyclic order x→y→z→x. Hence

$$
\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, \theta) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\mathrm{Rot}(\hat y, \theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}. \tag{4.2.5}
$$

Beginners often go wrong with $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat y, \theta)$: its −sin θ is in the lower left corner, whereas in the other two matrices the −sin θ is in the upper right. The reason is precisely the cyclic order. In a rotation about the y axis, z turns towards x, not x towards z. If x is written first and z second, the positions of the signs swap. The way to remember this is not to memorize the matrix but to write it out afresh each time by asking which axis stays still, in which plane the other two axes turn, and which turns towards which.

**Example 4.2.1** The orientation of the tool frame {b} relative to the base frame {s} is $R_{sb} = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, 30^\circ)$, that is,

$$
R_{sb} = {{ex4_2.Rsb}} .
$$

A point on the tool has components $p_b = (0.1, 0, 0.05)^{\mathsf T}$ m in {b}. Find its components in {s}.

By the subscript-cancellation rule (see the notation conventions), $p_s = R_{sb}\,p_b$:

$$
p_s = {{ex4_2.Rsb}} \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 \\ 0 \\ 0.05 \end{pmatrix} = {{ex4_2.p_s}}\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

The z component is unchanged and the x and y components have undergone a plane rotation of 30°, which agrees entirely with the intuitive picture of "turning about the z axis".

### 4.2.4 Three uses of a rotation matrix

In robotics the same rotation matrix is used in three ways. Mathematically they are one and the same thing, but they are read differently; beginners often mix them up, so they are worth setting apart clearly.

**First, as an orientation.** $R_{sb}$ describes the orientation of {b} relative to {s}, and its three columns are the three axes of {b}. This is the original meaning of Eq. (4.2.1).

**Second, as a change of coordinates.** The components of the same vector in two frames are related by

$$
p_s = R_{sb}\, p_b . \tag{4.2.6}
$$

The reason is this: the three components $(p_1, p_2, p_3)$ of $p_b$ say that the vector equals $p_1 \hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b + p_2 \hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_b + p_3 \hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_b$; replacing the three axes by their components in {s}, that is, by the three columns of $R_{sb}$, and adding them with these three coefficients is exactly multiplying the matrix by the column vector. Example 4.2.1 is this use.

**Third, as a rotation operator.** A vector (or a frame) is actually turned through some angle, giving a new vector, and both are expressed in the same frame:

$$
v' = R\, v . \tag{4.2.7}
$$

This is the active rotation of Section 4.1 extended to space. The word "operator" stresses what R does: it turns one vector into another.

The difference between the second and third uses is exactly the difference between passive and active rotation in Section 4.1.6: in one, the vector stays put and we read it against another set of axes; in the other, the axes stay put and the vector is turned. In a program both are the same line of matrix multiplication, which is all the more reason to be clear, before writing that line, about which of the two we are doing.

### 4.2.5 Composing rotations: the group and the order

Two rotations carried out one after the other are still a rotation. Suppose rotation $R_1$ is done first and then rotation $R_2$, both operators acting on vectors in the same frame. A vector first becomes $R_1 v$ and then $R_2 (R_1 v) = (R_2 R_1) v$, so the combined rotation is the product $R_2 R_1$. Note the order: the one done later is written on the left.

**Theorem 4.2.1** Under matrix multiplication SO(3) has the following four properties:

1. closure: if $R_1, R_2 \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$, then $R_1 R_2 \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$;
2. associativity: $(R_1 R_2) R_3 = R_1 (R_2 R_3)$;
3. identity: the identity matrix $I \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$, and $IR = RI = R$;
4. inverse: every $R \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ has an inverse $R^{-1} = R^{\mathsf T} \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$.

*Proof*　(1) $(R_1R_2)^{\mathsf T}(R_1R_2) = R_2^{\mathsf T}(R_1^{\mathsf T}R_1)R_2 = R_2^{\mathsf T}R_2 = I$, and $\det(R_1R_2) = \det R_1 \det R_2 = 1$. (2) Matrix multiplication is associative in any case. (3) Obvious. (4) By Eq. (4.2.2), $R^{\mathsf T}$ is the inverse of R; moreover $(R^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}R^{\mathsf T} = RR^{\mathsf T} = I$ and $\det R^{\mathsf T} = \det R = 1$, so $R^{\mathsf T} \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$.　∎

A set with an operation satisfying these four properties is called a **group** in mathematics. The idea of a group was introduced by Évariste Galois around 1830 in his study of the roots of equations, and later became the basic language for describing symmetry and transformations. For robotics, the four properties of a group say something plain: a rotation followed by a rotation is still a rotation, a rotation can be undone, and doing nothing also counts as a rotation.

The definition of a group does not include commutativity. For plane rotations, $R(\alpha)R(\beta) = R(\alpha+\beta) = R(\beta)R(\alpha)$, and the order does not matter (Exercise 4.1.3). Rotations in space, however, cannot in general be swapped in order, and this is the most important difference between rotations in space and rotations in the plane.

**Example 4.2.2** Lay a book flat on the table with its spine facing forward. Fix {s} to the table top: the x axis to the right, the y axis forward and the z axis up. The frame {b} on the book starts out coinciding with {s}, with the spine along $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_b$.

Method A: first turn 90° about $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_s$, then 90° about $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_s$. Method B: the two turns in the opposite order. The combined rotations of the two methods are

$$
\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, 90^\circ)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, 90^\circ) = {{ex4_2.A}}, \qquad
\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, 90^\circ)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, 90^\circ) = {{ex4_2.B}} .
$$

The two matrices are different. The second column tells where the spine ends up: in method A the spine points along ${{ex4_2.spine_A}}$, straight up; in method B it points along ${{ex4_2.spine_B}}$, horizontally to the left. The reader may like to try it with a real book; the result agrees exactly with the computation (Figure 4.2.2).

### 4.2.6 Rotation about fixed axes and rotation about body axes

Both turns in Example 4.2.2 are about axes of {s}. These axes are fixed to the table top and do not turn with the book; this is called **rotation about a fixed axis**. Another way of speaking, just as common, is to turn about the book's own axes, for example "turn the end-effector 45° about its own x axis". Here the axis moves together with the body; this is called **rotation about a body axis**, also rotation about a moving axis.

The two ways of speaking correspond to different matrix operations. Let the current orientation of {b} be $R_{sb}$, and suppose one more rotation is to be made, its amount given by the matrix R; the frame after the turn is written {b′}.

**Theorem 4.2.2** (1) If the axis of rotation is an axis of {s}, that is, R is expressed in {s}, then $R_{sb'} = R\,R_{sb}$ (premultiplication). (2) If the axis of rotation is an axis of {b} itself, that is, R is expressed in {b}, then $R_{sb'} = R_{sb}\,R$ (postmultiplication).

*Proof*　(1) R is a rotation operator in {s}; it turns each axis of {b} (each column of $R_{sb}$) into the corresponding axis of {b′}. By Eq. (4.2.7) each column is premultiplied by R, so $R_{sb'} = R\,R_{sb}$.

(2) That the rotation is described in {b} means that the orientation of {b′} relative to {b} is R: the components of the axes of {b′} in {b} are the columns of R. By Eq. (4.2.6), turning components in {b} into components in {s} only takes a premultiplication by $R_{sb}$, so the components of the axes of {b′} in {s} are $R_{sb}$ times the columns of R, that is, $R_{sb'} = R_{sb}\,R$. With the subscript-cancellation rule to be proved in Chapter 5, this reads $R_{sb'} = R_{sb}R_{bb'}$.　∎

The theorem can be remembered in one sentence: **premultiply for fixed axes, postmultiply for body axes**. It is used again and again in later chapters: the product-of-exponentials formula of Chapter 12 is written as a string of matrix products, and reading which axis each factor turns about relies on exactly this rule.

**Example 4.2.3** Starting from the orientation $R_{sb} = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, 30^\circ)$ of Example 4.2.1, turn 45° about the fixed axis $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_s$, and separately 45° about the body axis $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b$.

About the fixed axis (premultiplication):

$$
\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, 45^\circ)\, R_{sb} = {{ex4_2.R_fixed}} .
$$

About the body axis (postmultiplication):

$$
R_{sb}\, \mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, 45^\circ) = {{ex4_2.R_body}} .
$$

Both results satisfy Eqs. (4.2.2) and (4.2.3), but they are not the same. Check this against intuition: when turning about the body axis $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b$ itself does not move, so the first column of the postmultiplied result is the same as the first column of $R_{sb}$; when turning about the fixed axis $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_s$, the components of every axis along $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_s$ do not change, so the first row of the premultiplied result is the same as the first row of $R_{sb}$.

A plane rotation needs only one angle, whereas the rotation matrix of a rotation in space has nine numbers and three degrees of freedom. The next section proves a theorem given by Euler in 1775: however complicated the rotation matrix, every rotation in space can be seen as a turn through some angle about one fixed axis. This points the way to describing a rotation by three numbers.

**Engineer's notes**

- Robot controllers, ROS and the various simulation packages do not agree on what "orientation" means: some give $R_{sb}$, others its transpose $R_{bs}$. Before reading data, test with a known orientation (for example a 90° turn about z) to see whether the columns or the rows of the matrix are the axes of {b}.
- On a teach pendant, "tool-frame jogging" is rotation about body axes and "base-frame jogging" is rotation about fixed axes. Operators on the shop floor often confuse the two, and the end-effector drifts further off the more they adjust it.
- After many rotation matrices have been multiplied together, numerical error makes the result drift slightly out of SO(3); it needs to be orthonormalized from time to time (Section 4.8).
