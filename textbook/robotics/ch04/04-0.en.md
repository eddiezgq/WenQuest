title: Chapter Overview
---

Before a robot can grasp, assemble, weld or take a picture, it must be able to say which way its hand is "facing". A position can be given by three coordinates, but an orientation is not so simple: the same rotations carried out in a different order give a different result, and three angles that look intuitive suddenly fail at certain orientations. This chapter is devoted to the rotation of rigid bodies and answers three questions: how to describe an orientation with numbers, how to convert between these descriptions, and how to turn smoothly from one orientation to another.

The chapter starts from rotation in the plane, introduces the rotation matrix, and extends it to space, arriving at the special orthogonal group SO(3). On this basis it follows a line that is both historical and logical:

- in 1775 Euler proved that every rotation is a turn through some angle about some axis;
- in 1840 Rodrigues gave a neat formula for computing a rotation from its axis and angle, which can also be seen as a matrix exponential;
- in 1843 Hamilton discovered quaternions, which give a representation with no singularities that is also fast to compute with.

Euler angles, widely used in engineering, and their gimbal-lock problem are also explained along this line. The chapter ends with orientation interpolation, orientation error and the correction of numerical error, all of which a robot controller deals with every day.

The chapter uses the matrix operations, eigenvalues and singular value decomposition of Chapter 2, and the vectors, dot products, cross products and skew-symmetric matrices of Chapter 3. Section 4.4.4 uses derivatives and infinite series; readers who have only studied high-school mathematics may skip that subsection at first without losing the thread of what follows. The results of this chapter are the foundation of the later chapters: Chapter 5 combines rotation and translation into the pose of a rigid body, and Chapter 6 starts from Rodrigues' formula and extends it to screws and the product of exponentials, which is the central thread of the book.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. write down the rotation matrix for a rotation about a coordinate axis and about an arbitrary axis, and explain the geometric meaning of the columns of a rotation matrix;
2. distinguish the three uses of a rotation matrix, and determine correctly that a rotation about a fixed axis is composed by premultiplication and a rotation about a body axis by postmultiplication;
3. find the rotation axis and angle from a rotation matrix, convert between rotation matrices, axis–angle, Euler angles and quaternions, and handle correctly special cases such as angles of 0 and π;
4. explain what causes gimbal lock, and state where Euler angles are appropriate in engineering practice;
5. plan the rotation between two orientations with spherical linear interpolation, measure orientation error by the error angle, and orthonormalize a matrix with the singular value decomposition.
