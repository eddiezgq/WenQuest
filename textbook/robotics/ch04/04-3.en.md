### 4.3.1 The problem

In Example 4.2.3 the end-effector first turned 30° about the z axis of {s} and then 45° about its own x axis, and its final orientation was

$$
R = {{ex4_3.R}} .
$$

Now imagine that a new robot arm is to turn straight from the initial orientation to this one in a single step. Is there an axis such that one turn through some angle about it gets there at once? And if there is, how are this axis and this angle to be found from the nine numbers?

This is no idle question. Motion planning for a robot arm often needs to turn "as directly as possible" between two orientations; an orientation sensor gives a rotation matrix, while the engineer wants to know "how many degrees has it actually turned, and about which axis". Euler's rotation theorem, presented in this section, answers the first question: such an axis always exists. Its proof answers the second question at the same time.

### 4.3.2 Statement and history of the theorem

In 1775 Leonhard Euler submitted a paper on the motion of rigid bodies to the St Petersburg Academy of Sciences; it was published the following year. In it he proved a result about a sphere: whatever motion a sphere makes about its centre, there is always one diameter whose position is the same before and after the motion. In today's language it is the following theorem.

**Theorem 4.3.1 (Euler's rotation theorem)** Let $R \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$. Then there exist a unit vector $\hat\omega$ and an angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ such that R represents a turn through θ about $\hat\omega$. That is, $R\hat\omega = \hat\omega$, and in the plane perpendicular to $\hat\omega$, R is a plane rotation through the angle θ.

The line along $\hat\omega$ is called the **rotation axis** of the rotation. Euler's own proof was purely geometric. The proof given below uses the eigenvalues of Chapter 2; it is shorter, and it tells us directly how to compute.

### 4.3.3 Proof: the axis always exists

Points on the axis do not move under the rotation, so the direction $\hat\omega$ of the axis satisfies $R\hat\omega = \hat\omega$. In the language of Chapter 2, $\hat\omega$ is an eigenvector of R with eigenvalue 1. So proving that the axis exists means proving that 1 is an eigenvalue of R, that is, proving

$$
\det(R - I) = 0 . \tag{4.3.1}
$$

**Lemma 4.3.1** If $R \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$, then $\det(R - I) = 0$.

*Proof*　Since $RR^{\mathsf T} = I$, we can write $R - I$ as $R - RR^{\mathsf T} = R\,(I - R^{\mathsf T})$. Take determinants of both sides, and use $\det R = 1$ and the fact that transposing does not change a determinant:

$$
\det(R - I) = \det R \cdot \det(I - R^{\mathsf T}) = \det\big((I - R)^{\mathsf T}\big) = \det(I - R) .
$$

On the other hand, $I - R = -(R - I)$, and multiplying a whole 3×3 matrix by −1 multiplies its determinant by $(-1)^3 = -1$, so $\det(I - R) = -\det(R - I)$. Together the two equations give $\det(R - I) = -\det(R - I)$, hence $\det(R - I) = 0$.　∎

Note that the proof uses the fact that the order of the matrix, 3, is odd. In the plane $(-1)^2 = 1$ and the argument breaks down; indeed, a plane rotation with an angle other than 0 leaves no direction unchanged. This is the algebraic reason why "in the plane we turn about a point, in space we turn about an axis".

*Proof of Theorem 4.3.1*　By Lemma 4.3.1 there is a nonzero vector with $R\hat\omega = \hat\omega$; normalize it to a unit vector.

Now look at the plane perpendicular to $\hat\omega$. Let v be any vector in this plane, that is, $v \cdot \hat\omega = 0$. A rotation preserves dot products (Chapter 3), so

$$
(Rv) \cdot \hat\omega = (Rv) \cdot (R\hat\omega) = v \cdot \hat\omega = 0 ,
$$

that is, Rv still lies in this plane. In other words, R maps the plane onto itself and preserves lengths and angles. In the plane, take two mutually perpendicular unit vectors u and w such that $(u, w, \hat\omega)$ is a right-handed frame. Seen with these three vectors as coordinate axes, the action of R is written as the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} A & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, where the 2×2 matrix A describes its action in the plane. Since R preserves length, A satisfies $A^{\mathsf T}A = I$; since a determinant does not change under a change of frame (Chapter 2), $\det A = \det R = 1$. By Section 4.1.5, A can only be some plane rotation R(θ). If the θ obtained is negative, replacing $\hat\omega$ by $-\hat\omega$ makes θ positive, so we can always take $\theta \in [0, \pi]$.　∎

### 4.3.4 Finding the angle: the trace is invariant

The axis is found as an eigenvector. The angle can be found from a very simple quantity, the **trace** of the matrix, the sum of its main-diagonal entries, written tr R.

Imagine changing to a frame whose z axis lies exactly along the rotation axis $\hat\omega$. Seen in this frame, the rotation is a turn through θ about the z axis, with the matrix $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \theta)$ of Eq. (4.2.4), whose trace is $\cos\theta + \cos\theta + 1$. Under a change of frame, the rotation matrix becomes $Q^{\mathsf T} R Q$ by the rule of Chapter 3, where Q is the rotation matrix between the two frames; and Chapter 2 has proved that $\mathrm{tr}(AB) = \mathrm{tr}(BA)$, so

$$
\mathrm{tr}(Q^{\mathsf T} R Q) = \mathrm{tr}(R Q Q^{\mathsf T}) = \mathrm{tr}\,R .
$$

The trace does not change with the frame. This gives a relation that holds in every frame:

$$
\mathrm{tr}\,R = 1 + 2\cos\theta , \qquad \theta = \arccos\frac{\mathrm{tr}\,R - 1}{2} . \tag{4.3.2}
$$

For the same reason (a change of frame does not change the eigenvalues, Chapter 2), the three eigenvalues of R are the three eigenvalues of $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \theta)$: $1$ and $\cos\theta \pm i\sin\theta$. The last two are complex numbers, and their argument is exactly the angle of rotation.

**Example 4.3.1** Find the rotation axis and the angle of the matrix R at the start of this section.

The trace of R is ${{ex4_3.tr}}$. By Eq. (4.3.2),

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{ {{ex4_3.tr}} - 1}{2} = {{ex4_3.cos_theta}}, \qquad \theta \approx {{ex4_3.theta_deg:.2f}}^\circ .
$$

Solve the equation $(R - I)\hat\omega = 0$ and take a unit solution. There are two unit solutions pointing in opposite directions; we should choose the one that makes the angle θ positive. The test is: take the direction for which $\hat\omega \cdot (r_{32} - r_{23},\ r_{13} - r_{31},\ r_{21} - r_{12})^{\mathsf T} > 0$; the reason is given by Eq. (4.4.10) in Section 4.4.6. This gives

$$
\hat\omega = {{ex4_3.w}} .
$$

Check: turning through ${{ex4_3.theta_deg:.2f}}^\circ$ about this axis by Rodrigues' formula of the next section gives a matrix identical to R; the other two eigenvalues of R are ${{ex4_3.ev_re}} \pm {{ex4_3.ev_im}}\,i$, whose real part is exactly cos θ and whose imaginary part is exactly sin θ.

So the two-step rotation of Example 4.2.3, "first 30° about the z axis, then 45° about the body's own x axis", is equivalent to a single turn of about {{ex4_3.theta_deg:.1f}}° about the axis above. This axis is neither the z axis nor the x axis.

### 4.3.5 Two remarks

**Axis and angle are not unique.** A turn through θ about $\hat\omega$ and a turn through $-\theta$ about $-\hat\omega$ are the same rotation; so are turns through θ and through $\theta + 2\pi$ about $\hat\omega$. We agree to take $\theta \in [0, \pi]$; then, apart from the two special cases below, the axis and the angle are unique:

- θ = 0, that is, R = I: there is no rotation, and any axis can serve as the "axis";
- θ = π: a half turn, where $\hat\omega$ and $-\hat\omega$ give the same result and both directions are correct.

These two cases need special handling in computation, and Section 4.4 gives concrete methods. Errors in robot programs caused by them are not rare.

**Two rotations combined are still a turn about one axis.** By Theorem 4.2.1 the product of two rotation matrices is still in SO(3); and by Theorem 4.3.1 this product is again a single turn about some axis.

**Corollary 4.3.1** The composition of two rotations about any two axes is equivalent to a single rotation about a third axis.

Example 4.3.1 is an instance of this corollary. It tells the robot designer that, although the wrist of a robot arm usually has three revolute joints in a row, each turning about its own axis, however they combine, the final change in the orientation of the end-effector can always be seen as a single turn about one axis. Section 4.7 uses this to plan the "most direct" rotation path between two orientations.

### 4.3.6 Axes of rotation in everyday life

When you push a door open, the door turns about its hinge, and the axis is plain to see. When you spin a globe, the axis is the rod through the North and South Poles. Euler's theorem says something less obvious: even a string of complicated tumbles, for example tipping a Rubik's cube forward and then turning it to the right, must end in a result that a single turn about some invisible axis could have produced.

Euler's theorem shows that every rotation is determined by one axis and one angle, 3 numbers in all: 2 for the direction of the axis and 1 for the angle. In the next section we start from the axis and the angle and, going the other way, write down the rotation matrix; this is Rodrigues' formula.

**Engineer's notes**

- When the angle is found with Eq. (4.3.2), numerical error can make $(\mathrm{tr}\,R - 1)/2$ slightly greater than 1 or slightly less than −1, and taking arccos directly then gives "not a number" (NaN). A program should first clip it to [−1, 1].
- When the angle is close to 0 or π, finding the axis from an eigenvector is numerically unstable. Engineering software generally uses the logarithm map of Section 4.4 instead, and treats these two cases separately.
- The most natural measure of "how far apart" two orientations are is the angle of $R_1^{\mathsf T}R_2$; Section 4.8 uses it to define the orientation error.
