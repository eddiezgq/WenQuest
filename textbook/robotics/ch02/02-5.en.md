### 2.5.1 The problem: where is the tip of the tool?

The robot controller knows where the flange (the disc at the end of the arm on which tools are mounted) is, because the pose of the flange is computed from the joint angles by the kinematic formulas. But once a tool such as a welding torch, a glue gun or a probe has been mounted, the position of the tool tip relative to the flange can only be estimated from the drawings, and the drawing dimensions, assembly errors and the bending of the torch all cause deviations of a few millimetres. The tool tip is called the **tool centre point** (TCP), and determining its position relative to the flange is called TCP calibration. The method that industrial robots generally offer is the "multi-point touch method": a sharp pin is fixed on the worktable, the robot touches the same pin point with the tool tip in several different orientations, and each time the flange pose shown by the controller is recorded.

To make the reasoning clear, we first look at the planar case (Figure 2.5.1). Let the position of the flange at the i-th touch be $p_i$ and its angle $\varphi_i$, both read from the controller; the position t of the tool tip in the flange frame is unknown, and so is the position c of the pin in the base frame. By Section 4.1, the point t of the flange frame is at $R(\varphi_i)\,t + p_i$ in the base frame, and this should be the pin point c:

$$
R(\varphi_i)\,t - c = -p_i , \qquad i = 1, 2, \ldots, N . \tag{2.5.1}
$$

The unknowns are t and c, 4 in all; each touch gives 2 equations. Two touches give 4 equations, from which all the unknowns can be solved. But no touch can be lined up with the pin point exactly, and the readings of the controller have errors too. With only two touches, all of these errors go into the result; with a few more touches, the errors may partly cancel. But N touches give 2N equations, more than the 4 unknowns, and because of the errors these equations contradict one another: no t and c can satisfy all of them at once. So what t and c are "best"?

### 2.5.2 Overdetermined systems and residuals

Stack the 2N equations of Eq. (2.5.1) together. The unknown vector is $x = (t_x, t_y, c_x, c_y)^{\mathsf T}$, and touch i contributes two rows:

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} R(\varphi_1) & -I_2 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ R(\varphi_N) & -I_2 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb R^{2N\times 4}, \qquad
b = \begin{pmatrix} -p_1 \\ \vdots \\ -p_N \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb R^{2N}, \qquad Ax = b . \tag{2.5.2}
$$

A system of linear equations with more equations than unknowns is called an **overdetermined system**. In general it has no solution: Ax can only range over the linear combinations of the columns of A, that is, the range of A, which is a "plane" of at most 4 dimensions in $\mathbb R^{2N}$; and a b that carries errors is almost certain not to lie exactly on it.

Since we cannot make every equation hold, we settle for the next best thing and make "how far they fail to hold" as small as possible. For any candidate x, the vector

$$
r = b - Ax \tag{2.5.3}
$$

is called the **residual**; its components are the differences between the two sides of each equation. We want to choose x so that the length of the residual is smallest.

**Definition 2.5.1 (Least-squares solution)** The x that makes $\|b - Ax\|^2 = \sum_i r_i^2$ smallest is called the **least squares** solution of the system Ax = b, and is written $\hat x$.

Why take the sum of squares, rather than the sum of absolute values or the largest one? There are three reasons. First, the sum of squares is smooth everywhere, and its minimum can be found exactly by linear algebra, as below; the result is the solution of a system of linear equations. Second, geometrically the sum of squares is the squared length of the residual vector, so minimizing it means finding the shortest distance from b to the range, and the answer is the foot of the perpendicular, which is very intuitive. Third, Carl Friedrich Gauss proved that if the measurement errors are independent random quantities with zero mean and equal variance, the least-squares solution has the smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators; and if the errors are also normally distributed, it is the maximum likelihood estimator. Chapter 9 discusses these statistical properties.

The invention of the method of least squares has a famous story behind it. On New Year's Day 1801, Giuseppe Piazzi discovered Ceres; after he had followed it for about 40 days it disappeared into the glare of the sun. The 24-year-old Gauss computed its orbit from these few dozen days of observations by his own method, and on that basis astronomers found it again at the end of the year. Adrien-Marie Legendre was the first to publish the method of least squares, in 1805, and gave it its present name; when Gauss published it in 1809, he said that he had been using it since 1795. A 1981 paper by Stephen M. Stigler discusses this dispute over priority.

### 2.5.3 The normal equations

**Theorem 2.5.1 (Normal equations)** $\hat x$ is a least-squares solution of Ax = b if and only if the residual $b - A\hat x$ is orthogonal to every column of A, that is,

$$
A^{\mathsf T}A\,\hat x = A^{\mathsf T}b . \tag{2.5.4}
$$

If the columns of A are linearly independent (Section 2.6), $A^{\mathsf T}A$ is invertible and the least-squares solution is unique: $\hat x = (A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}b$.

*Proof*　Let $\hat x$ satisfy Eq. (2.5.4) and write $\hat r = b - A\hat x$; then $A^{\mathsf T}\hat r = 0$. For any x,

$$
\|b - Ax\|^2 = \|\hat r + A(\hat x - x)\|^2 = \|\hat r\|^2 + 2(\hat x - x)^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\hat r + \|A(\hat x - x)\|^2 = \|\hat r\|^2 + \|A(\hat x - x)\|^2 . \tag{2.5.5}
$$

The last term is non-negative, so $\|b - Ax\|^2 \ge \|b - A\hat x\|^2$, and $\hat x$ is a least-squares solution. This is Pythagoras' theorem: the residual $\hat r$ is perpendicular to the vector $A(\hat x - x)$ in the range. Conversely, if $A^{\mathsf T}(b - A\hat x) = g \neq 0$, take $x = \hat x + \varepsilon g$ with ε a small positive number; expanding shows that $\|b - Ax\|^2 = \|b - A\hat x\|^2 - 2\varepsilon\|g\|^2 + O(\varepsilon^2)$, which is smaller than before, so $\hat x$ is not a least-squares solution. Finally, if $A^{\mathsf T}Az = 0$, then $\|Az\|^2 = z^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}Az = 0$, so Az = 0; when the columns of A are linearly independent, z = 0, so $A^{\mathsf T}A$ is invertible.　∎

Eq. (2.5.4) is called the **normal equations**; "normal" here means "perpendicular": the residual is perpendicular to the range. Figure 2.5.2 shows what this means geometrically. $A\hat x$ is the **orthogonal projection** of b onto the range of A, that is, the point of the range nearest to b. When the columns of A are linearly independent, the matrix that takes b to $A\hat x$ is

$$
P = A(A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T} , \qquad P^2 = P , \qquad P^{\mathsf T} = P . \tag{2.5.6}
$$

$P^2 = P$ says that projecting twice is the same as projecting once; $P^{\mathsf T} = P$ says that the projection is a "perpendicular" one. Section 2.7 shows that when the columns of A are mutually orthogonal and of length 1, this expression simplifies to $P = AA^{\mathsf T}$.

### 2.5.4 Back to TCP calibration

**Example 2.5.1** The robot touches the same pin in 5 orientations, with angles {{ex2_5.phis_deg}}, and the flange positions read off (one $p_i^{\mathsf T}$ per row, in mm) are

$$
\begin{pmatrix} p_1^{\mathsf T} \\ \vdots \\ p_5^{\mathsf T} \end{pmatrix} = {{ex2_5.P}} .
$$

These data were generated by Program 2.5.1: the true values were taken as t = (120, 35) mm and c = (650, 150) mm, and a random error with a standard deviation of 0.3 mm was added at each touch. The advantage of doing it this way is that we can check how far the calibration result is from the true values.

By Eq. (2.5.2), A is a 10×4 matrix whose first two rows are ${{ex2_5.A_top}}$. The coefficient matrix and the right-hand side of the normal equations are

$$
A^{\mathsf T}A = {{ex2_5.AtA}} , \qquad A^{\mathsf T}b = {{ex2_5.Atb}}\ \mathrm{mm} .
$$

Solving gives the tool tip $\hat t = {{ex2_5.t_hat}}$ mm and the pin $\hat c = {{ex2_5.c_hat}}$ mm. Compared with the true values, the error of t is {{ex2_5.err_t:.3f}} mm and the error of c is {{ex2_5.err_c:.3f}} mm; the root mean square of the residual is {{ex2_5.rms:.3f}} mm, comparable to the error of each touch (0.3 mm). Program 2.5.1 verifies that the residual is orthogonal to all four columns of A, and solves the problem by three other methods, QR decomposition, singular value decomposition and the pseudoinverse, with the same result.

Using only touches 1 and 5, A is a 4×4 square matrix that can be inverted directly, and the resulting error of t is {{ex2_5.err_t2:.3f}} mm. A comparison based on a single trial is partly a matter of chance, so Program 2.5.1 also runs 4,000 simulations: the root-mean-square error of t is {{ex2_5.mc2:.3f}} mm with 2 touches, {{ex2_5.mc5:.3f}} mm with 5 and {{ex2_5.mc10:.3f}} mm with 10. As the number of touches grows the error shrinks, but not quickly. The reason is that the angles of all the orientations lie between −40° and 80°; the more the angles of the orientations differ, the smaller the condition number of A and the more accurate the calibration, and this is more effective than simply adding touches (Exercise 2.5.3). Real three-dimensional TCP calibration works on exactly the same principle, except that R(φ) is replaced by a 3×3 rotation matrix (Chapter 4); the common "four-point method" is the case N = 4.

### 2.5.5 The pseudoinverse

By Theorem 2.5.1, when the columns of A are linearly independent, the least-squares solution is $\hat x = A^+b$, where

$$
A^+ = (A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T} . \tag{2.5.7}
$$

$A^+$ plays the part of an "inverse matrix": it satisfies $A^+A = I$, but $AA^+ = P \neq I$ (unless A is an invertible square matrix). It is called the **pseudoinverse** of A. When the columns are linearly dependent, $A^{\mathsf T}A$ is not invertible and Eq. (2.5.7) fails; and when there are fewer equations than unknowns, the situation is different again. The singular value decomposition gives a unified definition.

**Definition 2.5.2 (Pseudoinverse)** Let $A = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}$, with non-zero singular values $\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_r$. The pseudoinverse of A is

$$
A^+ = V\Sigma^+U^{\mathsf T} = \sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{\sigma_i}\,v_iu_i^{\mathsf T} , \tag{2.5.8}
$$

where $\Sigma^+$ is the n×m matrix obtained by transposing Σ and replacing each non-zero $\sigma_i$ with $1/\sigma_i$, the zeros staying zero.

Compare Eq. (2.4.5), $A = \sum_i\sigma_iu_iv_i^{\mathsf T}$: each channel of A magnifies the $v_i$ direction by $\sigma_i$ and sends it to the $u_i$ direction; the pseudoinverse reverses each channel, shrinking the $u_i$ direction by $\sigma_i$ and sending it back to the $v_i$ direction; and the directions that A squashes to zero the pseudoinverse does not try to "restore" but sets to zero. When the columns of A are linearly independent, Eq. (2.5.8) is the same as Eq. (2.5.7) (Exercise 2.5.2).

**Theorem 2.5.2 (Penrose conditions)** For any real matrix A there is exactly one matrix X that satisfies all of

$$
AXA = A , \qquad XAX = X , \qquad (AX)^{\mathsf T} = AX , \qquad (XA)^{\mathsf T} = XA , \tag{2.5.9}
$$

and it is the $A^+$ of Eq. (2.5.8).

*Proof*　Existence: substituting Eq. (2.5.8), $AA^+ = U\Sigma\Sigma^+U^{\mathsf T}$ and $A^+A = V\Sigma^+\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}$, where $\Sigma\Sigma^+$ and $\Sigma^+\Sigma$ are both diagonal matrices with 1 or 0 on the diagonal; the four conditions can be checked one by one. Uniqueness: let X and Y both satisfy the four conditions. Then $X = XAX = X(AX)^{\mathsf T} = XX^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T} = XX^{\mathsf T}(AYA)^{\mathsf T} = X(AX)^{\mathsf T}(AY)^{\mathsf T} = XAXAY = XAY$; in the same way $Y = YAY = (YA)^{\mathsf T}Y = A^{\mathsf T}Y^{\mathsf T}Y = (AXA)^{\mathsf T}Y^{\mathsf T}Y = (XA)^{\mathsf T}(YA)^{\mathsf T}Y = XAYAY = XAY$. So X = Y.　∎

The idea of the pseudoinverse was put forward by E. H. Moore in 1920 and rediscovered independently by Arne Bjerhammar in 1951 and Roger Penrose in 1955, so it is also called the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse. The four conditions given by Penrose became its standard definition.

### 2.5.6 Fewer equations than unknowns: the minimum-norm solution

Another use of the pseudoinverse is just the opposite of least squares: there are fewer equations than unknowns, there are infinitely many solutions, and we want to pick the "best" one among them. Such a system is called an **underdetermined system**.

The planar 3R arm of Section 2.6 (with the dimensions of Chapter 12, $L_3 = 0.1$ m) has three joints, but the end-effector position has only two coordinates, so the Jacobian matrix J is 2×3. If the end-effector is to move with velocity v, $J\dot\theta = v$ is two equations in three unknowns. If the two rows of J are linearly independent, it has infinitely many solutions. The natural choice is to make the joints turn "least", that is, to make $\|\dot\theta\|$ smallest.

**Theorem 2.5.3 (Minimum-norm solution)** Let the rows of an m×n matrix A be linearly independent (m ≤ n). Then for any b, the shortest of all the solutions of Ax = b is

$$
x^+ = A^{\mathsf T}(AA^{\mathsf T})^{-1}b = A^+b , \tag{2.5.10}
$$

called the **minimum-norm solution**. It is orthogonal to the null space of A (Section 2.6), and every other solution equals $x^+$ plus a vector in the null space.

*Proof*　The rows are linearly independent, so $AA^{\mathsf T}$ is invertible (as in the proof of Theorem 2.5.1, arguing with $A^{\mathsf T}$), and $Ax^+ = AA^{\mathsf T}(AA^{\mathsf T})^{-1}b = b$, so $x^+$ is a solution. Let x be any solution and write $z = x - x^+$; then Az = 0. Since $x^+ = A^{\mathsf T}y$ (with $y = (AA^{\mathsf T})^{-1}b$), $z^{\mathsf T}x^+ = (Az)^{\mathsf T}y = 0$, so $\|x\|^2 = \|x^+\|^2 + \|z\|^2 \ge \|x^+\|^2$. Finally, substituting $A = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}$ one can check that $A^{\mathsf T}(AA^{\mathsf T})^{-1} = V\Sigma^+U^{\mathsf T}$.　∎

**Example 2.5.2** For the planar 3R arm at θ = (30°, 60°, −60°),

$$
J = {{ex2_5.J3}} , \qquad J^+ = {{ex2_5.Jp}} .
$$

If the end-effector is to move at 0.1 m/s in the x direction, the minimum-norm solution is $\dot\theta = J^+v = {{ex2_5.thd}}$ rad/s, of length {{ex2_5.thd_norm:.4f}} rad/s. Another solution, $\dot\theta' = {{ex2_5.thd_other}}$ rad/s, also moves the end-effector at 0.1 m/s in the x direction, but its length is {{ex2_5.other_norm:.4f}} rad/s. The difference between the two lies along the null-space direction ${{ex2_5.n}}$ of J; joint motion in this direction does not change the position of the end-effector, and Section 2.6 discusses it in detail.

Using the pseudoinverse to convert end-effector velocities into joint velocities is the core of the "resolved motion rate control" proposed by Daniel E. Whitney in 1969, and it is still the starting point for controlling redundant robot arms (Chapter 17).

### 2.5.7 How to solve numerically

The normal equations (2.5.4) are perfect in theory, but numerically they have a drawback.

**Theorem 2.5.4** Let the columns of an m×n matrix A (m ≥ n) be linearly independent. Then

$$
\kappa(A^{\mathsf T}A) = \kappa(A)^2 . \tag{2.5.11}
$$

*Proof*　From $A = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}$, $A^{\mathsf T}A = V\Sigma^{\mathsf T}\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}$, whose singular values are the $\sigma_i^2$; the ratio of the largest to the smallest is $(\sigma_1/\sigma_n)^2$.　∎

By Section 2.4.4, the condition number decides how much errors are magnified. If the condition number of A is $10^4$, solving the normal equations directly amounts to computing on a problem with condition number $10^8$, and of the roughly 16 significant digits of double-precision floating point, 8 are lost at a stroke. For Example 2.5.1, κ(A) = {{ex2_5.condA:.3f}} and $\kappa(A^{\mathsf T}A) = {{ex2_5.condAtA:.3f}}$; the problem is well-conditioned, and any method will do. But in the identification of dynamic parameters in Chapter 26, condition numbers in the thousands are common, and care is needed. There are two safer approaches. One is QR decomposition: write A as QR (Section 2.7) and solve $R\hat x = Q^{\mathsf T}b$, without forming $A^{\mathsf T}A$. The other is the singular value decomposition: solve by Eq. (2.5.8), which also gives a clear diagnosis when the columns of A are nearly dependent. `numpy.linalg.lstsq` in the numerical library uses the latter. Chapters 4 and 5 of Solomon's book compare these methods in detail.

### 2.5.8 An everyday example

**Example 2.5.3** Weights of 0 to 5 kg are hung one after another on a spring scale, and the extension y of the spring is read: 0.3, 10.2, 19.6, 30.4, 40.1, 49.6 mm. Fit the straight line $y = km + b_0$; the unknowns are k and $b_0$, and there are six equations. Solving by Eq. (2.5.4) gives $k = {{ex2_5.k:.3f}}$ mm/kg and $b_0 = {{ex2_5.b0:.3f}}$ mm, and the sum of squared residuals is {{ex2_5.sse:.3f}} mm². Figure 2.5.3 shows the fitted line and the residuals.

::: Figure 2.5.3
src: fig2_5_3

For line fitting, the two rows of the normal equations have a clear meaning. The second column of A is all ones, and "the residual is orthogonal to it" means that the residuals sum to zero, that is, the fitted line passes through the "centre of gravity" $(\bar m, \bar y)$ of the data. The first column of A consists of the values of m, and "the residual is orthogonal to it" means that the residuals are uncorrelated with m: no linear trend is left. This is exactly what we intuitively ask of "a good fit": the line is neither too high nor too low, and no systematic tilt can be seen in the residuals. This is just what is done when kitchen scales and bathroom scales are calibrated at the factory.

This section has used the "null space" twice: the least-squares solution is not unique when the columns are linearly dependent, and the minimum-norm solution is orthogonal to the null space. The next section is devoted to the null space and the range, which answer when a system of linear equations has a solution and how many solutions it has.

**Engineer's notes**

- In TCP calibration, make the orientations of the touches differ as much as possible (a wide range of angles, axes of rotation in varied directions); this improves accuracy more than simply adding touches. If the orientations differ too little, the columns of A are nearly dependent and the calibration result is extremely sensitive to errors.
- Always look at the residuals after calibration. The root mean square of the residuals should be comparable to the measurement error; if the residual of one touch is particularly large, the tool tip probably did not meet the pin squarely on that touch, and it should be removed and the computation repeated. Least squares is very sensitive to such "outliers"; Chapter 9 introduces more robust methods.
- To solve a least-squares problem, prefer `lstsq` (SVD) or QR decomposition; solve the normal equations directly only when A is well-conditioned, the problem is large and speed is needed.
- When the equations have different units or accuracies (for example, millimetres and radians together), weight them by accuracy first, or the less accurate equations will dominate the result. This is weighted least squares, discussed in Chapter 9.
- Near a singularity of A, the pseudoinverse magnifies the reciprocals of the very small singular values. When a robot arm is near a singular configuration, computing joint velocities with $J^+$ gives huge values; the damped least squares of Chapter 15 was designed for exactly this.
