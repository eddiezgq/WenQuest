### 2.3.1 The problem: giving way in the direction of the push

The compliant wrist of Section 2.2 has an awkward property: push it along $x_s$, and the restoring force points off in another direction. For assembly this means that when a part is pushed to one side, the wrist carries it off at a slant. Yet Figure 2.2.2 also shows that there are two special directions along which the force lies exactly on the line of the push; they are the wrist's own axes $x_b$ and $y_b$.

In the last section we knew the axes of the wrist first and then computed $K_s$. In practice it is often the other way round: once the wrist has been mounted, it is loaded direction by direction in the base frame and the displacements are measured, giving a measured stiffness matrix

$$
K = \begin{pmatrix} 1600 & 650 \\ 650 & 900 \end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{N/m} .
$$

Because of manufacturing and mounting errors, it does not agree exactly with the figures in the datasheet. The question is: from this matrix, how do we find the "stiff" and "soft" directions of the wrist, and the stiffness along each of them? In other words, in what frame does this matrix become diagonal? This is the eigenvalue problem.

### 2.3.2 Definition of eigenvalues and eigenvectors

A linear transformation both stretches and turns most vectors. What we are looking for are the vectors that are only stretched and not turned.

**Definition 2.3.1 (Eigenvalue and eigenvector)** Let A be an n×n matrix. If a number λ and a non-zero vector v satisfy

$$
A v = \lambda v , \tag{2.3.1}
$$

then λ is called an **eigenvalue** of A, and v an **eigenvector** of A belonging to λ.

v must be non-zero, or every λ would satisfy Eq. (2.3.1). If v is an eigenvector, so is every non-zero multiple cv, because A(cv) = c Av = λ(cv); so what is really determined is the line on which the eigenvector lies, the "eigendirection", and a unit vector on it is usually taken to represent it. λ tells us the factor of stretching along this direction: λ > 1 lengthens, 0 < λ < 1 shortens, λ < 0 also reverses the direction, and λ = 0 squashes the direction to zero. Figure 2.3.1 shows what two matrices do to a ring of vectors on the unit circle: most of the arrows are turned, and only the arrows along the eigendirections are merely stretched.

The prefix "eigen" is the German word *eigen*, meaning "own" or "inherent": eigenvalues and eigenvectors are things that belong to the transformation the matrix represents. The name comes from the work of David Hilbert on integral equations around 1904, but the problem itself is much older. Leonhard Euler, studying the rotation of rigid bodies, found that every rigid body has three "principal axes", and that when it turns about a principal axis its angular momentum points the same way as its angular velocity; Joseph-Louis Lagrange and Pierre-Simon Laplace met the same kind of problem in celestial mechanics; and Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved in 1829 that the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are all real, which is Theorem 2.3.3 below.

### 2.3.3 The characteristic equation

Eq. (2.3.1) can be rewritten as $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$. That it has a non-zero solution v means that the matrix $A - \lambda I$ squashes a non-zero vector to zero, so it is not invertible (Section 2.2.3). By Theorem 2.1.4,

$$
\det(\lambda I - A) = 0 . \tag{2.3.2}
$$

This is called the characteristic equation of A; its left-hand side is a polynomial of degree n in λ, the characteristic polynomial of Section 2.2. To find the eigenvalues is to find the roots of the characteristic polynomial; once an eigenvalue λ has been found, the eigenvectors are obtained by solving the homogeneous system $(A - \lambda I)v = 0$.

For a 2×2 matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$,

$$
\det(\lambda I - A) = (\lambda - a)(\lambda - d) - bc = \lambda^2 - (\mathrm{tr}\,A)\,\lambda + \det A . \tag{2.3.3}
$$

By the quadratic formula,

$$
\lambda_{1,2} = \frac{\mathrm{tr}\,A}{2} \pm \sqrt{\Big(\frac{\mathrm{tr}\,A}{2}\Big)^2 - \det A} . \tag{2.3.4}
$$

The two eigenvalues depend only on the trace and the determinant. This agrees with the conclusion of Section 2.2: similar matrices have the same trace and determinant, and hence the same eigenvalues. By Vieta's formulas, $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = \mathrm{tr}\,A$ and $\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \det A$. This relation holds for matrices of any order.

**Theorem 2.3.1** Let the characteristic polynomial of an n×n matrix A factor over the complex numbers as $(\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2)\cdots(\lambda - \lambda_n)$ (repeated roots counted with their multiplicity). Then

$$
\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \cdots + \lambda_n = \mathrm{tr}\,A , \qquad \lambda_1 \lambda_2 \cdots \lambda_n = \det A . \tag{2.3.5}
$$

*Proof*　Compare two coefficients of $\det(\lambda I - A)$ and $\prod_i(\lambda - \lambda_i)$. The constant term: setting λ = 0, the left-hand side is $\det(-A) = (-1)^n\det A$ and the right-hand side is $(-1)^n\prod_i\lambda_i$. The $\lambda^{n-1}$ term: on the right it is $-\sum_i\lambda_i$. Expand the left-hand side by the Leibniz formula; each term takes one entry from every row and every column. If a term leaves out one of the diagonal entries $\lambda - a_{ii}$, it leaves out at least two of them, and its degree in λ is at most n − 2. So $\lambda^{n-1}$ comes only from the product of the diagonal entries $\prod_i(\lambda - a_{ii})$, with coefficient $-\sum_i a_{ii} = -\mathrm{tr}\,A$.　∎

By the fundamental theorem of algebra, a polynomial of degree n has exactly n roots over the complex numbers, so an n×n matrix always has n eigenvalues (counted with multiplicity), though some of them may be complex.

### 2.3.4 Principal directions of stiffness

**Example 2.3.1** Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the measured stiffness matrix K, and compare them with the datasheet (2,000 N/m and 500 N/m, with the principal direction at 30° to $x_s$).

The trace is tr K = {{ex2_3.tr:.0f}} N/m and the determinant det K = {{ex2_3.det}} N²/m². By Eq. (2.3.4),

$$
\lambda_{1,2} = 1250 \pm \sqrt{1250^2 - {{ex2_3.det}}} = 1250 \pm \sqrt{ {{ex2_3.disc}} } = 1250 \pm {{ex2_3.sq:.2f}} ,
$$

that is, $\lambda_1 = {{ex2_3.lam1:.1f}}$ N/m and $\lambda_2 = {{ex2_3.lam2:.1f}}$ N/m. For $\lambda_1$, the first row of $(K - \lambda_1 I)v = 0$ is $(1600 - \lambda_1)v_1 + 650 v_2 = 0$; taking $v = (650,\ \lambda_1 - 1600)^{\mathsf T}$ and normalizing gives

$$
v_1 = {{ex2_3.v1}} , \qquad v_2 = {{ex2_3.v2}} .
$$

The angle between $v_1$ and the $x_s$ axis is {{ex2_3.phi1:.2f}}°, and $v_2$ is perpendicular to it. The angle can also be found directly from the formula

$$
\tan 2\varphi = \frac{2k_{12}}{k_{11} - k_{22}} = \frac{1300}{700} , \qquad 2\varphi = {{ex2_3.ang2phi:.2f}}^\circ , \tag{2.3.6}
$$

which comes from requiring the off-diagonal entries of $R(\varphi)^{\mathsf T} K R(\varphi)$ to be zero (Exercise 2.3.3). Compared with the datasheet, the stiffness in the stiff direction is {{ex2_3.dlam2:.1f}} N/m lower, the stiffness in the soft direction is higher by the same amount, and the principal direction is off by {{ex2_3.dphi:.2f}}°. The sum of the two eigenvalues equals the trace, the same as 2,000 + 500 in the datasheet, because the trace of the measured matrix also happens to be 2,500 N/m. Program 2.3.1 computes the result both with the formula and with a numerical library, and the two agree.

In the frame made up of $v_1$ and $v_2$, K becomes the diagonal matrix diag(λ₁, λ₂): push along $v_1$ and the force is only along $v_1$; push along $v_2$ and the force is only along $v_2$. In assembly, if we do not want a part that is pushed off course to be carried away at a slant, we should line up the direction of the hole's offset with one of the eigendirections, or choose a wrist whose two eigenvalues are equal, so that it is equally compliant in every direction.

### 2.3.5 Diagonalization

The method of the example can be generalized. If an n×n matrix A has n eigenvectors $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ and they form a basis of $\mathbb R^n$, let P be the matrix with these vectors as its columns. Then $AP = (\lambda_1 v_1, \ldots, \lambda_n v_n) = P\Lambda$, with Λ = diag(λ₁, …, λₙ). P is invertible, so

$$
A = P\Lambda P^{-1} , \qquad \Lambda = P^{-1} A P . \tag{2.3.7}
$$

**Theorem 2.3.2 (Diagonalization)** An n×n matrix A is similar to a diagonal matrix if and only if it has n eigenvectors that form a basis. The diagonal entries of the diagonal matrix are then the eigenvalues of A. If the n eigenvalues of A are all different, this condition is certainly satisfied.

*Proof*　The first part is Eq. (2.3.7) and its converse: if $P^{-1}AP = \Lambda$ is diagonal, then AP = PΛ, and the columns of P are eigenvectors. For the second part, it is enough to prove that eigenvectors $v_1, \ldots, v_k$ belonging to distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k$ are linearly independent (Definition 2.6.3), because n linearly independent vectors in $\mathbb R^n$ form a basis (Section 2.6.2). Suppose they are linearly dependent, that is, there are $c_i$, not all zero, with $\sum_i c_iv_i = 0$. Taking the largest index j with $c_j \neq 0$, we can write $v_j$ as a linear combination of the vectors before it (for j = 1 this would give $v_1 = 0$, which is impossible). Among the vectors that can be written in this way take the one with the smallest index, call it $v_k$, with $v_k = \sum_{i<k} c_i v_i$. Multiplying both sides by A gives $\lambda_k v_k = \sum_{i<k} c_i \lambda_i v_i$; multiplying both sides by $\lambda_k$ gives $\lambda_k v_k = \sum_{i<k} c_i\lambda_k v_i$. Subtracting, $\sum_{i<k} c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_k)v_i = 0$. Since k is the smallest, none of $v_1, \ldots, v_{k-1}$ can be expressed by the vectors before it, so every coefficient $c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda_k)$ is zero (linear independence, Section 2.6); and since $\lambda_i \neq \lambda_k$, every $c_i = 0$, so $v_k = 0$, a contradiction.　∎

Geometrically, to diagonalize is to find a frame in which the matrix only stretches along each of the coordinate axes. Once a matrix has been diagonalized, many computations become simple. For example, $A^k = P\Lambda^k P^{-1}$, and $\Lambda^k$ only needs each diagonal entry raised to the power.

### 2.3.6 Symmetric matrices: the spectral theorem

The stiffness matrix is symmetric: the force in y produced by a unit push along x equals the force in x produced by a unit push along y. This follows from Betti's reciprocal theorem in elasticity; the inertia tensor and the covariance matrix are symmetric too. The eigenvalue problem for symmetric matrices has particularly good properties.

**Theorem 2.3.3** The eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix are all real; eigenvectors belonging to different eigenvalues are orthogonal to each other.

*Proof*　Let $Av = \lambda v$ with v ≠ 0, allowing λ and v to be complex, and write $\bar v$ for the conjugate of v. Then $\bar v^{\mathsf T}Av = \lambda\,\bar v^{\mathsf T}v = \lambda\sum_i|v_i|^2$. The left-hand side is a number; its conjugate is $v^{\mathsf T}A\bar v$ (A is real), and since a number is its own transpose, $v^{\mathsf T}A\bar v = (v^{\mathsf T}A\bar v)^{\mathsf T} = \bar v^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}v = \bar v^{\mathsf T}Av$ (A is symmetric). So the left-hand side is real, and since $\sum_i|v_i|^2 > 0$, λ is real.

Now let $Av_1 = \lambda_1 v_1$ and $Av_2 = \lambda_2 v_2$ with $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$. Then $\lambda_1(v_1\cdot v_2) = (Av_1)^{\mathsf T}v_2 = v_1^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}v_2 = v_1^{\mathsf T}Av_2 = \lambda_2(v_1\cdot v_2)$, so $(\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)(v_1\cdot v_2) = 0$ and $v_1\cdot v_2 = 0$.　∎

**Theorem 2.3.4 (Spectral theorem)** Every n×n real symmetric matrix A can be written as

$$
A = Q\Lambda Q^{\mathsf T} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i\, q_i q_i^{\mathsf T} , \tag{2.3.8}
$$

where the columns $q_1, \ldots, q_n$ of Q are unit eigenvectors of A, mutually orthogonal, that is, $Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I$; and Λ = diag(λ₁, …, λₙ) is made up of the corresponding real eigenvalues. Such a Q exists even when there are repeated eigenvalues.

*Proof*　By induction on n. The case n = 1 is obvious. Suppose the statement holds for order n − 1. By Theorem 2.3.3, A has a real eigenvalue $\lambda_1$, and a corresponding real unit eigenvector $q_1$ can be chosen. By Corollary 2.7.1 (which is proved with the Gram–Schmidt process and does not depend on the results of this section), extend $q_1$ to a set of mutually orthogonal unit vectors $q_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n$ of $\mathbb R^n$, forming a matrix $Q_1$ with $Q_1^{\mathsf T}Q_1 = I$. Then $B = Q_1^{\mathsf T}AQ_1$ is also symmetric, and its first column is $Q_1^{\mathsf T}Aq_1 = \lambda_1 Q_1^{\mathsf T}q_1 = \lambda_1 e_1$; by symmetry its first row is $\lambda_1 e_1^{\mathsf T}$ as well. So $B = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & B' \end{pmatrix}$, where B′ is a symmetric matrix of order n − 1. By the induction hypothesis, $B' = Q'\Lambda'Q'^{\mathsf T}$. Let $Q = Q_1\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & Q' \end{pmatrix}$; one can check that $Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I$ and $A = Q\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \Lambda')\,Q^{\mathsf T}$.　∎

The name "spectral" comes from physics: the lines of an atomic spectrum correspond to the eigenvalues of a certain operator. The second form of Eq. (2.3.8) splits A into a sum of n simple matrices. $q_iq_i^{\mathsf T}$ is the projection onto the direction of $q_i$ (Section 2.7), and what A does is to resolve a vector along the principal directions, multiply each part by its $\lambda_i$, and put the parts back together. For Example 2.3.1, Program 2.3.1 checks that $K = \lambda_1 v_1v_1^{\mathsf T} + \lambda_2 v_2v_2^{\mathsf T}$. Chapter 3 applies the spectral theorem to the inertia tensor, whose eigenvectors are the principal axes of the rigid body.

### 2.3.7 Positive definite matrices

When the wrist is pushed aside by δ, the energy stored in the spring is

$$
E = \tfrac12\,\delta^{\mathsf T} K \delta . \tag{2.3.9}
$$

For a real spring, the stored energy should be positive whichever way it is pushed. An expression of the form $x^{\mathsf T}Ax$ is called a **quadratic form**.

**Definition 2.3.2 (Positive definite matrix)** A symmetric matrix A is called a **positive definite matrix** if $x^{\mathsf T}Ax > 0$ for all x ≠ 0; if it only satisfies $x^{\mathsf T}Ax \ge 0$, it is called a **positive semidefinite matrix**.

**Theorem 2.3.5** A symmetric matrix A is positive definite if and only if all its eigenvalues are positive, and positive semidefinite if and only if all its eigenvalues are non-negative.

*Proof*　By Eq. (2.3.8), $x^{\mathsf T}Ax = \sum_i\lambda_i(q_i^{\mathsf T}x)^2$. If every $\lambda_i > 0$, the sum is positive, because when x ≠ 0 the $q_i^{\mathsf T}x$ are not all zero (Q is invertible). Conversely, taking $x = q_i$ gives $x^{\mathsf T}Ax = \lambda_i > 0$. The semidefinite case is the same.　∎

Both eigenvalues of the measured stiffness matrix are positive, so it is positive definite: pushing 1 mm along $x_s$ stores the energy $E = \tfrac12 \times 1600 \times 0.001^2 = {{ex2_3.energy_mJ:.1f}}$ mJ. If a measured stiffness matrix has a negative eigenvalue, either the measurement or the computation is wrong, or the structure has lost its stability. Chapter 26 shows that the mass matrix of a robot arm is always positive definite too, and its quadratic form is the kinetic energy.

### 2.3.8 Non-symmetric matrices: complex eigenvalues and too few eigenvectors

Non-symmetric matrices do not have such good properties. Three cases are worth knowing.

**Example 2.3.2** (a) The plane rotation R(30°) leaves no direction unchanged, so it has no real eigenvector. By Eq. (2.3.4), with tr R = 2cos 30° and det R = 1,

$$
\lambda_{1,2} = \cos 30^\circ \pm \sqrt{\cos^2 30^\circ - 1} = \cos 30^\circ \pm i\sin 30^\circ ,
$$

a pair of complex conjugates whose arguments ±30° are exactly the angle of rotation. Section 4.3 shows that the three eigenvalues of a rotation in space are 1 and $\cos\theta \pm i\sin\theta$, and that the eigenvector belonging to the eigenvalue 1 is the axis of rotation. This is exactly an algebraic proof of Euler's rotation theorem.

(b) The shear $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has the characteristic polynomial $(\lambda - 1)^2$, so the eigenvalue 1 is a double root, but $(A - I)v = 0$ has only one direction of solutions, the multiples of $v = (1, 0)^{\mathsf T}$. There are not enough eigenvectors to form a basis, so the shear cannot be diagonalized. Such a matrix is called defective.

(c) Stretching a rubber sheet gives the transformation $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & 0.5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Its eigenvalues are 1.5 and 1, with eigendirections at 0° and 135° (Figure 2.3.1, right). A can be diagonalized, but the two eigendirections are not perpendicular, and P is not an orthogonal matrix.

### 2.3.9 How to compute: power iteration

The formula for the roots works only for very small matrices: polynomial equations of degree five and higher have no general formula for their roots in radicals (Niels Henrik Abel, 1824), and the characteristic polynomial of an n×n matrix has degree n. In practice eigenvalues are computed by iterative methods. The simplest is **power iteration**: take a starting vector $x_0$ and compute $x_{k+1} = Ax_k/\|Ax_k\|$ again and again.

Suppose A can be diagonalized, $x_0 = c_1v_1 + \cdots + c_nv_n$, $|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2| \ge \cdots$, and $c_1 \ne 0$. Then

$$
A^k x_0 = c_1\lambda_1^k\Big(v_1 + \sum_{i\ge2}\frac{c_i}{c_1}\Big(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\Big)^k v_i\Big) . \tag{2.3.10}
$$

The other terms in the brackets tend to zero as fast as $(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^k$, so the direction of $x_k$ tends to $v_1$.

**Example 2.3.3** Run power iteration on the measured stiffness matrix, starting from $x_0 = (1, 0)^{\mathsf T}$ and estimating the eigenvalue at each step by $x_k^{\mathsf T}Kx_k$. With $\lambda_2/\lambda_1 = {{ex2_3.ratio:.4f}}$, the convergence is fast: the relative error at step 5 is ${{ex2_3.err5:sci1}}$, and it reaches $10^{-10}$ at step {{ex2_3.n_iter}}. The closer the two eigenvalues are, the slower the convergence.

Power iteration was put forward by Richard von Mises and Hilda Pollaczek-Geiringer in 1929. The standard method in numerical libraries for finding all the eigenvalues is the QR algorithm, proposed independently by John G. F. Francis and Vera N. Kublanovskaya around 1961; in spirit it is "power iteration on many vectors at once, keeping them orthogonal all the time by the method of Section 2.7". For symmetric matrices a dedicated function (such as `numpy.linalg.eigh`) should be used; it guarantees real results and orthogonal eigenvectors, and it is faster and more accurate.

### 2.3.10 An everyday example

Draw a circle and a few diameters through its centre on a square rubber sheet, then take hold of two sides and stretch it, shifting the sides slightly past each other at the same time. The circle becomes an ellipse, and most of the diameters are turned through some angle by the stretching; but there are always two diameters that still lie on their original lines after the stretching, only longer or shorter. They are the two eigendirections of this deformation. If the sheet is simply stretched along two mutually perpendicular directions (the symmetric case), these two diameters are perpendicular to each other; if the stretching also involves a shift (the non-symmetric case), they are no longer perpendicular. In mechanics the two directions in the symmetric case are called the principal strain directions, and the stress tensor of Chapter 3 has principal directions in the same way.

The symmetric matrix K of this section turns the unit circle into an ellipse whose semi-axes lie along the eigendirections. The next section shows that every matrix, including non-symmetric ones and even ones that are not square, turns the unit circle into an ellipse, and that what describes this ellipse is the singular values.

**Engineer's notes**

- For symmetric matrices (stiffness, inertia, covariance), always compute eigenvalues with an `eigh`-type function; the general `eig` may give "complex" results with tiny imaginary parts and eigenvectors that are not orthogonal.
- The sign and length of an eigenvector are not unique. When comparing results from two computations or two pieces of software, first normalize them in the same way, then fix the sign in the same way (for example, make the first non-zero component positive).
- When two eigenvalues are very close, the eigendirections are extremely sensitive to measurement error: for an isotropic wrist, the "principal direction" can be almost any direction. When reporting a principal direction, report the difference between the two eigenvalues as well.
- To check whether a measured stiffness or inertia matrix is reasonable, first see whether it is symmetric (the non-symmetric part is usually measurement error; take $(K + K^{\mathsf T})/2$), then see whether its eigenvalues are all positive.
