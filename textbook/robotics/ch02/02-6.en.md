### 2.6.1 The problem: the end-effector stays still, but can the arm still move?

Hold a coin down on the table with your hand. The palm does not move, yet the elbow can still swing up and down. The human arm has this freedom because the shoulder, elbow and wrist provide more joints than the task "hold one point down" needs. A robot arm is the same. The planar 3R arm of Chapter 12 has three revolute joints, $L_1 = 0.425$ m, $L_2 = 0.392$ m, $L_3 = 0.1$ m; if the task only asks the end-effector to reach a given position in the plane (two coordinates), without caring which way the end-effector points, then there is one joint too many. This situation, in which there are more joints than the task needs, is called **redundancy**.

Redundancy raises two questions. First, which joint motions do not change the position of the end-effector? These motions make up the arm's "room to manoeuvre", and they can be used to avoid obstacles, to keep away from joint limits, or to keep the arm in a dexterous configuration. Second, in which directions can the end-effector move? Section 2.1 showed that when the arm is straight the end-effector cannot move along the arm; under what circumstances does a redundant arm also lose a direction?

In the notation of Section 2.5, the end-effector velocity is $v = J\dot\theta$, where J is a 2×3 matrix. The first question asks for all solutions of the equation $J\dot\theta = 0$; the second asks for all the values that $J\dot\theta$ can take. The former is called the null space of J, the latter the range of J. To say how "large" they are, we first need the concepts of subspace, linear independence, basis and dimension.

### 2.6.2 Subspaces and span

In three-dimensional space, a line or a plane through the origin has this property: the sum of any two vectors in it is still in it, and any multiple of a vector in it is still in it. Sets of this kind are the basic objects that linear algebra studies.

**Definition 2.6.1 (Subspace)** A non-empty subset S of $\mathbb R^n$ that is closed under addition and scalar multiplication, that is, x + y ∈ S whenever x, y ∈ S, and cx ∈ S whenever x ∈ S, is called a **subspace** of $\mathbb R^n$.

A subspace always contains the zero vector (take c = 0). In $\mathbb R^3$ there are only four kinds of subspace: {0}, containing only the zero vector; lines through the origin; planes through the origin; and $\mathbb R^3$ itself. A line that does not pass through the origin is not a subspace.

**Definition 2.6.2 (Span)** The set of all linear combinations of the vectors $v_1, \ldots, v_k$,

$$
\mathrm{span}\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\} = \{c_1v_1 + \cdots + c_kv_k : c_1, \ldots, c_k \in \mathbb R\} \tag{2.6.1}
$$

is called the subspace they **span**.

For example, two vectors that are not collinear span a plane; but if a third vector lies in this plane, adding it still spans the same plane, and it is "superfluous". The next concept makes "no superfluous vectors" precise.

**Definition 2.6.3 (Linear independence)** If

$$
c_1v_1 + c_2v_2 + \cdots + c_kv_k = 0 , \tag{2.6.2}
$$

holds only when $c_1 = \cdots = c_k = 0$, the vectors $v_1, \ldots, v_k$ are said to be **linearly independent**; otherwise they are said to be **linearly dependent**.

Linear dependence is equivalent to at least one of the vectors being a linear combination of the others: if $c_j \neq 0$, we can solve Eq. (2.6.2) for $v_j$. If the vectors are arranged as a matrix $V = (v_1, \ldots, v_k)$, Eq. (2.6.2) is just Vc = 0; linear independence means that this equation has only the zero solution.

**Definition 2.6.4 (Basis and dimension)** A set of vectors in a subspace S that is linearly independent and spans S is called a **basis** of S; the number of vectors in a basis is called the **dimension** of S, written dim S.

For this definition to make sense, we must be sure that all bases of the same subspace contain the same number of vectors.

**Theorem 2.6.1** If $w_1, \ldots, w_m$ all lie in $\mathrm{span}\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\}$ and are linearly independent, then m ≤ k. Consequently any two bases of the same subspace contain the same number of vectors.

*Proof*　Write each $w_j$ as $w_j = \sum_i a_{ij}v_i$, giving a k×m matrix $A = (a_{ij})$. If m > k, the homogeneous system Ac = 0 has fewer equations (k) than unknowns (m); elimination can always bring it to echelon form with at least one unknown free to take any value, so there is a non-zero solution c. Then $\sum_j c_jw_j = \sum_i(\sum_j a_{ij}c_j)v_i = 0$, contradicting the linear independence of the $w_j$. If S has two bases, with k and m vectors respectively, applying this result each way round gives m ≤ k and k ≤ m.　∎

The dimension of $\mathbb R^n$ is n, and $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ is a basis. It also follows that any n linearly independent vectors $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ in $\mathbb R^n$ form a basis. First a simple fact: if w is not in $\mathrm{span}\{v_1, \ldots, v_n\}$, then $v_1, \ldots, v_n, w$ are still linearly independent, because if $c_0 \neq 0$ in $c_0w + \sum_i c_iv_i = 0$, w could be expressed in terms of the $v_i$; so $c_0 = 0$, and then every $c_i = 0$. Hence, if $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ did not span $\mathbb R^n$, adding a vector not in their span would give n + 1 linearly independent vectors in $\mathbb R^n = \mathrm{span}\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$, contradicting Theorem 2.6.1. The definition of a basis in Section 2.2 (every vector can be written uniquely as a linear combination of them) agrees with the one here: "can be written" is spanning, and "uniquely" is linear independence.

### 2.6.3 Null space and range

**Definition 2.6.5 (Null space and range)** For an m×n matrix A,

$$
N(A) = \{x \in \mathbb R^n : Ax = 0\} , \qquad R(A) = \{Ax : x \in \mathbb R^n\} \tag{2.6.3}
$$

are called the **null space** and the **range** of A respectively. The range is the subspace spanned by the columns of A, so it is also called the column space. The dimension of the range is called the **rank** of A, written rank A.

The null space is a subspace of $\mathbb R^n$ (the input space): if Ax = 0 and Ay = 0, then A(x + y) = 0 and A(cx) = 0. The range is a subspace of $\mathbb R^m$ (the output space), because the sum of linear combinations of the columns is still a linear combination of the columns. The dimension of the null space is called the **nullity**, a name introduced by Sylvester in 1884.

For a robot arm, these two subspaces have a very concrete meaning:

- $N(J)$ is the set of all joint velocities that give zero end-effector velocity, that is, the directions of motion in which "the end-effector stays still while the arm moves". This motion of a redundant arm is called **self-motion**;
- $R(J)$ is the set of all end-effector velocities that can be reached in the current configuration. If rank J is less than the dimension of the end-effector velocity, the end-effector cannot move in some directions; this is a singular configuration.

**Example 2.6.1** For the planar 3R arm at θ = (30°, 60°, −60°), the method of Section 2.1 (column i is "the vector from joint i to the end-effector" turned 90° anticlockwise) gives

$$
J = {{ex2_6.J}} .
$$

Its two rows are linearly independent, so rank J = {{ex2_6.rank}}: $R(J)$ is the whole plane, and the end-effector can move in any direction. The null space satisfies two equations in three unknowns; by the rank–nullity theorem below it is one-dimensional, a line through the origin. To find its direction vector n we can solve Jn = 0 directly, or use the cross product of Chapter 3: a vector perpendicular to both rows of J is the cross product of the two rows. After normalization,

$$
n = {{ex2_6.n}} .
$$

When the three joints turn in the proportions of n, the end-effector is momentarily still: joint 3 turns the most, and joints 1 and 2 turn by equal amounts in opposite directions. Program 2.6.1 computes it again with the singular value decomposition and gets the same direction.

To keep the end-effector still over a period of time, we cannot keep turning along the same n, because J changes with the configuration and so does n. Program 2.6.1 constructs such a motion directly in another way: let $\theta_1$ go from 27° to 55°, and for each $\theta_1$ find $\theta_2$ and $\theta_3$ by geometry (the inverse kinematics of a 2R arm, Chapter 14) so that the end-effector always stays at ${{ex2_6.tip}}$ m. Figure 2.6.1 draws several of these configurations: ({{ex2_6.q27}}) at $\theta_1 = 27°$ and ({{ex2_6.q55}}) at $\theta_1 = 55°$. The program also verifies that the tangent of this "configuration curve" at θ = (30°, 60°, −60°) is exactly the null-space direction n. The null space gives the instantaneous direction of the self-motion.

### 2.6.4 The rank–nullity theorem

The larger the null space, the smaller the range. The following theorem makes this intuition precise.

**Theorem 2.6.2 (Rank–nullity theorem)** For an m×n matrix A,

$$
\mathrm{rank}\,A + \dim N(A) = n . \tag{2.6.4}
$$

*Proof*　Let $z_1, \ldots, z_k$ be a basis of N(A). Extend it to a basis $z_1, \ldots, z_k, y_1, \ldots, y_r$ of $\mathbb R^n$, with k + r = n (the extension can always be done: as long as $\mathbb R^n$ has not yet been spanned, take from $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ a vector not in the subspace already spanned and add it; linear independence is preserved). We show that $Ay_1, \ldots, Ay_r$ is a basis of R(A), so that rank A = r. Spanning: any x can be written as $\sum a_iz_i + \sum b_jy_j$, and $Ax = \sum b_jAy_j$ because $Az_i = 0$. Linear independence: if $\sum c_jAy_j = 0$, then $\sum c_jy_j \in N(A)$ and can be written as $\sum d_iz_i$, so $\sum c_jy_j - \sum d_iz_i = 0$; the whole basis is linearly independent, so every $c_j = 0$.　∎

The meaning of the theorem is that A splits the n-dimensional input space into two parts: one part (the null space) is squashed to zero, and the rest is carried one-to-one onto the range, with the dimension conserved. For Example 2.6.1, 3 = 2 + 1: of the three joint degrees of freedom, two are used to move the end-effector and one is redundant.

**Example 2.6.2** The arm is straight, θ = (30°, 0°, 0°). Now all three joints lie on one straight line, and the three columns of J are all perpendicular to the arm and parallel to one another:

$$
J_0 = {{ex2_6.J0}} .
$$

rank $J_0$ = {{ex2_6.rank0}}, so by Theorem 2.6.2 the null space becomes two-dimensional. A basis can be taken as ${{ex2_6.n_a}}$ and ${{ex2_6.n_b}}$ (the components are taken from the distances of the joints to the end-effector, in m, and only express ratios): the first moves only joints 2 and 3, the second only joints 1 and 2, and both keep the end-effector momentarily still (Exercise 2.6.3). The range is reduced to a single line: the end-effector can only move perpendicular to the arm, and a velocity along the arm, ${{ex2_6.left0}}$ (that is, in the 30° direction), cannot be obtained in any way. Redundancy does not remove singularities; it only lets the arm avoid them in more configurations.

### 2.6.5 The four fundamental subspaces

An m×n matrix A brings two more subspaces with it: the range of $A^{\mathsf T}$, $R(A^{\mathsf T})$, which is the subspace spanned by the rows of A and is called the **row space**; and the null space of $A^{\mathsf T}$, $N(A^{\mathsf T})$, called the **left null space** (because $A^{\mathsf T}y = 0$ is the same as $y^{\mathsf T}A = 0$). Strang called these four subspaces and the relations between them "the fundamental theorem of linear algebra".

**Theorem 2.6.3** Let A be an m×n matrix of rank r, with singular value decomposition $A = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}$. Then

1. $R(A^{\mathsf T})$ is spanned by $v_1, \ldots, v_r$ and $N(A)$ by $v_{r+1}, \ldots, v_n$; $R(A)$ is spanned by $u_1, \ldots, u_r$ and $N(A^{\mathsf T})$ by $u_{r+1}, \ldots, u_m$;
2. the dimension of the row space equals the dimension of the column space; both are r ("row rank equals column rank");
3. in $\mathbb R^n$ the null space is orthogonal to the row space, and in $\mathbb R^m$ the range is orthogonal to the left null space, that is,

$$
N(A) \perp R(A^{\mathsf T}) , \qquad R(A) \perp N(A^{\mathsf T}) . \tag{2.6.5}
$$

*Proof*　By Eq. (2.4.5), $A = \sum_{i\le r}\sigma_iu_iv_i^{\mathsf T}$ and $A^{\mathsf T} = \sum_{i\le r}\sigma_iv_iu_i^{\mathsf T}$. So Ax is always a linear combination of $u_1, \ldots, u_r$, and taking $x = v_i/\sigma_i$ gives each $u_i$; hence R(A) is spanned by them. In the same way $R(A^{\mathsf T})$ is spanned by $v_1, \ldots, v_r$. Also $Av_j = 0$ (j > r), so these n − r mutually orthogonal vectors all lie in N(A); by Theorem 2.6.2 the dimension of N(A) is exactly n − r, so they form a basis. The same holds for $N(A^{\mathsf T})$. (2) and (3) follow directly from the columns of U and V being mutually orthogonal.　∎

Eq. (2.6.5) can also be seen directly without the singular value decomposition: Ax = 0 says precisely that the dot product of x with every row of A is zero. Figure 2.6.2 draws the four subspaces together. A carries the row space one-to-one onto the range and squashes the null space to zero; any x can be split into a part in the row space and a part in the null space, and A "sees" only the former.

From this we immediately get the condition for a linear system to have a solution, and the structure of its solutions.

**Theorem 2.6.4** The linear system Ax = b has a solution if and only if b ∈ R(A), and also if and only if b is orthogonal to every vector in the left null space. If $x_p$ is one solution, then all the solutions are

$$
x = x_p + z , \qquad z \in N(A) . \tag{2.6.6}
$$

*Proof*　The first statement is the definition of the range. The second: by Theorem 2.6.3, $u_1, \ldots, u_m$ is an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb R^m$, R(A) is spanned by the first r of them and the left null space by the last m − r. Write b as $b = \sum_i c_iu_i$ and take the dot product with $u_j$; since the vectors are mutually orthogonal and of length 1, $c_j = u_j\cdot b$. b is orthogonal to the left null space if and only if the coefficients $c_i = u_i\cdot b$ with i > r are all zero, that is, b is a linear combination of $u_1, \ldots, u_r$, b ∈ R(A). If x and $x_p$ are both solutions, then $A(x - x_p) = 0$.　∎

For the straight arm of Example 2.6.2, the left null space is spanned by ${{ex2_6.left0}}$; the required end-effector velocity v must be perpendicular to it, that is, it must have no component along the arm, for $J_0\dot\theta = v$ to have a solution. Eq. (2.6.6) says that the joint velocities of a redundant arm are always "a particular solution plus an arbitrary self-motion"; the minimum-norm solution of Section 2.5 is the one among them that is orthogonal to the null space.

### 2.6.6 Null-space projection: adjusting the arm without disturbing the end-effector

A redundant arm can carry out its end-effector task and at the same time use self-motion to do something else. Suppose we would like the joints to move with some velocity z (for example, to move joint 3 away from its limit), but without affecting the end-effector. The way to do it is to keep only the part of z that lies in the null space.

**Theorem 2.6.5 (Null-space projection)** Let the rows of the m×n matrix A be linearly independent, and let $A^+$ be its pseudoinverse. Then

$$
P_N = I - A^+A \tag{2.6.7}
$$

satisfies $AP_N = 0$, $P_N^2 = P_N$ and $P_N^{\mathsf T} = P_N$, and it projects any vector orthogonally onto N(A). Consequently, for any z,

$$
\dot\theta = J^+v + (I - J^+J)\,z \tag{2.6.8}
$$

satisfies $J\dot\theta = v$.

*Proof*　By the Penrose conditions (2.5.9), $AA^+A = A$, so $AP_N = A - AA^+A = 0$; $(A^+A)^2 = A^+(AA^+A) = A^+A$, so $P_N^2 = I - 2A^+A + A^+A = P_N$; $A^+A$ is symmetric, so $P_N$ is symmetric. The range of $P_N$ lies in N(A). For any x, the difference $x - P_Nx = A^+Ax = A^{\mathsf T}(AA^{\mathsf T})^{-1}Ax$ lies in the row space $R(A^{\mathsf T})$, which by Eq. (2.6.5) is orthogonal to N(A); so $P_Nx$ is exactly the orthogonal projection of x onto N(A).　∎

**Example 2.6.3** For the configuration of Example 2.6.1,

$$
P_N = I - J^+J = {{ex2_6.P}} .
$$

One can verify that $P_N = nn^{\mathsf T}$: the null space is one-dimensional, and projecting onto it means taking the component along n. If the end-effector is to move at 0.1 m/s in the x direction, the minimum-norm solution is $J^+v = {{ex2_6.thd0}}$ rad/s. Joint 3 is now at −60°; suppose we would like it to turn towards 0°, and take z = (0, 0, 1) rad/s. Then $P_Nz = {{ex2_6.Pz}}$, and so

$$
\dot\theta = J^+v + P_Nz = {{ex2_6.thd}}\ \mathrm{rad/s} .
$$

Joint 3 turns much faster, joints 1 and 2 compensate accordingly, and the end-effector velocity is still exactly (0.1, 0) m/s. Eq. (2.6.8) was proposed by A. Liégeois in 1977 and is still the most widely used form in the control of redundant robots; Chapter 17 discusses it, and its various "secondary tasks", systematically.

### 2.6.7 An everyday example

Lay your palm flat on the table and, keeping the palm still, try raising and lowering your elbow. The shoulder, elbow and wrist all change together, yet the palm does not move at all: this is self-motion, and the path the elbow can follow is the "configuration curve". Now try straightening the arm fully to press on a distant point: you will find that the elbow has almost no room left, and that it is hard to push the hand any farther: this is a singular configuration, and the range has lost a dimension.

Mathematically, the human arm from shoulder to wrist has seven degrees of freedom (3 at the shoulder, 1 at the elbow, 1 for forearm rotation, 2 at the wrist), whereas placing the hand at a given position with a given orientation in space needs only six; so the human arm is redundant by nature. Seven-axis collaborative robots such as the Franka Panda imitate exactly this, and Chapter 17 will use it as the example of a redundant robot.

The next section returns to a seemingly simple question: given a few directions, how do we tidy them into a set of mutually perpendicular unit vectors? Its answer, the Gram–Schmidt process, has already been cited several times in this chapter.

**Engineer's notes**

- When judging rank numerically, do not ask whether the matrix "equals zero"; ask whether the singular values fall below a threshold (for example $10^{-10}$ times the largest singular value). The `matrix_rank` of numerical libraries does exactly this; the threshold should match the precision of the data.
- The null-space motion of a redundant arm must be recomputed with Eq. (2.6.8) in every control cycle; the n of one instant cannot be used indefinitely, or the end-effector will slowly drift.
- Near a singular configuration both $J^+$ and $P_N$ change violently (the dimension of the null space may suddenly increase). In engineering practice, damped least squares (Chapter 15) is commonly used to smooth out such jumps.
- An arm whose number of joints exactly equals the dimension of the task (such as a six-axis arm doing a six-dimensional task) has no null-space motion; avoiding obstacles and joint limits can then only be done by path planning, which is one of the reasons seven-axis arms are popular.
