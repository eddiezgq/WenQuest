### 2.4.1 The problem: in which direction does the end-effector move fastest?

Go back to the planar 2R arm of Section 2.1, with θ = (30°, 60°). Suppose the two joint motors are equally capable, and give the joint speeds a "budget": $\dot\theta_1^2 + \dot\theta_2^2 = 1$ (rad/s)², so that the joint velocity vector $\dot\theta$ lies on the unit circle. Within this budget, in which direction can the end-effector move fastest? In which direction slowest? And how fast are the fastest and the slowest?

This question bears directly on how the arm is used. If the end-effector moves slowly in some direction, the joints must turn very fast to move it that way; conversely, the same joint torques can produce a large end-effector force along that direction, just as an outstretched arm most easily holds up a weight along the line of the arm (Chapter 16 proves from $\tau = J^{\mathsf T}F$ that velocity and force are two sides of the same thing). Section 2.1 showed that when the arm is straight, the end-effector cannot move along the line of the arm at all; that is the extreme case of this question. Now we want a quantitative answer: for any configuration, what shape is the range of end-effector velocities that can be reached?

The answer is an ellipse. All the $\dot\theta$ on the unit circle, transformed by J, form an ellipse whose semi-major axis is the fastest direction and whose semi-minor axis is the slowest. This holds for every matrix; it need not be symmetric, or even square. The tool that expresses it is the singular value decomposition, one of the most useful decompositions in linear algebra.

### 2.4.2 Why the image of the unit circle is an ellipse

The squared length of the end-effector velocity is

$$
\|v\|^2 = (J\dot\theta)^{\mathsf T}(J\dot\theta) = \dot\theta^{\mathsf T}\,(J^{\mathsf T}J)\,\dot\theta . \tag{2.4.1}
$$

The matrix $J^{\mathsf T}J$ is symmetric (Exercise 2.1.2), and positive semidefinite, because $x^{\mathsf T}J^{\mathsf T}Jx = \|Jx\|^2 \ge 0$ for every x. By the spectral theorem (Theorem 2.3.4), it has mutually orthogonal unit eigenvectors $v_1, v_2$ and non-negative eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge 0$:

$$
J^{\mathsf T}J = V\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2)\,V^{\mathsf T}, \qquad V = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 & v_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i} . \tag{2.4.2}
$$

Write the unit vector $\dot\theta$ as $\dot\theta = c_1v_1 + c_2v_2$, with $c_1^2 + c_2^2 = 1$. By Eq. (2.4.1), $\|v\|^2 = \sigma_1^2c_1^2 + \sigma_2^2c_2^2$, which is largest, $\sigma_1^2$, when $\dot\theta = v_1$, and smallest, $\sigma_2^2$, when $\dot\theta = v_2$.

Now see where $v_1$ and $v_2$ are sent. Let

$$
u_i = \frac{J v_i}{\sigma_i} \qquad (\sigma_i > 0) . \tag{2.4.3}
$$

These are unit vectors, and they are orthogonal to each other: $u_1^{\mathsf T}u_2 = v_1^{\mathsf T}J^{\mathsf T}Jv_2/(\sigma_1\sigma_2) = \lambda_2 v_1^{\mathsf T}v_2/(\sigma_1\sigma_2) = 0$. Hence

$$
J\dot\theta = c_1 \sigma_1 u_1 + c_2 \sigma_2 u_2 .
$$

In coordinates with $u_1$ and $u_2$ as axes, the image has coordinates $(\sigma_1c_1, \sigma_2c_2)$, which satisfy $(\cdot/\sigma_1)^2 + (\cdot/\sigma_2)^2 = 1$, exactly the equation of an ellipse with semi-axes $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$. This proves: **the image of the unit circle is an ellipse whose semi-axes have lengths $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$ and directions $u_1$, $u_2$; they are the images of $v_1$, $v_2$ on the unit circle.**

### 2.4.3 The singular value decomposition theorem

Writing the derivation above in matrix form gives the general theorem. First we give a definition that is used again and again in this section and in Section 2.7.

**Definition 2.4.1 (Orthogonal matrix)** An n×n matrix Q with $Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I$ is called an **orthogonal matrix**; that is, its columns are mutually orthogonal unit vectors. Then $Q^{-1} = Q^{\mathsf T}$, and $\|Qx\| = \|x\|$ for all x.

The last statement follows from $\|Qx\|^2 = x^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}Qx = x^{\mathsf T}x$. An orthogonal matrix preserves lengths and angles; in the plane it is either a rotation (det Q = 1) or a reflection (det Q = −1). Section 2.7 discusses its properties systematically.

**Definition 2.4.2 (Singular value decomposition)** Writing an m×n matrix A in the form

$$
A = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T} \tag{2.4.4}
$$

where U is an m×m orthogonal matrix, V is an n×n orthogonal matrix, and Σ is an m×n matrix that has non-negative entries $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \cdots \ge 0$ only in the (i, i) positions and zeros elsewhere, is called the **singular value decomposition** of A. The $\sigma_i$ are called the **singular values** of A, and the columns of U and V are called the left and right singular vectors.

**Theorem 2.4.1 (Existence of the singular value decomposition)** Every m×n real matrix has a singular value decomposition. The singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^{\mathsf T}A$ and are uniquely determined by A.

*Proof*　$A^{\mathsf T}A$ is an n×n positive semidefinite symmetric matrix. By the spectral theorem, take mutually orthogonal unit eigenvectors $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ of it, with eigenvalues in decreasing order $\lambda_1 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_r > 0 = \lambda_{r+1} = \cdots = \lambda_n$, and let $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$ and $V = (v_1, \ldots, v_n)$. For i ≤ r, define $u_i$ by Eq. (2.4.3); the same computation as in Section 2.4.2 shows that $u_1, \ldots, u_r$ are mutually orthogonal unit vectors in $\mathbb R^m$. By Corollary 2.7.1, extend them to a set of mutually orthogonal unit vectors $u_1, \ldots, u_m$ of $\mathbb R^m$, forming U. For i > r, $\|Av_i\|^2 = v_i^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}Av_i = \lambda_i = 0$, so $Av_i = 0$. Hence $Av_i = \sigma_iu_i$ for every i (for i > r both sides are the zero vector), which together say $AV = U\Sigma$; multiplying both sides on the right by $V^{\mathsf T}$ gives Eq. (2.4.4). Finally, if $A = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}$, then $A^{\mathsf T}A = V\Sigma^{\mathsf T}\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}$, so each $\sigma_i^2$ must be an eigenvalue of $A^{\mathsf T}A$.　∎

The geometric meaning of the singular value decomposition is read from Eq. (2.4.4) from right to left (Figure 2.4.1):

1. $V^{\mathsf T}$ is a rotation (or reflection) that turns $v_1, v_2, \ldots$ onto the coordinate axes;
2. Σ stretches along each coordinate axis by the factors $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \ldots$ (when m ≠ n it also adds or removes some dimensions);
3. U makes another rotation (or reflection) that turns the coordinate axes onto $u_1, u_2, \ldots$.

**Every linear transformation is "a rotation, a stretch along mutually perpendicular directions, and another rotation".** The diagonalization of Section 2.3 requires the matrix to have enough eigenvectors, and the P it uses is in general not an orthogonal matrix; the singular value decomposition holds for every matrix and uses orthogonal matrices on both sides, at the price of using two different sets of directions, V and U, for input and output. The singular value decomposition of a symmetric positive definite matrix is its spectral decomposition, with U = V = Q.

Expanding Eq. (2.4.4) column by column gives another commonly used form:

$$
A = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^{\mathsf T} + \sigma_2 u_2 v_2^{\mathsf T} + \cdots + \sigma_r u_r v_r^{\mathsf T} . \tag{2.4.5}
$$

Each term $u_iv_i^{\mathsf T}$ is a matrix of rank 1: it projects the input vector onto $v_i$ and sends the result out along $u_i$. A is the sum of r such "single channels", and the gain of channel i is $\sigma_i$.

**Example 2.4.1** Following the proof of Theorem 2.4.1, find the singular value decomposition of the Jacobian matrix of the 2R arm at θ = (30°, 60°).

From Example 2.1.1,

$$
J = {{ex2_4.J}} , \qquad J^{\mathsf T}J = {{ex2_4.JtJ}} .
$$

The eigenvalues of $J^{\mathsf T}J$ are $\lambda_1 = {{ex2_4.lam1:.5f}}$ and $\lambda_2 = {{ex2_4.lam2:.5f}}$, so the singular values are $\sigma_1 = {{ex2_4.s1:.5f}}$ and $\sigma_2 = {{ex2_4.s2:.5f}}$ (in m/rad). The corresponding unit eigenvectors, and the $u_i$ computed from Eq. (2.4.3), are

$$
V = {{ex2_4.V}} , \qquad U = {{ex2_4.U}} .
$$

Program 2.4.1 verifies that $U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T} = J$ and checks the singular values against the `svd` function of a numerical library. What the result means: with joint velocities $v_1 = {{ex2_4.v1}}$ (both joints turning the same way, joint 1 somewhat faster), the end-effector moves fastest, at the speed $\sigma_1 = {{ex2_4.s1:.4f}}$ m/s, in the direction $u_1$ at {{ex2_4.u1_ang:.1f}}° to the x axis, roughly perpendicular to the line from the base to the end-effector; with joint velocities $v_2$ the end-effector moves slowest, at only {{ex2_4.s2:.4f}} m/s. The two differ by a factor of {{ex2_4.cond:.2f}}.

### 2.4.4 What the singular values tell us

**Theorem 2.4.2** Let the singular values of an m×n matrix A be $\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_p \ge 0$, with p = min(m, n). Then

1. for every unit vector x, $\|Ax\| \le \sigma_1$, with equality when $x = v_1$; if m ≥ n, also $\|Ax\| \ge \sigma_n$, with equality when $x = v_n$;
2. the rank of A (Section 2.6) equals the number r of non-zero singular values;
3. $\|A\|_F^2 = \sigma_1^2 + \cdots + \sigma_p^2$;
4. if A is square, $|\det A| = \sigma_1\sigma_2\cdots\sigma_n$.

*Proof*　(1) Let $y = V^{\mathsf T}x$; since V is orthogonal, $\|y\| = 1$. U is orthogonal too, so $\|Ax\| = \|U\Sigma y\| = \|\Sigma y\| = \sqrt{\sum_i \sigma_i^2y_i^2}$, which lies between $\sigma_n$ and $\sigma_1$ (when m ≥ n) and is largest when $y = e_1$, that is, $x = v_1$. (2) By Eq. (2.4.5), the range of A is spanned by $u_1, \ldots, u_r$, which are mutually orthogonal. (3) By Eq. (2.1.18) and Theorem 2.1.6, $\|A\|_F^2 = \mathrm{tr}(A^{\mathsf T}A) = \mathrm{tr}(V\Sigma^{\mathsf T}\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}) = \mathrm{tr}(\Sigma^{\mathsf T}\Sigma)$. (4) $|\det A| = |\det U|\,\det\Sigma\,|\det V^{\mathsf T}| = \prod_i\sigma_i$, because the determinant of an orthogonal matrix is ±1 (Exercise 2.1.4).　∎

The largest stretch $\sigma_1$ in (1) is called the 2-norm of A and is written $\|A\|_2$. By (1), the singular values condense what a matrix does into a few numbers: the most it can magnify a vector, and the least. Their ratio is the standard measure of how "ill-conditioned" a matrix is.

**Definition 2.4.3 (Condition number)** The **condition number** of an invertible square matrix A is

$$
\kappa(A) = \frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}} \ge 1 . \tag{2.4.6}
$$

What the condition number means is the magnification of errors. When solving a system Ax = b, if b has a relative error, the relative error of x can be magnified by up to κ(A) times; the proof is in the part of Solomon's book on the sensitivity analysis of linear systems. An orthogonal matrix has condition number 1 and is the "healthiest" matrix; a singular matrix has an infinite condition number. The condition number does not depend on the choice of the unit of length (as long as the input and the output each use one unit throughout), and this is why it is better than the determinant for judging whether a matrix is "nearly singular". For Example 2.4.1, κ(J) = {{ex2_4.cond:.3f}}.

### 2.4.5 Manipulability of a robot arm

In 1985 Tsuneo Yoshikawa proposed measuring the ability of a robot arm to move in a given configuration by the ellipse (in space, the ellipsoid) of end-effector velocities corresponding to unit joint velocities, called the manipulability ellipsoid; and he used the "size" of the ellipsoid, that is, the product of its semi-axes, as a numerical index.

**Definition 2.4.4 (Manipulability)** The **manipulability** of a robot arm in the configuration θ is

$$
w(\theta) = \sigma_1\sigma_2\cdots\sigma_m = \sqrt{\det\big(J(\theta)J(\theta)^{\mathsf T}\big)} , \tag{2.4.7}
$$

where the $\sigma_i$ are the singular values of J(θ) and m is the dimension of the end-effector velocity.

The second equality follows from $JJ^{\mathsf T} = U\Sigma\Sigma^{\mathsf T}U^{\mathsf T}$. For the planar 2R arm, J is square, so by Theorem 2.4.2 and Theorem 2.1.5,

$$
w = \sigma_1\sigma_2 = |\det J| = L_1L_2|\sin\theta_2| . \tag{2.4.8}
$$

This gives the det J of Section 2.1 a clear geometric meaning: it is the area of the manipulability ellipse divided by π.

**Example 2.4.2** (a) For Example 2.4.1, $w = \sigma_1\sigma_2 = {{ex2_4.w:.5f}}$ m², the same as $L_1L_2\sin 60^\circ$. By Eq. (2.4.8), w is largest when $\theta_2 = 90^\circ$, with $w_{\max} = L_1L_2 = {{ex2_4.wmax:.4f}}$ m². So for this arm, the upper arm perpendicular to the forearm is the "most dexterous" configuration.

(b) The arm nearly straight, θ = (30°, 5°). The singular values are $\sigma_1 = {{ex2_4.s1_5:.4f}}$ and $\sigma_2 = {{ex2_4.s2_5:.5f}}$, and the condition number is {{ex2_4.cond5:.1f}}. The slowest direction $u_2$ differs by only {{ex2_4.ang_radial:.2f}}° from the line from the base to the end-effector, that is, it is along the arm. To move the end-effector in this direction at 0.1 m/s, the joint velocities must reach a magnitude of $0.1/\sigma_2 = {{ex2_4.need:.2f}}$ rad/s, whereas along the fastest direction only {{ex2_4.need_fast:.3f}} rad/s is needed.

Figure 2.4.2 shows the manipulability ellipses in three configurations, and how $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$ and w vary with $\theta_2$. As the arm nears the straight configuration the ellipse degenerates into a line segment, and the same happens as it nears the folded one. Avoiding these configurations in path planning, or arranging at the design stage for the usual working positions to lie in regions where w is large, is the most direct use of manipulability; Chapter 16 extends it to six-axis robot arms, and Chapter 17 uses it as an objective to optimize for redundant robot arms.

### 2.4.6 Low-rank approximation

Eq. (2.4.5) writes A as a sum of rank-1 matrices, with the terms ordered by $\sigma_i$ from largest to smallest. If only the first k terms are kept,

$$
A_k = \sigma_1u_1v_1^{\mathsf T} + \cdots + \sigma_ku_kv_k^{\mathsf T} , \tag{2.4.9}
$$

how much is lost?

**Theorem 2.4.3 (Eckart–Young theorem)** Among all m×n matrices B of rank at most k, $A_k$ makes both $\|A - B\|_2$ and $\|A - B\|_F$ smallest, and the minimum values are $\sigma_{k+1}$ and $\sqrt{\sigma_{k+1}^2 + \cdots + \sigma_p^2}$ respectively.

*Outline of the proof (2-norm)*　$A - A_k = \sum_{i>k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^{\mathsf T}$, and by Theorem 2.4.2 its 2-norm is $\sigma_{k+1}$. On the other hand, let B have rank at most k; then its null space has dimension at least n − k (the rank–nullity theorem of Section 2.6), so it must share a unit vector x with the (k + 1)-dimensional space spanned by $v_1, \ldots, v_{k+1}$. For this x, $\|(A - B)x\| = \|Ax\| \ge \sigma_{k+1}$. For the F-norm, see the original paper of Eckart and Young.　∎

The theorem was proved by Carl Eckart and Gale Young in 1936 for data analysis in psychometrics (they proved the F-norm case; Leon Mirsky extended it in 1960 to a class of norms that includes the 2-norm), and today it is the mathematical basis of data compression, principal component analysis and recommender systems. In robotics it appears in two kinds of problem. One is finding "the nearest special matrix": Section 4.8 finds the rotation matrix nearest to a matrix M that contains errors by replacing all the singular values of M with 1, which follows the same line of thought as this theorem. The other is judging the "true dimension" of data: if only the first two or three singular values of a set of motion data are significant, the motion actually takes place in only two or three directions. For the J of Example 2.4.1, the error of the best rank-1 approximation is exactly $\sigma_2$, and Program 2.4.1 checks this.

### 2.4.7 History and computation

The singular value decomposition has a shorter history than the eigenvalue decomposition, but it still goes back a hundred and fifty years. Eugenio Beltrami in 1873 and Camille Jordan in 1874 each independently gave this decomposition for square matrices, and James Joseph Sylvester gave another derivation in 1889; Erhard Schmidt extended it to integral operators in 1907 and proved a form of the low-rank approximation. The name "singular value" comes from the literature on integral equations and became fixed only after the 1930s. A 1993 paper by G. W. Stewart gives a detailed account of this history.

In computation, one should never do as the proof of Theorem 2.4.1 does and form $A^{\mathsf T}A$ first and then find its eigenvalues: the condition number of $A^{\mathsf T}A$ is the square of that of A (Section 2.5), and very small singular values get drowned in rounding errors. Numerical libraries use the algorithm proposed by Gene Golub and William Kahan in 1965 and completed by Golub and Christian Reinsch in 1970, which applies orthogonal transformations to A directly and never forms $A^{\mathsf T}A$. The program of this section computes by the steps of the proof only to show the derivation; in actual use, just call `numpy.linalg.svd`.

### 2.4.8 An everyday example

In the afternoon, with the sun shining at a slant, the shadow on the ground of a round traffic sign standing upright by the road is an ellipse; at another time of day the size and orientation of the ellipse change, but it is always an ellipse. This is no coincidence. Projection by parallel light is a linear transformation (Section 2.2.6), and the singular value decomposition says that every linear transformation turns a circle into an ellipse. The semi-major and semi-minor axes of the ellipse are the two singular values of this projection, and the directions of the major and minor axes are the left singular vectors. The lower the sun, the longer the shadow stretches, the further apart the two singular values, and the larger the condition number; when the sun lies exactly in the plane of the sign, the shadow is squashed into a line segment and one singular value becomes zero. That is what "singular" means.

This section has answered "in which direction does the end-effector move fast". The next section answers a more practical question: when there are more equations than unknowns, and all of them carry measurement errors, how do we get the best solution? The singular value decomposition appears again there, giving the general definition of the pseudoinverse.

**Engineer's notes**

- To judge whether a robot arm is near a singularity, look at the smallest singular value or the condition number, not at det J: the condition number does not depend on units, and the smallest singular value has a clear physical meaning (along the hardest direction to move, how many m/s of end-effector speed each rad/s of joint speed buys).
- The Jacobian matrix of a six-axis robot arm contains both linear velocity (m/s) and angular velocity (rad/s), with different dimensions, so if its singular values are computed directly, the result depends on the choice of the unit of length. A common remedy is to divide the linear-velocity part by a characteristic length (such as the length of the arm), or to analyse translation and rotation separately (Chapter 16).
- Controllers often set a threshold on the smallest singular value: below it they lower the speed or switch to damped least squares (Chapter 15), so that the joint velocities do not run out of control.
- `numpy.linalg.svd` returns $V^{\mathsf T}$, not V; the signs of singular vectors are not unique, so take care when comparing results.
