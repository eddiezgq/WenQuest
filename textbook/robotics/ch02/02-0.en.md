title: Chapter Overview
---

Almost everything that flows through a robot controller is a set of numbers: six joint angles, three coordinates of the end-effector, the components of a force. Arrange these numbers as vectors and the relations between them as matrices, and many problems that look quite different acquire one way of writing and one set of methods for solving them: how the end-effector moves when the joints turn; how the stiffness of a wrist is expressed in different frames; in which configurations a robot arm "cannot push"; how to calibrate a tool from a batch of measurements with errors; how the extra joints of a redundant arm can adjust the arm while the end-effector stays still; how to tidy three inaccurately taught points into a proper frame. The linear algebra of this chapter is that way of writing and those methods.

The chapter starts from high-school mathematics and builds vectors and matrices from the beginning, but, as an academic textbook should, it gives definitions, theorems and proofs. Every section starts from a robot problem. Two planar robot arms run through the whole chapter: the two-joint 2R arm and the three-joint 3R arm, with link lengths taken from the upper arm and forearm of the UR5e, as in Chapter 12.

- Section 2.1 introduces vectors, matrices and their operations through the velocity relation of the 2R arm, together with two numbers used again and again later: the determinant and the trace;
- Section 2.2 regards a matrix as a linear transformation, and discusses how the matrix of one transformation changes from one frame to another and which quantities do not depend on the frame;
- Section 2.3 discusses eigenvalues and eigenvectors and uses them to find the principal directions of the stiffness of a compliant wrist;
- Section 2.4 introduces the singular value decomposition: every matrix turns the unit circle into an ellipse, and this gives the manipulability of a robot arm;
- Section 2.5 discusses how to solve a system with more equations than unknowns and with measurement errors, that is, the method of least squares, uses it to calibrate the tool centre point, and then introduces the pseudoinverse;
- Section 2.6 discusses the null space and the range, and explains the self-motion of redundant arms;
- Section 2.7 introduces orthonormalization by the Gram–Schmidt process and uses it to build a workpiece frame from three taught points and to correct the orientation matrix given by a sensor.

The results of this chapter are tools for the chapters that follow. Chapter 3 builds on them to distinguish a vector itself from its components; Chapter 4 uses eigenvalues to prove Euler's rotation theorem, uses the trace to find the angle of a rotation, and uses the Gram–Schmidt process and the singular value decomposition to orthonormalize rotation matrices; the Jacobian matrices, singular configurations and redundancy of Chapters 16 and 17, the parameter identification of Chapter 26 and the hand–eye calibration of Chapter 50 all use the contents of this chapter directly.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. carry out operations on vectors and matrices with confidence, taking "a linear combination of the columns" as the basic way of understanding matrix multiplication; compute determinants and traces, and use det(AB) = det A det B and tr(AB) = tr(BA);
2. write down the matrix of a linear transformation, explain how it changes when the frame changes, and why the trace, the determinant and the eigenvalues do not change;
3. find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of 2×2 and 3×3 matrices, and use the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices;
4. explain, from the singular value decomposition, the geometric meaning of the unit circle becoming an ellipse, and compute the manipulability and condition number of a planar arm;
5. process calibration data by least squares, choose correctly between the normal equations, the QR decomposition and the singular value decomposition, and use the pseudoinverse to find the minimum-norm solution for a redundant arm;
6. find the null space and the range, use the rank–nullity theorem, and describe the self-motion of a redundant arm by projection onto the null space;
7. build an orthonormal basis with the Gram–Schmidt process, and explain how it is related to the QR decomposition and to the orthonormalization of matrices, and what to watch for numerically.
