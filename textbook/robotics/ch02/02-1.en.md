### 2.1.1 The problem: where does the end-effector go when the joints turn?

Figure 2.1.1 shows a robot arm with two joints working in a vertical plane, called a planar 2R arm (R stands for a revolute joint). To allow comparison with later chapters, we take the lengths of the two links equal to the upper arm and forearm of the UR5e, $L_1 = 0.425$ m and $L_2 = 0.392$ m. The angle $\theta_1$ of joint 1 is measured from the x axis, and the angle $\theta_2$ of joint 2 from the extension of the first link, both positive anticlockwise. Suppose that at this moment $\theta_1 = 30^\circ$ and $\theta_2 = 60^\circ$, and the controller commands joint 1 to turn at 0.5 rad/s and joint 2 at −1.0 rad/s. What is the velocity of the end-effector at this moment, and which way does it point?

The problem has two features. First, the answer is "a set of numbers": the velocity of the end-effector has two components, x and y, and the joint velocities are also two numbers. Second, as we shall see, each component of the end-effector velocity is a weighted sum of the two joint velocities, and the weights depend only on the present configuration of the arm. The mathematics that deals with "sets of numbers" and "weighted sums" is linear algebra. This section first sets up its basic objects, vectors and matrices, and two numbers that appear again and again in later chapters: the determinant and the trace.

### 2.1.2 Vectors

Describing the position of the end-effector takes two numbers in the plane and three in space; describing the configuration of the UR5e takes six joint angles. Arranging such an ordered set of numbers in a column, and regarding and operating on it as a whole, gives a vector.

**Definition 2.1.1 (Vector)** A column of n real numbers $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ in order,

$$
x = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{pmatrix} = (x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)^{\mathsf T} \tag{2.1.1}
$$

is called an n-dimensional **vector**, and $x_i$ is called its i-th component. The set of all n-dimensional vectors is written $\mathbb R^n$. To save space, a vector is often written across the line, with a T at the upper right (read "transpose", see Section 2.1.4) to show that it is really a column.

In this book vectors are written as lowercase italic letters, such as x, v, θ. By contrast, a single real number is called a **scalar**. Chapter 3 will further distinguish "the vector itself" from "its components in some frame"; in this chapter a vector is just a column of numbers, and no choice of frame is involved.

There are two basic operations on vectors. Let $x, y \in \mathbb R^n$ and let c be a scalar:

$$
x + y = (x_1 + y_1, \ldots, x_n + y_n)^{\mathsf T}, \qquad c\,x = (c x_1, \ldots, c x_n)^{\mathsf T}, \tag{2.1.2}
$$

that is, components are added one by one, and each component is multiplied by c. In the plane, x + y is the diagonal of the parallelogram with sides x and y, and cx is x stretched by a factor |c| (and reversed if c is negative). Putting the two operations together, $c_1 x_1 + c_2 x_2 + \cdots + c_k x_k$ is called a **linear combination** of the vectors $x_1, \ldots, x_k$, and the $c_i$ are called its coefficients. "Linear combination" is the most basic concept of this chapter, and every later section uses it.

**Definition 2.1.2 (Dot product and norm)** The dot product of two n-dimensional vectors is the sum of the products of corresponding components; the length of a vector (its Euclidean **norm**) is the square root of its dot product with itself:

$$
x \cdot y = x^{\mathsf T} y = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i y_i , \qquad \|x\| = \sqrt{x \cdot x} = \sqrt{x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2} . \tag{2.1.3}
$$

A vector of length 1 is called a **unit vector**. If $x \cdot y = 0$, x and y are said to be **orthogonal**.

In the plane and in space, the law of cosines shows that $x \cdot y = \|x\|\,\|y\|\cos\alpha$, where α is the angle between the two vectors, so orthogonal means perpendicular; Chapter 3 discusses the geometric meaning of the **dot product** in detail. In n dimensions we turn this round and use the formula to define the angle; for that we must make sure that the cosine on the right does not exceed 1.

**Theorem 2.1.1 (Cauchy–Schwarz inequality)** For any $x, y \in \mathbb R^n$,

$$
|x \cdot y| \le \|x\|\,\|y\| , \tag{2.1.4}
$$

with equality if and only if one of x, y is a multiple of the other.

*Proof*　If y = 0, both sides are 0. Let y ≠ 0. For every real number t, $\|x - t y\|^2 \ge 0$. Expanding,

$$
\|x\|^2 - 2t\,(x \cdot y) + t^2 \|y\|^2 \ge 0 .
$$

This is a quadratic function of t that is non-negative for every t, so its discriminant is not greater than zero: $4(x\cdot y)^2 - 4\|x\|^2\|y\|^2 \le 0$, which is Eq. (2.1.4). Equality holds if and only if there is a t with $x - ty = 0$.　∎

### 2.1.3 The velocity of the end-effector: a linear combination of columns

Back to the 2R arm. By plane geometry, the positions of the elbow (the centre of joint 2) and of the end-effector are

$$
p_e = \begin{pmatrix} L_1 \cos\theta_1 \\ L_1 \sin\theta_1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
p = p_e + \begin{pmatrix} L_2 \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \\ L_2 \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \end{pmatrix} . \tag{2.1.5}
$$

In our example, $p_e = {{ex2_1.elbow}}$ m and $p = {{ex2_1.tip}}$ m.

First turn joint 1 alone, with joint 2 locked. The whole arm then turns about the origin like a rigid rod, and the end-effector moves on a circle whose radius is its distance ‖p‖ from the origin. From high-school physics, a point moving on a circle with angular velocity $\dot\theta_1$ has a velocity of magnitude $\|p\|\,|\dot\theta_1|$, directed perpendicular to the radius; for an anticlockwise turn it points in the direction of the radius turned anticlockwise through 90°. Turning $p = (p_x, p_y)^{\mathsf T}$ anticlockwise through 90° gives $(-p_y, p_x)^{\mathsf T}$ (Section 4.1 will show that this is exactly what the rotation matrix for 90° does), so the velocity of the end-effector is

$$
\dot\theta_1 \begin{pmatrix} -p_y \\ p_x \end{pmatrix} = \dot\theta_1\, j_1 , \qquad j_1 = \begin{pmatrix} -p_y \\ p_x \end{pmatrix} . \tag{2.1.6}
$$

Next turn joint 2 alone. The first link now stays still and the second turns about the elbow; the same reasoning gives $\dot\theta_2\, j_2$, where $j_2$ is "the vector from the elbow to the end-effector", $p - p_e$, turned anticlockwise through 90°. When both joints turn at once, the displacement of the end-effector over a very short time is the sum of the two displacements (Chapter 16 proves this rigorously with derivatives), and so

$$
v = \dot\theta_1\, j_1 + \dot\theta_2\, j_2 . \tag{2.1.7}
$$

The velocity of the end-effector is a linear combination of two fixed vectors $j_1$ and $j_2$, and the coefficients are the two joint velocities. Figure 2.1.1 shows this relation: the two partial velocities form a parallelogram, and the velocity of the end-effector is its diagonal.

::: Figure 2.1.1
src: fig2_1_1
caption: A planar 2R arm at θ = (30°, 60°): with joint 1 alone turning, the velocity of the end-effector is perpendicular to the line from the base to the end-effector (blue); with joint 2 alone, it is perpendicular to the line from the elbow to the end-effector (gold); their sum is the velocity of the end-effector (black)
:::

Put $j_1$ and $j_2$ side by side as a table of numbers with two columns, and call it J; then Eq. (2.1.7) can be written as "table times vector". Such a table of numbers is a matrix.

**Definition 2.1.3 (Matrix, and a matrix times a vector)** A table of numbers with m rows and n columns,

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & \cdots & a_{1n} \\ \vdots & & \vdots \\ a_{m1} & \cdots & a_{mn} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & \cdots & a_n \end{pmatrix}
$$

is called an m×n **matrix**; $a_{ij}$ is the entry in row i and column j, and $a_j \in \mathbb R^m$ is its j-th column. The product of the matrix A with a vector $x \in \mathbb R^n$ is defined as the linear combination of the columns of A whose coefficients are the components of x:

$$
A x = x_1 a_1 + x_2 a_2 + \cdots + x_n a_n , \qquad (Ax)_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij} x_j . \tag{2.1.8}
$$

The two forms in Eq. (2.1.8) are two ways of looking at the same thing. The left one "reads by columns": Ax is a linear combination of the columns of A. The right one "reads by rows": the i-th component of Ax is the dot product of row i of A with x. Reading by columns says what the result "is made of"; reading by rows is convenient for computing one component at a time. Both should become second nature.

For the 2R arm, substituting Eq. (2.1.5) into $j_1$ and $j_2$ gives

$$
v = J\dot\theta, \qquad
J = \begin{pmatrix} -L_1\sin\theta_1 - L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & -L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \end{pmatrix} . \tag{2.1.9}
$$

This matrix, which turns joint velocities into the velocity of the end-effector, is called the **Jacobian matrix** of the arm, after the German mathematician Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi, who studied the matrix of partial derivatives of functions of several variables. It is the subject of Chapter 16; in this chapter we study it as a concrete matrix. Note that J depends only on θ, not on $\dot\theta$: for a given configuration of the arm, the relation between the velocity of the end-effector and the joint velocities is a "weighted sum", and that is exactly why linear algebra can deal with it.

**Example 2.1.1** Find the velocity of the end-effector of the 2R arm at θ = (30°, 60°) with $\dot\theta = (0.5, -1.0)^{\mathsf T}$ rad/s.

By Eq. (2.1.9),

$$
J = {{ex2_1.J}} \ \mathrm{m/rad} .
$$

The second entry of the second column is zero because $\theta_1 + \theta_2 = 90^\circ$: the forearm points straight up, so with joint 2 alone turning the end-effector moves horizontally. Reading by columns:

$$
v = 0.5 \times {{ex2_1.j1}} + (-1.0) \times {{ex2_1.j2}} = {{ex2_1.v}}\ \mathrm{m/s} .
$$

Reading by rows, $v_x = (-{{ex2_1.tip_y:.4f}})(0.5) + (-0.392)(-1.0) = {{ex2_1.vx:.5f}}$ m/s and $v_y = ({{ex2_1.tip_x:.4f}})(0.5) + 0 \times (-1.0) = {{ex2_1.vy:.5f}}$ m/s, the same result. The end-effector moves up and to the right at {{ex2_1.speed:.4f}} m/s. The cosine of the angle between the two columns is $j_1 \cdot j_2 / (\|j_1\|\|j_2\|) = {{ex2_1.cos12:.4f}}$, so the columns are about {{ex2_1.ang12:.1f}}° apart. Program 2.1.1 computes the result both ways and checks that Eq. (2.1.9) agrees with the geometric construction of Figure 2.1.1.

The units of the Jacobian matrix deserve a word. $\dot\theta$ is in rad/s and v in m/s, so the entries of J are in m/rad. Since the radian is dimensionless, this is often written simply as m; but keep in mind that each column of J says "how many metres the end-effector moves for each radian that joint turns".

### 2.1.4 Matrix multiplication: two transformations one after the other

Many palletizing robots drive the forearm through a parallelogram linkage: the second motor sits on the base and, through the linkage, directly controls the angle of the forearm to the horizontal, $\varphi_2 = \theta_1 + \theta_2$, rather than its angle $\theta_2$ to the upper arm. The motor then does not have to turn with the upper arm, and the upper arm is lighter. Let the angles controlled by the two motors be $\varphi = (\varphi_1, \varphi_2)^{\mathsf T}$ with $\varphi_1 = \theta_1$; then

$$
\theta_1 = \varphi_1 , \qquad \theta_2 = -\varphi_1 + \varphi_2 , \qquad \text{that is} \qquad \dot\theta = C\dot\varphi , \quad C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The velocity of the end-effector is $v = J\dot\theta = J(C\dot\varphi)$. These are two "matrix times vector" steps one after the other: first C turns the angular velocities on the motor side into joint angular velocities, then J turns the joint angular velocities into the velocity of the end-effector. Can J and C be combined beforehand into one matrix, so that the work is done in one step? Reading by columns, $C\dot\varphi = \dot\varphi_1 c_1 + \dot\varphi_2 c_2$ (where $c_k$ is the k-th column of C), so

$$
J(C\dot\varphi) = \dot\varphi_1 (J c_1) + \dot\varphi_2 (J c_2) .
$$

This shows that the k-th column of the combined matrix is $J c_k$. That is the definition of **matrix multiplication**.

**Definition 2.1.4 (Matrix multiplication)** Let A be an m×n matrix and B an n×p matrix with columns $b_1, \ldots, b_p$. The product of A and B is the m×p matrix

$$
AB = \begin{pmatrix} A b_1 & A b_2 & \cdots & A b_p \end{pmatrix}, \qquad (AB)_{ik} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij} b_{jk} . \tag{2.1.10}
$$

AB makes sense only when the number of columns of A equals the number of rows of B. By the definition, $(AB)x = A(Bx)$ for every x: doing B and then A is the same as doing AB once. Note the order: the factor written on the right acts first.

**Theorem 2.1.2 (Laws of matrix multiplication)** Whenever the products make sense:

1. associative law: $(AB)C = A(BC)$;
2. distributive laws: $A(B + C) = AB + AC$ and $(A + B)C = AC + BC$;
3. the commutative law does not hold in general: AB and BA are in general not equal, and one of them may not even make sense.

*Proof*　(1) For any vector x, using $(AB)x = A(Bx)$ from the definition repeatedly, $((AB)C)x = (AB)(Cx) = A(B(Cx)) = A((BC)x) = (A(BC))x$. The two matrices act in the same way on every x; taking x to be the vector whose k-th component is 1 and all others 0 shows that their k-th columns are the same. (2) Check entry by entry from Eq. (2.1.10). (3) The example below gives a counterexample.　∎

**Example 2.1.2** For the configuration of the arm in Example 2.1.1, the angular velocities on the motor side are $\dot\varphi = (0.5, -0.5)^{\mathsf T}$ rad/s (the joint velocities they correspond to are exactly $C\dot\varphi = (0.5, -1.0)^{\mathsf T}$). The combined matrix and the product taken in the other order are

$$
JC = {{ex2_1.JC}}, \qquad CJ = {{ex2_1.CJ}} .
$$

$(JC)\dot\varphi$ is the same as v in Example 2.1.1. JC ≠ CJ. Moreover CJ has no physical meaning: the output of J is a velocity of the end-effector (m/s), while the input of C should be motor angular velocities (rad/s), so the units do not even match. The order in a matrix product is not a matter of form; it corresponds to the order in which things act.

For convenience of writing we introduce a few more common notions.

**Definition 2.1.5 (Transpose, symmetric matrix, identity matrix, diagonal matrix)** Interchanging the rows and columns of an m×n matrix A gives an n×m matrix called its **transpose**, written $A^{\mathsf T}$, with $(A^{\mathsf T})_{ij} = a_{ji}$. A square matrix with $A^{\mathsf T} = A$ is called a **symmetric matrix**. The n×n matrix whose entries on the main diagonal are all 1 and all other entries 0 is called the **identity matrix**, written I or $I_n$; Ix = x for every x. A square matrix whose entries off the main diagonal are all 0 is called a **diagonal matrix**, written $\mathrm{diag}(d_1, \ldots, d_n)$.

Transposition and multiplication are related by

$$
(AB)^{\mathsf T} = B^{\mathsf T} A^{\mathsf T} . \tag{2.1.11}
$$

Proof: the (k, i) entries of both sides equal $\sum_j a_{ij} b_{jk}$. Note that the order is reversed. This rule is used again and again in Chapter 4 to prove the properties of rotation matrices. The dot product can also be written as a matrix product: $x \cdot y = x^{\mathsf T} y$, a 1×n matrix times an n×1 matrix, giving a 1×1 matrix, that is, a number.

### 2.1.5 The inverse matrix: joint velocities from the velocity of the end-effector

In practice the reverse problem is more common: if the end-effector is to move with a given velocity, how fast should the joints turn? This calls for "undoing" the action of J.

**Definition 2.1.6 (Inverse matrix)** For an n×n matrix A, if there is an n×n matrix B with $AB = BA = I$, A is called invertible and B is called the **inverse matrix** of A, written $A^{-1}$. A square matrix that is not invertible is called a **singular matrix**.

If the inverse exists, it is unique: if B and B′ both satisfy the definition, then $B = BI = B(AB') = (BA)B' = B'$. For a 2×2 matrix, direct checking shows that

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} \qquad (ad - bc \neq 0) . \tag{2.1.12}
$$

That is, swap the two entries on the main diagonal, change the sign of the two entries on the other diagonal, and divide by ad − bc. This number ad − bc is the determinant of the next subsection.

**Theorem 2.1.3** If A and B are both invertible n×n matrices, then AB is invertible and $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$; $A^{\mathsf T}$ is invertible and $(A^{\mathsf T})^{-1} = (A^{-1})^{\mathsf T}$.

*Proof*　$(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I$, and similarly on the other side. By Eq. (2.1.11), $A^{\mathsf T}(A^{-1})^{\mathsf T} = (A^{-1}A)^{\mathsf T} = I^{\mathsf T} = I$.　∎

Once again the order is reversed, for the same reason as with putting on and taking off clothes: what goes on first comes off last.

**Example 2.1.3** The end-effector is to move straight up at 0.1 m/s, that is, $v = (0, 0.1)^{\mathsf T}$ m/s. Find the joint velocities.

By Eq. (2.1.12),

$$
J^{-1} = {{ex2_1.Jinv}} , \qquad \dot\theta = J^{-1} v = {{ex2_1.thd_need}}\ \mathrm{rad/s} .
$$

Joint 1 turns at {{ex2_1.thd1_need:.4f}} rad/s and joint 2 at {{ex2_1.thd2_need:.4f}} rad/s, that is, in the opposite direction to joint 1. Multiplying back by J gives exactly $(0, 0.1)^{\mathsf T}$. Program 2.1.1 also checks this result with the general solver of a numerical library, and verifies Theorem 2.1.3.

In actual computation the inverse matrix is rarely worked out and then multiplied; there are faster and more accurate ways to solve $J\dot\theta = v$ (elimination, see Chapter 3 of Solomon's book). The inverse matrix is mainly a tool for derivation and for expressing results.

### 2.1.6 The determinant

In Eq. (2.1.12), ad − bc decides whether a 2×2 matrix can be inverted. For a general square matrix, the number that plays this role is called the determinant.

**Definition 2.1.7 (Determinant)** The determinant of a 1×1 matrix (a) is a itself. The determinant of a 2×2 matrix is

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc . \tag{2.1.13}
$$

The **determinant** of an n×n matrix (n ≥ 2) is defined recursively by expansion along the first row:

$$
\det A = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (-1)^{1+j}\, a_{1j}\, \det A_{1j} , \tag{2.1.14}
$$

where $A_{1j}$ is the (n−1)×(n−1) matrix left after deleting row 1 and column j of A, and $(-1)^{1+j}\det A_{1j}$ is called the **cofactor** of the entry $a_{1j}$.

Carrying the recursion all the way down, det A is a sum of n! terms:

$$
\det A = \sum_{\sigma} \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)\, a_{1\sigma(1)}\, a_{2\sigma(2)} \cdots a_{n\sigma(n)} .
$$

Each term is a product of one entry from each row and each column: from row i it takes column σ(i). The column numbers σ(1), …, σ(n) are a rearrangement of 1, 2, …, n, called a **permutation**, and the sum runs over all n! permutations. A permutation can be restored to 1, 2, …, n by successive swaps of two entries; whichever sequence of swaps is used, the parity of their number is always the same; sgn σ = +1 when it is even and sgn σ = −1 when it is odd. The formula above is called the Leibniz formula. It agrees with the definition given by the recursion (2.1.14): in an expansion along the first row, once $a_{1j}$ has been chosen, the remaining terms are exactly the expansion of the submatrix $A_{1j}$, and the sign $(-1)^{1+j}$ accounts for exactly the j − 1 swaps needed to move j to the front; induction on n confirms this. That the parity is well defined, and this equivalence, are purely combinatorial facts; for the details see Chapter 5 of Strang's book. From the Leibniz formula one can also prove that expanding along any row or any column gives the same result; this is called the Laplace expansion theorem.

**Example 2.1.4** Compute by expansion along the first row:

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\det A = 2\det\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix} - 1\cdot\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix} + 0 = {{ex2_1.cof1:.0f}} + ({{ex2_1.cof2:.0f}}) + {{ex2_1.cof3:.0f}} = {{ex2_1.det3:.0f}} .
$$

Program 2.1.1 computes it again by expansion along the second column and gets the same result.

**Theorem 2.1.4 (Properties of the determinant)** Let A and B be n×n matrices.

1. $\det A^{\mathsf T} = \det A$;
2. interchanging two rows (or two columns) changes the sign of the determinant; hence a matrix with two equal rows has determinant zero;
3. the determinant is linear in each row (each column); hence adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant unchanged;
4. the determinant of a triangular matrix (all zeros below, or above, the main diagonal) is the product of the entries on the main diagonal;
5. $\det(AB) = \det A\,\det B$;
6. A is invertible if and only if $\det A \neq 0$, and then $\det A^{-1} = 1/\det A$.

*Proof*　For 2×2 matrices every item can be checked directly, for example (5): let $B = \begin{pmatrix} e & f \\ g & h \end{pmatrix}$; then

$$
\det(AB) = (ae + bg)(cf + dh) - (af + bh)(ce + dg) = adeh + bcfg - adfg - bceh = (ad - bc)(eh - fg) .
$$

For general n, (1)–(4) follow by comparing the Leibniz formula term by term. (1) Permutations σ correspond one to one with their inverse permutations, and the two have the same parity; the term of $\det A^{\mathsf T}$ belonging to $\sigma^{-1}$ and the term of $\det A$ belonging to σ are products of the same n entries, so the two sums agree term by term. (2) Interchanging rows k and l leaves the factors of every term unchanged, but the corresponding permutation gets one extra swap and its parity changes, so every term changes sign; if the two rows are equal, the interchange leaves the matrix unchanged, so det A = −det A and det A = 0. (3) Each term contains exactly one entry of row k, and only once, so det A is a linear function of the entries of row k; adding c times row l to row k gives, by linearity, the original determinant plus c times the determinant of the matrix "with row k replaced by row l", which has two equal rows and is zero. The statements about columns follow from (1). (4) Take an upper triangular matrix: if a term has σ(i) < i for some i, it contains an entry below the main diagonal and is zero; the only permutation with σ(i) ≥ i for every i is the identity permutation, so only the product of the entries on the main diagonal remains.

(5) Write $b_k$ for the k-th column of B; then the k-th column of AB is $Ab_k$ (Definition 2.1.4). Regard

$$
D(b_1, \ldots, b_n) = \det\begin{pmatrix} Ab_1 & \cdots & Ab_n \end{pmatrix} = \det(AB)
$$

as a function of the columns of B. By $A(cb + c'b') = cAb + c'Ab'$ and property (3), D is linear in each column; by property (2), interchanging two columns of B changes the sign of D, and D = 0 when B has two equal columns. Writing each column as $b_k = \sum_{i} b_{ik}e_i$ (where $e_i$ is the vector whose i-th component is 1 and all others 0) and expanding column by column using linearity gives a sum of $n^n$ terms:

$$
D(b_1, \ldots, b_n) = \sum_{i_1, \ldots, i_n} b_{i_1 1}\, b_{i_2 2} \cdots b_{i_n n}\, D(e_{i_1}, \ldots, e_{i_n}) .
$$

If two of $i_1, \ldots, i_n$ are equal, $D(e_{i_1}, \ldots, e_{i_n})$ has two equal columns and is zero. In each remaining term, $(i_1, \ldots, i_n)$ is a permutation τ; bringing $e_{\tau(1)}, \ldots, e_{\tau(n)}$ into the order $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ by a number of interchanges of two columns changes the sign once per interchange, so $D(e_{\tau(1)}, \ldots, e_{\tau(n)}) = \mathrm{sgn}(\tau)\,D(e_1, \ldots, e_n) = \mathrm{sgn}(\tau)\det(AI) = \mathrm{sgn}(\tau)\det A$. Hence

$$
\det(AB) = \det A \sum_{\tau}\mathrm{sgn}(\tau)\, b_{\tau(1)1}\, b_{\tau(2)2} \cdots b_{\tau(n)n} = \det A\,\det B^{\mathsf T} = \det A\,\det B ,
$$

where the sum in the middle is exactly the Leibniz formula for $B^{\mathsf T}$, and the last step uses (1). This proof does not need to assume that A is invertible.

(6) If A is invertible, then by (5), $\det A\,\det A^{-1} = \det I = 1$, so $\det A \neq 0$ and $\det A^{-1} = 1/\det A$. Conversely, let det A ≠ 0. Write $C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j}\det A_{ij}$, where $A_{ij}$ is the submatrix left after deleting row i and column j of A, and let C be the n×n matrix with entries $C_{ij}$. $(AC^{\mathsf T})_{ii} = \sum_j a_{ij}C_{ij}$ is the expansion of det A along row i and equals det A; when k ≠ i, $(AC^{\mathsf T})_{ki} = \sum_j a_{kj}C_{ij}$ is the expansion along row i of the matrix obtained by replacing row i of A by row k; this matrix has two equal rows, and its determinant is zero. So $AC^{\mathsf T} = (\det A)\,I$; likewise, expanding along columns gives $C^{\mathsf T}A = (\det A)\,I$. Hence $A^{-1} = C^{\mathsf T}/\det A$. The matrix $C^{\mathsf T}$ is called the adjugate of A, written adj A; Eq. (2.1.12) is the special case n = 2 of this formula.　∎

The geometric meaning of the determinant is discussed in Section 2.2: it is the factor by which a linear transformation magnifies area (volume in three dimensions), a signed "oriented area". Here we first look at what it means for a robot arm.

**Theorem 2.1.5** The determinant of the Jacobian matrix (2.1.9) of a planar 2R arm is

$$
\det J = L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2 , \tag{2.1.15}
$$

independent of $\theta_1$. When the arm is straight ($\theta_2 = 0$) or completely folded ($\theta_2 = \pm 180^\circ$), det J = 0 and J is not invertible.

*Proof*　Write $s_1 = \sin\theta_1$, $c_1 = \cos\theta_1$, $s_{12} = \sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)$, $c_{12} = \cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)$. By Eq. (2.1.13),

$$
\det J = (-L_1 s_1 - L_2 s_{12})\,L_2 c_{12} - (-L_2 s_{12})(L_1 c_1 + L_2 c_{12}) = L_1 L_2 (s_{12} c_1 - c_{12} s_1) = L_1 L_2 \sin\theta_2 ,
$$

where the last step uses the sine difference identity.　∎

In our example, $\det J = 0.425 \times 0.392 \times \sin 60^\circ = {{ex2_1.detJ:.5f}}$ m². Figure 2.1.2 shows how det J varies with $\theta_2$. It is largest at $\theta_2 = 90^\circ$ and zero when the arm is straight or folded. When the arm is straight, the two columns $j_1$ and $j_2$ are both perpendicular to the arm and so parallel to each other; their linear combinations can only point perpendicular to the arm, and however the joints turn, the end-effector cannot move along the arm. Such a configuration is called a singular configuration and is discussed in detail in Chapter 16. Near it, by Eq. (2.1.12), the entries of $J^{-1}$ are proportional to 1/det J, so to move the end-effector slowly along the arm, the joint velocities become very large. This is why a robot "suddenly spins wildly" at the edge of its workspace. Section 2.4 describes this phenomenon more finely with singular values.

The idea of the determinant is old. Seki Takakazu used it in 1683 to eliminate unknowns from systems of equations, and Leibniz, in a letter to l'Hôpital in 1693, used it to discuss when a system of linear equations has a solution. The modern use of the word "determinant" (*déterminant*) and the product theorem (5) come from a paper of Augustin-Louis Cauchy in 1812; Jacques Binet gave the product theorem independently in the same year. The word "matrix" was introduced by James Joseph Sylvester in 1850, and Arthur Cayley set out the operations on matrices systematically in a paper of 1858.

### 2.1.7 The trace

The determinant condenses a square matrix into one number; the trace is another, simpler condensation.

**Definition 2.1.8 (Trace)** The sum of the entries on the main diagonal of an n×n matrix A is called its **trace**, written tr A:

$$
\mathrm{tr}\,A = a_{11} + a_{22} + \cdots + a_{nn} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{ii} . \tag{2.1.16}
$$

The definition of the trace is so simple that its usefulness is easily underrated. Chapter 4 will show that the trace of a rotation matrix gives the angle of rotation directly, $\mathrm{tr}\,R = 1 + 2\cos\theta$; and that this result can be used in any frame depends on item (3) of the following theorem.

**Theorem 2.1.6 (Properties of the trace)**

1. $\mathrm{tr}(A + B) = \mathrm{tr}\,A + \mathrm{tr}\,B$, $\mathrm{tr}(cA) = c\,\mathrm{tr}\,A$, $\mathrm{tr}\,A^{\mathsf T} = \mathrm{tr}\,A$;
2. if A is an m×n matrix and B an n×m matrix, then tr(AB) = tr(BA), see Eq. (2.1.17);
3. the trace is unchanged under cyclic permutation: $\mathrm{tr}(ABC) = \mathrm{tr}(BCA) = \mathrm{tr}(CAB)$; in particular, if P is invertible, then $\mathrm{tr}(P^{-1}AP) = \mathrm{tr}\,A$.

$$
\mathrm{tr}(AB) = \mathrm{tr}(BA) . \tag{2.1.17}
$$

*Proof*　(1) Follows directly from the definition. (2) AB is an m×m matrix and BA an n×n matrix; the two may be of different sizes. By Eq. (2.1.10),

$$
\mathrm{tr}(AB) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} (AB)_{ii} = \sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij} b_{ji} , \qquad
\mathrm{tr}(BA) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (BA)_{jj} = \sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{i=1}^{m} b_{ji} a_{ij} .
$$

The two double sums are sums of the same mn products $a_{ij}b_{ji}$, added in a different order, so they are equal. (3) Regard BC as one matrix; by (2), $\mathrm{tr}(A(BC)) = \mathrm{tr}((BC)A)$; applying it once more gives the third equality. Taking $A \to P^{-1}$, $B \to A$, $C \to P$ gives $\mathrm{tr}(P^{-1}AP) = \mathrm{tr}(APP^{-1}) = \mathrm{tr}\,A$.　∎

Note that (3) allows only "cyclic" moves, that is, moving the leftmost factor to the far right; it does not allow the order to be changed at will. In general $\mathrm{tr}(ABC) \neq \mathrm{tr}(ACB)$.

**Example 2.1.5** Verify the properties of the trace.

(a) For Example 2.1.2, $\mathrm{tr}(JC) = {{ex2_1.trJC:.4f}}$ and $\mathrm{tr}(CJ) = {{ex2_1.trCJ:.4f}}$, which are equal, even though JC ≠ CJ.

(b) Take the 2×3 matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ and the 3×2 matrix $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$:

$$
AB = {{ex2_1.AB}} , \qquad BA = {{ex2_1.BA}} ;
$$

one is 2×2 and the other 3×3, and both traces equal {{ex2_1.trAB:.0f}}.

(c) Take $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $Q = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$; then $\mathrm{tr}(PQS) = \mathrm{tr}(SPQ) = {{ex2_1.trPQS:.0f}}$, whereas $\mathrm{tr}(PSQ) = {{ex2_1.trPSQ:.0f}}$: cyclic moves are allowed, swaps are not.

The trace also gives a common way of measuring the "size" of a matrix. Regard all the entries of the matrix as one long vector and take its length:

$$
\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^2} = \sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(A^{\mathsf T}A)} . \tag{2.1.18}
$$

The second equality holds because the j-th diagonal entry of $A^{\mathsf T}A$ is the dot product of column j of A with itself. This quantity is called the **Frobenius norm**. For J of Example 2.1.1, $\|J\|_F = {{ex2_1.froJ:.4f}}$. Section 4.8 uses it to measure how far apart two rotation matrices are, and uses Eq. (2.1.17) to turn "find the nearest rotation matrix" into maximizing a trace.

### 2.1.8 An everyday example

You go to the supermarket to buy apples and oranges. Suppose apples cost 12 yuan and contain about 40 mg of vitamin C per kilogram; oranges cost 8 yuan and contain about 500 mg per kilogram. If you buy $x_1$ kg of apples and $x_2$ kg of oranges, the total price and the total amount of vitamin C are

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \text{total price} \\ \text{vitamin C} \end{pmatrix} = x_1 \begin{pmatrix} 12 \\ 40 \end{pmatrix} + x_2 \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 500 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 12 & 8 \\ 40 & 500 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

Each kind of fruit is a column of the matrix, and the amounts bought are the coefficients; this is exactly "reading by columns" in Eq. (2.1.8). Conversely, given a budget and a target for vitamin C, finding how much of each to buy means finding the inverse matrix. If the two fruits happened to have exactly the same "ratio of price to vitamin C", the two columns would be parallel and the determinant zero, and it would be impossible to meet any two targets at once; this is the same thing as the end-effector being able to move in only one direction when the arm is straight.

This section has operated on the Jacobian matrix as a set of numbers. The next section takes another point of view: it regards a matrix as a "deformation" of the whole plane, and studies how the matrix of one deformation changes from one frame to another.

**Engineer's notes**

- When writing a program, first check the sizes of the matrices: m×n times n×p gives m×p. Most errors in matrix code can be caught just by asking "do the dimensions match?".
- Do not carelessly write the two factors of a matrix product in the wrong order. To check, work out which one acts on the vector first, and write that one on the right; then check that the units join up, as in Example 2.1.2.
- To solve $J\dot\theta = v$, use the solver of a numerical library (such as `numpy.linalg.solve`); do not compute the inverse and then multiply. Near a singularity neither is reliable, and the pseudoinverse of Section 2.5 or the damped least squares of Chapter 15 should be used instead.
- The size of det J depends on the unit of length: for the same robot, det J of the 2R arm is a million times larger when lengths are in millimetres. Whether the arm is "near a singularity" cannot be judged from the absolute size of det J alone; look at a dimensionless quantity such as the condition number of Section 2.4.
- Always use radians for angles in programs; if the sin and cos in Eq. (2.1.9) are given degrees by mistake, the values of J are completely wrong, and no error is reported.
