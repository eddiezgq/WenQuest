### 2.7.1 The problem: teach three points, build a workpiece frame

A workpiece placed on a worktable does not necessarily have its edges parallel to the coordinate axes of the robot base. For convenience in programming, the operator often sets up a **user frame** (also called a workpiece frame) on the workpiece, and from then on all points are taught relative to it; when the workpiece is moved, only this frame needs to be set up again, and the program need not change. Almost every industrial robot offers a "three-point method": the tool tip touches three points in turn, $p_0$ as the origin, $p_1$ on the positive x axis, and $p_2$ in the xy plane, on the positive-y side.

Suppose the three teaching operations give (in mm)

$$
p_0 = (500, -200, 30)^{\mathsf T}, \qquad p_1 = (800, -195, 31)^{\mathsf T}, \qquad p_2 = (505, 100, 29)^{\mathsf T} .
$$

So the x direction is roughly $a_1 = p_1 - p_0 = {{ex2_7.a1}}$, and the y direction roughly $a_2 = p_2 - p_0 = {{ex2_7.a2}}$. The trouble is that these two directions are not perpendicular: their dot product is {{ex2_7.dot12:.0f}} mm², and the angle between them is {{ex2_7.ang:.2f}}°. Hand tremor and pointing errors during teaching mean that no three taught points can ever give two exactly perpendicular directions. Yet the three axes of a frame must be mutually perpendicular and of length 1, or the rotation matrices of Chapter 4 do not hold. How do we get a proper set of coordinate axes from these two "skewed" directions?

The same problem turns up everywhere in robotics: an orientation matrix given by an inertial measurement unit or a calibration program has columns that are not exactly orthogonal because of errors; a controller that multiplies rotation matrices over and over sees them drift slowly away from orthogonality through rounding errors (Section 4.8). Turning a set of vectors into mutually orthogonal unit vectors is called **orthonormalization**. The Gram–Schmidt process is the most direct way to do it, and the earlier sections of this chapter have already used it in proving the spectral theorem and the singular value decomposition.

### 2.7.2 Orthonormal bases and orthogonal projection

**Definition 2.7.1 (Orthonormal basis)** If the vectors $q_1, \ldots, q_k$ satisfy $q_i\cdot q_j = 0$ (i ≠ j) and $\|q_i\| = 1$, they are called a set of orthonormal vectors; if they also form a basis of a subspace S, they are called an **orthonormal basis** of S.

Orthonormal vectors are always linearly independent: if $\sum c_iq_i = 0$, taking the dot product with $q_j$ gives $c_j = 0$. The greatest advantage of an orthonormal basis is that finding coordinates requires no system of equations: if $x = \sum c_iq_i$, taking the dot product with $q_j$ gives at once $c_j = q_j\cdot x$. This is in sharp contrast to a general basis P in Section 2.2, which needs $P^{-1}$.

The core operation of the Gram–Schmidt process is projection. Projecting a vector a onto the line of a unit vector e gives

$$
\mathrm{proj}_e\,a = (e\cdot a)\,e , \tag{2.7.1}
$$

and the remaining part $a - (e\cdot a)e$ is perpendicular to e, because $e\cdot(a - (e\cdot a)e) = e\cdot a - (e\cdot a)(e\cdot e) = 0$. This is the special case $\|e\| = 1$ of least squares with one unknown in Section 2.5 (Figure 2.5.2, left). Generalizing to a subspace:

**Theorem 2.7.1 (Projection onto a subspace)** Let $q_1, \ldots, q_k$ be an orthonormal basis of a subspace S, and $Q = (q_1, \ldots, q_k)$. Then for any vector a,

$$
\hat a = \sum_{i=1}^{k}(q_i\cdot a)\,q_i = QQ^{\mathsf T}a \tag{2.7.2}
$$

is the point of S closest to a, and $a - \hat a$ is perpendicular to every vector in S.

*Proof*　For each j, $q_j\cdot(a - \hat a) = q_j\cdot a - \sum_i(q_i\cdot a)(q_j\cdot q_i) = q_j\cdot a - q_j\cdot a = 0$, so $a - \hat a$ is perpendicular to every basis vector of S, and hence to every vector in S. By Theorem 2.5.1 (with A = Q), $\hat a$ is the closest point in the least-squares sense. Since $Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I$, the projection matrix $Q(Q^{\mathsf T}Q)^{-1}Q^{\mathsf T}$ of Eq. (2.5.6) simplifies to $QQ^{\mathsf T}$.　∎

### 2.7.3 The Gram–Schmidt process

Given linearly independent vectors $a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n$, handle them one at a time: the first only needs to be normalized; for each later one, first subtract its projections onto the directions already obtained, so that the remaining part is perpendicular to all of them, then normalize. As a formula, for j = 1, 2, …, n:

$$
w_j = a_j - \sum_{i=1}^{j-1}(q_i\cdot a_j)\,q_i , \qquad q_j = \frac{w_j}{\|w_j\|} . \tag{2.7.3}
$$

Figure 2.7.1 draws the three steps in the plane: $a_1$ is normalized to give $q_1$; $a_2$ has its projection onto $q_1$ subtracted, and the remainder $w_2$ is perpendicular to $q_1$; $w_2$ is normalized to give $q_2$.

**Theorem 2.7.2** If $a_1, \ldots, a_n$ are linearly independent, then each $w_j$ of Eq. (2.7.3) is non-zero, the resulting $q_1, \ldots, q_n$ are orthonormal, and for each k,

$$
\mathrm{span}\{q_1, \ldots, q_k\} = \mathrm{span}\{a_1, \ldots, a_k\} .
$$

*Proof*　By induction on k. For k = 1, $q_1 = a_1/\|a_1\|$ and $a_1 \neq 0$ (a linearly independent set contains no zero vector). Suppose the statement holds for k − 1. By Eq. (2.7.3), $w_k$ is $a_k$ minus a linear combination of $q_1, \ldots, q_{k-1}$, and the latter belongs to $\mathrm{span}\{a_1, \ldots, a_{k-1}\}$. If $w_k = 0$, then $a_k$ would be a linear combination of $a_1, \ldots, a_{k-1}$, contradicting linear independence. By Theorem 2.7.1, $w_k$ is perpendicular to all of $q_1, \ldots, q_{k-1}$, and so is $q_k$ after normalization. Finally, $q_k$ is a linear combination of $a_1, \ldots, a_k$, and $a_k = w_k + \sum_{i<k}(q_i\cdot a_k)q_i$ is a linear combination of $q_1, \ldots, q_k$, so the two sets of vectors span the same subspace.　∎

**Corollary 2.7.1** Any set of orthonormal vectors $q_1, \ldots, q_k$ in $\mathbb R^n$ can be extended to an orthonormal basis $q_1, \ldots, q_n$ of $\mathbb R^n$.

*Proof*　Process the vectors $q_1, \ldots, q_k, e_1, \ldots, e_n$ in turn by Eq. (2.7.3), but discard any vector for which $w_j = 0$ (it is a linear combination of the preceding vectors). The first k vectors are already orthonormal and are left unchanged by the processing. By the proof of Theorem 2.7.2, the vectors kept are mutually orthogonal and of length 1, and they span the same subspace as the whole original set; the latter contains $e_1, \ldots, e_n$, so it is $\mathbb R^n$. Orthonormal vectors are linearly independent (Section 2.7.2), so they form a basis of $\mathbb R^n$, and by Theorem 2.6.1 there are exactly n of them.　∎

The proof of the spectral theorem in Section 2.3 and the proof that the singular value decomposition exists in Section 2.4 use exactly this corollary.

The last statement of Theorem 2.7.2 is important: the first k new vectors span the same subspace as the first k original vectors. For a coordinate frame, this means that **the direction of the first axis keeps the direction of $a_1$ exactly, and the plane of the first and second axes keeps the plane of $a_1$ and $a_2$ exactly**. This is both the strength of the Gram–Schmidt process (it respects the order of teaching) and its weakness (all the error is pushed onto the later axes), and we shall see both sides below.

The method is older than its name. P.-S. Laplace, in his *Théorie analytique des probabilités* of 1812 and its later supplements, already used this orthogonalization process in least-squares computations. J. P. Gram used it in 1883 to construct orthogonal function expansions in the least-squares sense; E. Schmidt, studying integral equations in 1907, gave the recursive form (2.7.3) that is standard today. The name "Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization" came into use in the 1930s. The 2013 survey by S. J. Leon, Å. Björck and W. Gander tells this history in detail.

### 2.7.4 Back to the workpiece frame

**Example 2.7.1** Building the workpiece frame {u} from the taught points.

Step one: the x axis takes the direction of $a_1$: $\|a_1\| = {{ex2_7.n1:.3f}}$ mm,

$$
x_u = q_1 = \frac{a_1}{\|a_1\|} = {{ex2_7.e1}} .
$$

Step two: subtract from $a_2$ its component along $x_u$. $q_1\cdot a_2 = {{ex2_7.r12:.3f}}$ mm,

$$
w_2 = a_2 - (q_1\cdot a_2)\,q_1 = {{ex2_7.w2}}\ \mathrm{mm}, \qquad y_u = q_2 = \frac{w_2}{\|w_2\|} = {{ex2_7.e2}} .
$$

Step three: the z axis must be perpendicular to both $x_u$ and $y_u$. In three-dimensional space there are only two such unit vectors, opposite to each other, and we want the one that makes $(x_u, y_u, z_u)$ a right-handed frame. One way is to carry on with the Gram–Schmidt process: take a roughly upward vector $a_3 = (0, 0, 1)^{\mathsf T}$, subtract its projections onto $x_u$ and $y_u$, and normalize, giving $z_u = {{ex2_7.e3}}$. The other way is to take the cross product $x_u \times y_u$ of Chapter 3; Program 2.7.1 checks that the two agree. The three axes form the matrix

$$
R_{su} = \begin{pmatrix} x_u & y_u & z_u \end{pmatrix} = {{ex2_7.Rf}} ,
$$

which satisfies $R_{su}^{\mathsf T}R_{su} = I$ and $\det R_{su} = 1$ and is a valid rotation matrix (Section 4.2). Figure 2.7.2 sketches the process.

Note that $y_u$ does not lie along the taught direction $a_2$; it has turned through {{ex2_7.dev_y:.2f}}° towards the direction perpendicular to $x_u$: the teaching error is borne entirely by the y axis, while the x axis lies exactly along $p_0 \to p_1$. This is just what the convention of the three-point method intends: $p_1$ fixes the x axis, and $p_2$ is used only to fix the plane. If $a_2$ is processed first and $a_1$ second, the y axis lies exactly along $p_0 \to p_2$ and the x axis changes by {{ex2_7.dx_alt:.2f}}°. So when teaching, the most important direction should be put on the first point, for example by making $p_0 \to p_1$ run along the edge of the workpiece that is machined most accurately.

Using $a_3 = (0, 0, 1)^{\mathsf T}$ to find the z axis has one precondition: the plane of the workpiece must not be vertical, otherwise $a_3$ lies in the xy plane and nothing is left after the projections are subtracted. The cross product has no such restriction, so controllers generally use the cross product.

### 2.7.5 The QR decomposition

Writing Eq. (2.7.3) the other way round, $a_j = \sum_{i<j}(q_i\cdot a_j)q_i + \|w_j\|q_j$: each $a_j$ is a linear combination of $q_1, \ldots, q_j$, using only the first j of them. Written in matrix form, this gives an important decomposition.

**Theorem 2.7.3 (QR decomposition)** Let the columns of an m×n matrix A (m ≥ n) be linearly independent. Then

$$
A = QR , \qquad r_{ij} = q_i\cdot a_j\ (i < j), \quad r_{jj} = \|w_j\|, \quad r_{ij} = 0\ (i > j) , \tag{2.7.4}
$$

where Q is an m×n matrix with orthonormal columns, $Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I_n$, and R is an n×n upper triangular matrix with positive diagonal entries.

The proof is the sentence above. For Example 2.7.1, in the QR decomposition of $A = (a_1, a_2)$ the two columns of Q are just $x_u$ and $y_u$, and

$$
R = {{ex2_7.Rqr}}\ \mathrm{mm} .
$$

The upper-right entry, {{ex2_7.r12:.3f}} mm, measures how far $a_2$ leans towards $a_1$; the more accurate the teaching, the closer it is to zero.

One of the main uses of the **QR decomposition** is solving least-squares problems. Substituting A = QR into the normal equations (2.5.4) gives $R^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}QR\hat x = R^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}b$, that is, $R^{\mathsf T}R\hat x = R^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}b$. R is invertible, so cancelling $R^{\mathsf T}$ gives

$$
R\,\hat x = Q^{\mathsf T}b . \tag{2.7.5}
$$

R is upper triangular, so $\hat x$ is found by back-substitution, starting from the last equation. This never forms $A^{\mathsf T}A$, and so avoids the squaring of the condition number described in Section 2.5.7. The QR method in Program 2.5.1 works this way.

### 2.7.6 Properties of orthogonal matrices

When A is square, the Q of the QR decomposition is an orthogonal matrix as defined in Section 2.4. Its properties are collected below; the rotation matrices of Chapter 4 are a special case.

**Theorem 2.7.4 (Properties of orthogonal matrices)** Let Q be an n×n matrix. The following are equivalent:

1. $Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I$ (the columns are orthonormal);
2. $QQ^{\mathsf T} = I$ (the rows are orthonormal);
3. for all x and y, $(Qx)\cdot(Qy) = x\cdot y$ (the dot product is preserved, and hence lengths and angles).

Moreover, an orthogonal matrix satisfies: $\det Q = \pm 1$; the product of two orthogonal matrices is again an orthogonal matrix; every entry of Q has absolute value at most 1.

*Proof*　(1)⇒(2): by Theorem 2.1.4, $(\det Q)^2 = \det(Q^{\mathsf T}Q) = 1$, so Q is invertible. Multiplying $Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I$ on the right by $Q^{-1}$ gives $Q^{\mathsf T} = Q^{-1}$, and so $QQ^{\mathsf T} = QQ^{-1} = I$. (2)⇒(1) in the same way. (1)⇒(3): $(Qx)^{\mathsf T}(Qy) = x^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}Qy = x^{\mathsf T}y$. (3)⇒(1): taking $x = e_i$, $y = e_j$ gives $q_i\cdot q_j = e_i\cdot e_j$. Determinant: $1 = \det(Q^{\mathsf T}Q) = (\det Q)^2$. Product: $(Q_1Q_2)^{\mathsf T}Q_1Q_2 = Q_2^{\mathsf T}Q_1^{\mathsf T}Q_1Q_2 = I$. Entries: every column is a unit vector, so the squares of its components add up to 1, and each component has absolute value at most 1.　∎

$$
(Qx)\cdot(Qy) = x\cdot y , \qquad \det Q = \pm 1 . \tag{2.7.6}
$$

An orthogonal matrix with determinant +1 is a rotation; one with determinant −1 contains a reflection. In the three-point method, the third axis is taken as $x_u\times y_u$ and not its opposite precisely to make sure that $\det R_{su} = +1$. The last two properties are used in Section 4.8 to prove that "the singular value decomposition gives the nearest rotation matrix".

### 2.7.7 Re-orthonormalizing a sensor orientation matrix

**Example 2.7.2** The mounting orientation matrix given by the calibration of a sensor carries errors:

$$
M = {{ex2_7.M}} ,
$$

the largest deviation of $M^{\mathsf T}M$ from I is {{ex2_7.orth_err_M:.4f}}, and it is not a rotation matrix. Applying Gram–Schmidt orthonormalization to the three columns of M in turn gives

$$
R_{\mathrm{GS}} = {{ex2_7.G}} .
$$

$R_{\mathrm{GS}}$ is a rotation matrix. Its first column has exactly the same direction as the first column of M; the direction of the second column has changed by {{ex2_7.turn2:.2f}}°, and that of the third by {{ex2_7.turn3:.2f}}°. All the error has been pushed onto the last two columns, although the three columns of M were "equal" to begin with and there is no reason to favour the first. $\|R_{\mathrm{GS}} - M\|_F = {{ex2_7.dist_G:.4f}}$. Section 4.8 treats the same matrix; there, the singular value decomposition gives the rotation matrix nearest to M among all rotation matrices, treating the three columns alike, and the distance is smaller too. For the small corrections made in every cycle of a real-time controller, the Gram–Schmidt process is still widely used because it needs so little computation.

### 2.7.8 Numerical stability: the modified Gram–Schmidt process

Eq. (2.7.3) is perfectly sound in exact arithmetic, but it has a hidden danger in floating-point arithmetic. When $a_j$ is almost linearly dependent on the preceding vectors, $w_j$ is the difference of two almost equal vectors, and many significant digits cancel; and in Eq. (2.7.3) all the projection coefficients $q_i\cdot a_j$ are computed with the original $a_j$, so the errors produced in earlier steps are not "seen" or corrected by later steps, and the resulting $q_j$ are no longer mutually orthogonal.

The remedy is simple: after each projection is subtracted, use the updated vector to compute the next projection. That is, in the loop over j, set $w \leftarrow a_j$, and then for i = 1, …, j − 1 in turn do

$$
w \leftarrow w - (q_i\cdot w)\,q_i , \tag{2.7.7}
$$

and finally $q_j = w/\|w\|$. In exact arithmetic this is identical to Eq. (2.7.3), because $q_i\cdot w$ and $q_i\cdot a_j$ differ only by the parts already subtracted, which are perpendicular to $q_i$. This is called the **modified Gram–Schmidt process**. J. R. Rice showed its superiority by numerical experiments in 1966, and Björck gave a rigorous error analysis in 1967.

**Example 2.7.3** Take three almost linearly dependent vectors, namely the three columns of the matrix

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ \varepsilon & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \varepsilon & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \varepsilon \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \varepsilon = 10^{-8}.
$$

With the classical Gram–Schmidt process, the largest deviation of $Q^{\mathsf T}Q$ from I for the resulting Q is {{ex2_7.loss_c:.2f}}, and the second and third columns are nearly at 60° instead of 90°; with the modified method the deviation is ${{ex2_7.loss_m:sci1}}$; with the QR decomposition of numerical libraries, based on the reflections proposed by A. S. Householder in 1958, the deviation is ${{ex2_7.loss_h:sci1}}$, which is machine precision. So when there are only two or three vectors and they are clearly not collinear (as in three-point teaching), the classical method is entirely adequate; when there are many vectors, or they may be nearly dependent, use the modified method, or call the QR decomposition of a numerical library directly.

### 2.7.9 An everyday example

A carpenter marking out a board wants to draw a line perpendicular to the edge of the board. He does not "average" the two edges; he holds a try square against the edge of the board and draws along the other arm of the square: the edge of the board is the first axis and is kept exactly; the second line is forced by the square to be perpendicular to it. This is exactly what the Gram–Schmidt process does: first settle on one direction, then remove from the second direction its part along the first. If the edge of the board is itself not straight, the "right angle" drawn is right only relative to that edge, and the error is borne by the second line; this is just the asymmetry revealed by Example 2.7.2.

Starting from "a list of numbers", this chapter has built up vectors, matrices and their operations, seen the geometric meaning of a matrix as a linear transformation, and assembled the tools of eigenvalues, singular values, least squares, null spaces and orthonormalization. On this basis Chapter 3 will distinguish between "a vector itself" and "its components in a particular frame", in preparation for describing the rotation of rigid bodies.

**Engineer's notes**

- In three-point teaching, the three points should be as far apart as possible (several hundred millimetres), and the two directions as close to perpendicular as possible. If the points are too close together, the same pointing error causes a much larger angular error.
- After orthonormalization, check two things: the deviation of $R^{\mathsf T}R$ from I (it should be of the order of $10^{-12}$), and whether $\det R$ is +1. A determinant of −1 means that one axis has been reversed and the frame has become left-handed; this is a common and dangerous mistake on the shop floor.
- A rotation matrix that a controller has built up by repeated multiplication only needs correcting with the Gram–Schmidt process once every so many cycles, at a cost of a few dozen multiplications; when an optimal correction is needed (for example in post-processing calibration results), use the singular value decomposition method of Section 4.8.
- When writing your own orthonormalization routine, use the modified Gram–Schmidt process, or call the `qr` of a numerical library directly; note that the R returned by a numerical library may have negative diagonal entries, in which case the corresponding columns of Q must have their signs changed too.
