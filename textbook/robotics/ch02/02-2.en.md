### 2.2.1 The problem: one stiffness, two matrices

In assembly there is always some position error between a part and its hole. So that the part can "find its own way" and slide into the hole, a compliant wrist is often mounted at the end of the robot arm: it acts like a set of springs and gives way a little when pushed sideways. The remote centre compliance (RCC) device is the typical example; it was developed for precision assembly at the Charles Stark Draper Laboratory in the United States in the mid-to-late 1970s, and Paul C. Watson applied for a patent on it in 1976.

Suppose a compliant wrist has two special directions in the plane: it is "stiffest" along its own $x_b$ axis, producing a restoring force of 2,000 N for each metre of offset, and "softest" along its $y_b$ axis, producing only 500 N for each metre of offset. In the wrist's own frame {b}, the relation between the offset $\delta_b$ and the restoring force $f_b$ is written

$$
f_b = K_b\,\delta_b , \qquad K_b = \begin{pmatrix} 2000 & 0 \\ 0 & 500 \end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{N/m} .
$$

Here the restoring force is taken as positive when it points the same way as the offset (the actual force points the opposite way; the difference is a minus sign, which does not affect the discussion below). When the wrist is mounted on the robot arm, {b} is turned through 30° relative to the base frame {s}. The robot program computes forces and displacements in {s}, and needs the matrix $K_s$ of the same stiffness in {s}. How is it related to $K_b$? The entries of the two matrices are obviously different; what, then, do they have in common that lets us recognize at a glance that "this is the same wrist"?

To answer this question we must first regard a matrix as a geometric operation, that is, a linear transformation, and then study how the matrix representing one linear transformation changes when the frame changes. The answer is the similarity transformation, under which the trace, the determinant and the eigenvalues are unchanged. Chapter 4 uses this result to prove that the trace of a rotation matrix gives the angle of rotation.

### 2.2.2 Linear transformations

A stiffness matrix turns an "offset" into a "force", a Jacobian matrix turns "joint velocities" into "the velocity of the end-effector", and a rotation matrix turns "the position before the turn" into "the position after the turn". They have something in common: doubling the input doubles the output, and adding two inputs adds the outputs. Hooke's law for springs and the superposition of velocities for small turns both have this property.

**Definition 2.2.1 (Linear transformation)** A rule T that sends vectors of $\mathbb R^n$ to vectors of $\mathbb R^m$ is called a **linear transformation** if, for any vectors x, y and any scalars α, β,

$$
T(\alpha x + \beta y) = \alpha\,T(x) + \beta\,T(y) . \tag{2.2.1}
$$

The transformation that sends every vector to itself is called the **identity transformation**.

A linear transformation turns a linear combination into the linear combination of the images with the same coefficients. This gives a result that looks simple but is crucial: once we know how T acts on a few vectors, we know how it acts on every vector.

**Theorem 2.2.1 (The matrix of a linear transformation)** Let $e_j$ be the vector whose j-th component is 1 and all others 0. For any linear transformation T from $\mathbb R^n$ to $\mathbb R^m$, let

$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} T(e_1) & T(e_2) & \cdots & T(e_n) \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \text{then} \qquad T(x) = A x \quad \text{for every } x . \tag{2.2.2}
$$

Conversely, every m×n matrix A defines a linear transformation by x ↦ Ax. Moreover, doing the transformation of B first and then that of A corresponds, taken together, to the matrix AB.

*Proof*　Any x can be written $x = x_1 e_1 + \cdots + x_n e_n$. By Eq. (2.2.1), $T(x) = x_1 T(e_1) + \cdots + x_n T(e_n)$, which by Eq. (2.1.8) is exactly Ax. Conversely, by the distributive law of matrix multiplication, $A(\alpha x + \beta y) = \alpha Ax + \beta Ay$. The last sentence is just the definition of matrix multiplication in Section 2.1.4.　∎

Theorem 2.2.1 gives a way of reading a matrix: **Column j of a matrix is what the j-th basis vector $e_j$ looks like after the transformation.** When Chapter 4 explains the columns of a rotation matrix as "the turned coordinate axes", this is what it means.

Some basic linear transformations of the plane and their matrices are

$$
R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \quad
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & k \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
\begin{pmatrix} s_1 & 0 \\ 0 & s_2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \tag{2.2.3}
$$

namely an anticlockwise rotation through θ; a **shear** in the x direction (each point moves in the x direction by k times its y coordinate); a scaling by factors $s_1$ and $s_2$ along the two coordinate axes; and a reflection in the x axis. Check each of them with Theorem 2.2.1: for example, the shear leaves $e_1 = (1, 0)^{\mathsf T}$ where it is and pushes $e_2 = (0, 1)^{\mathsf T}$ to $(k, 1)^{\mathsf T}$, and these two images are exactly the two columns of the matrix. Figure 2.2.1 shows what these transformations do to a square grid and to the unit square.

### 2.2.3 The geometric meaning of the determinant

The unit square has sides $e_1$ and $e_2$; after the transformation it becomes the parallelogram whose sides are the two columns of A. What is its area?

**Theorem 2.2.2** The area of the parallelogram whose sides are the two columns $a = (a_1, a_2)^{\mathsf T}$ and $b = (b_1, b_2)^{\mathsf T}$ of a 2×2 matrix A equals $|\det A|$. Hence the linear transformation x ↦ Ax magnifies the area of every figure in the plane by the factor $|\det A|$. The sign of det A tells whether the orientation has been reversed: when it is positive, turning anticlockwise from a to b takes no more than 180°; when it is negative, the figure has been flipped into its mirror image.

*Proof*　Let the angle between a and b be α; the area of the parallelogram is $S = \|a\|\,\|b\|\sin\alpha$. Since $a \cdot b = \|a\|\,\|b\|\cos\alpha$,

$$
S^2 = \|a\|^2\|b\|^2 - (a\cdot b)^2 = (a_1^2 + a_2^2)(b_1^2 + b_2^2) - (a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2)^2 = (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)^2 = (\det A)^2 . \tag{2.2.4}
$$

The identity in the middle can be checked by expanding; it is called Lagrange's identity. Any figure can be approximated by many small squares, each of which becomes a small parallelogram whose area is magnified by |det A|, so the same holds for the whole figure. As for the sign: let the angles that a and b make with the positive x axis (anticlockwise positive) be α and β; then $a = \|a\|(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)^{\mathsf T}$, $b = \|b\|(\cos\beta, \sin\beta)^{\mathsf T}$ and

$$
\det A = a_1b_2 - a_2b_1 = \|a\|\,\|b\|\,(\cos\alpha\sin\beta - \sin\alpha\cos\beta) = \|a\|\,\|b\|\sin(\beta - \alpha) .
$$

So det A > 0 if and only if the anticlockwise angle from a to b lies between 0 and 180°, the same sense of turning as from $e_1$ to $e_2$; when det A < 0 the sense is reversed and the figure has been flipped into its mirror image.　∎

In space the same reasoning shows that the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the three columns of a 3×3 matrix equals the absolute value of its determinant (Chapter 3 proves this with the scalar triple product). With this, every property in Theorem 2.1.4 has an intuitive explanation. The determinant of a product is the product of the determinants because, for two deformations in succession, the factors by which area is magnified multiply. The determinant is zero when the plane is squashed into a line or a point: information is lost, the transformation cannot be undone, and so the matrix is not invertible. In Figure 2.2.1, the rotation and the shear preserve area, det = 1; the scaling by 1.5 and 0.5 multiplies area by {{ex2_2.det_scale:.2f}}; the reflection has determinant −1, leaving area unchanged but reversing orientation.

Look back at the 2R arm of Section 2.1: det J is the area that the unit square in the plane of joint velocities has after it is carried into the plane of end-effector velocities. When the arm is straight, det J = 0 and the square is squashed into a line segment; this is the geometric meaning of "the end-effector can move in only one direction".

### 2.2.4 Bases and coordinates

So far, the components of vectors have always been written relative to the "standard" set of directions $e_1, \ldots, e_n$. But the standard directions are not sacred. The wrist's own $x_b$ and $y_b$ axes are more natural directions for the compliant wrist; on a tray placed at an angle (turned on the table), the directions along the two edges of the tray are more natural.

**Definition 2.2.2 (Basis and coordinates)** If n vectors $p_1, \ldots, p_n$ of $\mathbb R^n$ are such that every vector x can be written in exactly one way as a linear combination of them,

$$
x = x'_1 p_1 + x'_2 p_2 + \cdots + x'_n p_n = P x' , \qquad P = \begin{pmatrix} p_1 & \cdots & p_n \end{pmatrix}, \tag{2.2.5}
$$

then $p_1, \ldots, p_n$ are called a **basis** of $\mathbb R^n$, and $x' = (x'_1, \ldots, x'_n)^{\mathsf T}$ are called the coordinates of x in this basis.

"Can be written in exactly one way" is the same as saying that the equation Px′ = x has exactly one solution for every x, that is, that P is invertible. So n vectors form a basis if and only if the matrix with them as columns is invertible, that is, has a non-zero determinant. Section 2.6 returns to this concept from the point of view of "linear independence". Eq. (2.2.5) also tells us how to convert between the two sets of coordinates:

$$
x = P x' , \qquad x' = P^{-1} x .
$$

In the notation of Chapter 3 and the notation conventions, if the new basis is made of the components in {s} of the two axes of frame {b}, then P is $R_{sb}$, and $x = P x'$ is $p_s = R_{sb}\,p_b$. Going from one basis to another is called a **change of basis**.

### 2.2.5 How the matrix changes with the basis: similarity transformations

We can now answer the question at the start of this section. Let the matrix of a linear transformation T in the standard basis be A, that is, y = Ax. After changing to a basis $p_1, \ldots, p_n$, the coordinates of x and y in the new basis are x′ = P⁻¹x and y′ = P⁻¹y. Then

$$
y' = P^{-1} y = P^{-1} A x = P^{-1} A P\, x' .
$$

**Theorem 2.2.3 (Change-of-basis formula)** If a linear transformation has matrix A in the standard basis, its matrix in the coordinates whose basis is the columns of P is

$$
A' = P^{-1} A P . \tag{2.2.6}
$$

If the new basis is given by the axes of a frame {b}, then $P = R_{sb}$ satisfies $R_{sb}^{-1} = R_{sb}^{\mathsf T}$ (Section 4.1), and

$$
A_b = R_{sb}^{\mathsf T}\, A_s\, R_{sb} , \qquad A_s = R_{sb}\, A_b\, R_{sb}^{\mathsf T} . \tag{2.2.7}
$$

Eq. (2.2.6) is read from right to left: first P turns new coordinates into old ones, then A does the transformation, and finally P⁻¹ turns the result back into new coordinates. Chapter 3 calls Eq. (2.2.7) the transformation law of a second-order tensor.

**Definition 2.2.3 (Similar matrices)** For n×n matrices A and B, if there is an invertible matrix P with $B = P^{-1}AP$, A and B are said to be similar, and are **similar matrices** of each other; the operation that takes A to $P^{-1}AP$ is called a **similarity transformation**.

Two similar matrices are two ways of "writing" the same linear transformation in two different bases. Their entries may be very different, but they describe the same geometric operation. Quantities that reflect the geometric operation itself, and not the choice of basis, should be unchanged by similarity transformations.

**Theorem 2.2.4 (Similarity invariants)** If $B = P^{-1}AP$, then

1. $\det B = \det A$;
2. $\mathrm{tr}\,B = \mathrm{tr}\,A$;
3. A and B have the same characteristic polynomial, and hence the same eigenvalues, that is,

$$
\det(\lambda I - B) = \det(\lambda I - A) \quad \text{for every } \lambda . \tag{2.2.8}
$$

*Proof*　(1) By Theorem 2.1.4, $\det B = \det P^{-1}\det A\det P = \det A$, since $\det P^{-1}\det P = \det(P^{-1}P) = 1$. (2) This is item (3) of Theorem 2.1.6. (3) Note that $\lambda I = P^{-1}(\lambda I)P$, so $\lambda I - B = P^{-1}(\lambda I - A)P$, and (1) gives the result.　∎

$\det(\lambda I - A)$ in Eq. (2.2.8) is a polynomial of degree n in λ, called the **characteristic polynomial** of A, and its roots are the eigenvalues discussed in Section 2.3. So what Theorem 2.2.4 says is this: the determinant (the factor by which area is magnified), the trace and the eigenvalues do not change with the frame. They are properties of the linear transformation itself.

**Example 2.2.1** Find the stiffness matrix of the compliant wrist in the base frame {s}, and the restoring force when the wrist is pushed 1 mm in the $x_s$ direction.

{b} is turned through 30° relative to {s}:

$$
R_{sb} = {{ex2_2.R}} .
$$

By Eq. (2.2.7),

$$
K_s = R_{sb} K_b R_{sb}^{\mathsf T} = {{ex2_2.Ks}}\ \mathrm{N/m} . \tag{2.2.9}
$$

$K_s$ is no longer a diagonal matrix: the off-diagonal entries say that a push along $x_s$ produces a force in the $y_s$ direction. Pushing δ = 1 mm along $x_s$, that is, $\delta_s = (0.001, 0)^{\mathsf T}$ m,

$$
f_s = K_s \delta_s = {{ex2_2.f_s}}\ \mathrm{N} ;
$$

the force is {{ex2_2.f_ang:.1f}}° off the direction of the push, leaning towards the stiffer $x_b$ axis (Figure 2.2.2). Computing again in {b}: $\delta_b = R_{sb}^{\mathsf T}\delta_s = {{ex2_2.delta_b}}$ mm and $f_b = K_b\delta_b = {{ex2_2.f_b}}$ N; converting back to {s} with $R_{sb}$ gives the same $f_s$.

The entries of the two matrices are all different, but $\mathrm{tr}\,K_s = {{ex2_2.trK:.0f}}$ N/m $= 2000 + 500$ and $\det K_s = {{ex2_2.detK:.0f}}$ N²/m² $= 2000 \times 500$, exactly the same as for $K_b$. The start of this section asked "how can we recognize at a glance that this is the same wrist?"; the trace and the determinant are part of the answer, and Section 2.3 will show that the full answer is the eigenvalues.

**Example 2.2.2** A change of basis need not be to mutually perpendicular axes. On a tray placed at an angle (turned on the table), take basis vectors along the two edges of the tray, $p_1 = (1, 0)^{\mathsf T}$ and $p_2 = (0.5, 1)^{\mathsf T}$, which are not perpendicular. By Eq. (2.2.6), the matrix of the same stiffness in this basis is

$$
K' = P^{-1} K_s P = {{ex2_2.Kp}} ,
$$

which has lost even its symmetry. Yet $\mathrm{tr}\,K' = {{ex2_2.trKp:.0f}}$, $\det K' = {{ex2_2.detKp:.0f}}$, and the characteristic polynomial is also the same as that of $K_s$. Symmetry depends on whether the basis vectors are mutually perpendicular and of length 1, whereas the trace, the determinant and the eigenvalues do not depend on any choice of basis. ### 2.2.6 An everyday example

In the evening, with the sun low, the glazing bars of a floor-length window cast a shadow on the floor. The bars form a grid of squares, and the shadow is still a grid, only "pushed askew": the bottom edge of the window frame, which touches the floor, is its own shadow; the vertical bars cast shadows lying on the floor in the direction of the sunlight. Take a point on the bottom edge of the frame as the origin and send each point of the window to its shadow on the floor; this is a linear transformation, the shear of Eq. (2.2.3) combined with a scaling. Recording it in different Cartesian frames, for example one along the window frame and one along the joints between the floor tiles, gives different matrices; but the factor by which the shadow magnifies area, that is, the absolute value of the determinant, does not change with the choice of frame.

The next section answers the question this section leaves open: among all possible frames, which one makes the stiffness matrix simplest? The answer is the frame made of eigenvectors.

**Engineer's notes**

- To read a transformation matrix, read its columns first: column j is the image of the j-th basis vector. Faced with an unfamiliar calibration matrix or stiffness matrix, this is the quickest way to understand it.
- To convert "matrix-type" quantities such as stiffness, inertia or covariance between two frames, use Eq. (2.2.7), multiplying by a rotation matrix once on each side; to convert "vector-type" quantities such as force or velocity, multiply only once. Leaving out a factor, or multiplying on the wrong side, is one of the most common mistakes in the field.
- The easiest way to check that a conversion is correct is to compare the traces and the determinants: they should be exactly the same before and after the conversion (within numerical error).
- For the stiffness or compliance matrices given in a product manual, always confirm in which frame, and about which reference point, they are written; for the same wrist, a different reference point gives a different matrix (Chapter 31).
