### 5.5.1 The problem

At the loading station of the speed-reducer workshop, gear blanks arrive on a conveyor and drop into a tray, with a different position and angle every time: the position varies by a few centimetres and the angle is arbitrary. A fixed camera above the worktable takes an image of the tray, and the vision software recognizes the gear blank and reports its pose relative to the camera, $T_{co}$ (methods of pose estimation are covered in Chapter 54). The UR5e is to reach down from directly above with its gripper, grip the gear blank by its outer cylindrical surface and carry it into the inspection fixture.

What the robot controller needs is a target written in its own base frame: the pose $T_{sb}$ that the flange should reach, from which inverse kinematics (Chapters 14 and 15) computes the six joint angles. Between one reading of the camera and one target for the controller lie several frames, those of the camera, the worktable, the base, the flange, the gripper and the workpiece, and each step has its own data source and its own error. This section brings together the tools of the previous four sections to solve this problem completely, and answers a question of equal engineering importance: if the camera is mounted slightly askew, by how much will the gripper miss?

### 5.5.2 The poses in the chain of frames

Figure 5.1.1 and Table 5.1.1 have already given the frames of the workstation. The grasping task needs one more frame: the grasp frame {g}, fixed to the workpiece, which states "where the tool frame should be when the gripper grasps". The gear blank is 30 mm thick and the gripper grips it from above at half its thickness, so the origin of {g} lies on the z axis of {o}, 15 mm above the bottom face; the gripper approaches the workpiece from above, so $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_g$ points vertically downwards; we take $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_g = \hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_o$, and then $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_g = -\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_o$:

$$
T_{og} = {{ex5_5.Tog}} .
$$

Table 5.5.1 lists all the poses in the chain of frames and where they come from. They are exactly the edges of the frame tree of Figure 5.4.1: the first two are fixed edges, $T_{co}$ is an edge obtained by measurement, $T_{sb}$ is determined by the joint variables, and $T_{og}$ and $T_{bt}$ are design data.

:::

| Pose | Meaning | Data source | When updated |
|---|---|---|---|
| $T_{ws}$ | Robot base relative to the worktable | Installation drawing, or measured by touching datum points on the table top with the tool tip | When the robot is reinstalled |
| $T_{wc}$ | Camera relative to the worktable | Camera calibration (Chapters 50 and 54) | When the camera is knocked or reinstalled |
| $T_{co}$ | Workpiece relative to the camera | Visual pose estimation (Chapter 54) | With every image taken |
| $T_{og}$ | Grasp point relative to the workpiece | Grasp design, fixed from the workpiece drawing | When the workpiece type changes |
| $T_{bt}$ | Tool relative to the flange | Gripper drawing or tool centre point calibration (Section 2.5) | When the gripper is changed |
| $T_{sb}(\theta)$ | Flange relative to the base | Computed from encoder readings by forward kinematics (Chapter 12) | At all times |

### 5.5.3 The closure equation

The grasp succeeds when the tool frame {t} of the gripper coincides with the grasp frame {g}, that is, $T_{gt} = I_4$. There are then two ways to get from the worktable to the tool (Figure 5.5.1): one through the robot, $\{w\} \to \{s\} \to \{b\} \to \{t\}$; the other through the camera and the workpiece, $\{w\} \to \{c\} \to \{o\} \to \{g\} = \{t\}$. Both paths describe the same pose $T_{wt}$, so

$$
T_{ws}\,T_{sb}(\theta)\,T_{bt} = T_{wc}\,T_{co}\,T_{og} . \tag{5.5.1}
$$

Eq. (5.5.1) is called the closure equation of this task; it is an instance of the loop condition (5.4.3) of Section 5.4.3. Only $T_{sb}$ is unknown in it. We solve for it with the subscript cancellation rule: premultiplying both sides by $T_{ws}^{-1} = T_{sw}$ and postmultiplying by $T_{bt}^{-1} = T_{tb}$ gives

$$
T_{sb}^{*} = T_{sw}\,T_{wc}\,T_{co}\,T_{og}\,T_{tb} . \tag{5.5.2}
$$

The subscripts on the right are, in order, sw, wc, co, og, tb: the adjacent subscripts of the first four all cancel, leaving sg; the last pair, g and t, do not match, but at the grasp {g} and {t} coincide and $T_{gt} = I_4$, so this factor can be inserted between them, and the meaning of the whole product is then $T_{sb}$. Inserting $T_{gt}$ is not a formal game: if the gripper is first to stop 50 mm directly above the workpiece (the pre-grasp pose) and then descend along the approach direction, we need only replace $T_{gt}$ by $\mathrm{Trans}(0,\ 0,\ -0.05)$, leaving the other factors unchanged.

**Example 5.5.1** The pose estimate of the camera gives

$$
T_{co} = {{ex5_5.Tco}} .
$$

Find the pose that the flange should reach when the arm grasps.

So that the result can be checked, this camera reading was produced in the virtual workstation: the gear blank sits in the tray with the centre of its bottom face at $(0.80,\ 0.45,\ 0.02)$ m, turned through 25° about the vertical axis, and the camera reading was computed from this known position and the pose of the camera, so that it stands for an error-free measurement. Below we pretend not to know this position and solve using the camera reading alone.

First we read $T_{co}$: the fourth column says that the centre of the bottom face of the gear blank lies in front of the camera, {{ex5_5.dist_co:.4f}} m from the optical centre, with a positive x component and a negative y component, so it is slightly to the upper right of the image; the third column, $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_o$, has a z component close to −1 in the camera frame, which says that the axis of the workpiece points roughly towards the camera.

Step 1: transfer the workpiece to the worktable frame, $T_{wo} = T_{wc}\,T_{co}$, with $T_{wc}$ from Example 5.2.1:

$$
T_{wo} = {{ex5_5.Two}} .
$$

The fourth column is exactly the position in the tray, (0.80, 0.45, 0.02) m; the upper-left block is the rotation matrix of a turn through 25° about the z axis (cos 25° and sin 25° appear where they should). This step already agrees with the known position in the virtual workstation.

Step 2: transfer to the base frame and add the grasp offset, $T_{sg} = T_{sw}\,T_{wo}\,T_{og}$, where $T_{sw} = T_{ws}^{-1}$ is found from Eq. (5.3.6):

$$
T_{sw} = {{ex5_5.Tsw}} , \qquad
T_{sg} = {{ex5_5.Tsg}} .
$$

In {s} the grasp point lies at ${{ex5_5.psg}}$ m; compared with the coordinates of the centre of the gear blank in Example 5.1.1, it is merely 15 mm higher. The third column is $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_g = (0, 0, -1)$, pointing vertically downwards.

Step 3: remove the tool, $T_{sb}^{*} = T_{sg}\,T_{tb}$:

$$
T_{sb}^{*} = {{ex5_5.Tsb_goal}} .
$$

The centre of the flange is at ${{ex5_5.psb}}$ m, 0.15 m above the grasp point, which is exactly the length of the gripper; the second column is the flange normal $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_b = (0, 0, -1)$, pointing downwards. For the pre-grasp pose, the centre of the flange is at ${{ex5_5.psb_pre}}$ m.

Program 5.5.1 obtains the same $T_{sg}$ in three ways: by the chain written out by hand from Eq. (5.5.2); by hanging $T_{co}$ under {c} in the frame tree and using the general path algorithm of Section 5.4; and by direct comparison with the known position of the workpiece in the virtual workstation. All three agree. It also takes a marker point on the edge of the top face of the gear blank and transfers it into {s} along each of the two paths; both give ${{ex5_5.m_s}}$ m.

Finally we check whether the robot can reach the target. Using the numerical inverse kinematics of Chapter 15, starting from an initial guess with "elbow up, wrist down", we obtain the joint angles $\theta^{*}$ = ({{ex5_5.th_deg}}). Substituting $\theta^{*}$ into the forward kinematics algorithm of the web 3D model (independent of the product-of-exponentials formula used for the inverse), the flange pose computed differs from $T_{sb}^{*}$ by ${{ex5_5.err_reach:sci1}}$, and the two sides of the closure equation (5.5.1) are equal. The arm in Lab 5.5 reaches for the gear blank with exactly these joint angles.

The UR controller displays the tool pose in the form (x, y, z, rx, ry, rz), where the last three numbers are the rotation vector $\hat\omega\theta$, i.e. the exponential coordinates of Section 4.4. Written in this form, $T_{sg}$ has the position ({{ex5_5.ur_x:.1f}}, {{ex5_5.ur_y:.1f}}, {{ex5_5.ur_z:.1f}}) mm and the rotation vector ({{ex5_5.rx:.4f}}, {{ex5_5.ry:.4f}}, {{ex5_5.rz:.4f}}) rad, whose length is π, that is, a turn through {{ex5_5.rv_deg:.0f}}°. When the angle is exactly 180°, $\hat\omega\pi$ and $-\hat\omega\pi$ represent the same rotation (Section 4.4), and the sign shown by the controller may jump between the two; to compare two poses in this case, compare the rotation matrices, not the rotation vectors.

### 5.5.4 Propagation of errors along the chain of frames

Every factor in Eq. (5.5.2) has an error. The most common in engineering practice, and the one that most deserves vigilance, is the angular error, because distance magnifies it.

Suppose the actual orientation of the camera is turned from the calibrated value by a further small angle δ about some axis $\hat\omega$ (a unit vector) of the camera's own frame. By Theorem 5.3.3 the actual pose is $\tilde T_{wc} = T_{wc}\,(\mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega, \delta),\ 0)$. The $T_{co}$ measured by the camera is relative to the actual camera, so the true position of the workpiece is given by $\tilde T_{wc}T_{co}$; the program, however, computes with the calibrated value $T_{wc}$. The difference between the two is the miss of the gripper. Writing $p_{cg}$ for the position of the grasp point in the camera frame, the position error of the grasp point is

$$
e = \big| R_{wc}\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega, \delta)\,p_{cg} - R_{wc}\,p_{cg} \big| = \big| (\mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega, \delta) - I)\,p_{cg} \big| .
$$

When δ is small, Rodrigues' formula (4.4.5) gives $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega, \delta) \approx I + \delta\,[\hat\omega]$, and so

$$
e \approx \big| \delta\,\hat\omega \times p_{cg} \big| = \delta\,\rho , \tag{5.5.3}
$$

where ρ is the distance from the grasp point to the axis (the line through the optical centre of the camera in the direction $\hat\omega$). Eq. (5.5.3) has the same form as $v = \omega \times r$ in Section 3.6: a small angular error amounts to a tiny turn about some axis, and the farther a point is from the axis, the more it is displaced. When the axis is perpendicular to the optical axis, the length of this "lever" is the distance from the camera to the workpiece (Figure 5.5.2).

**Example 5.5.2** The camera mounting angle has an error of δ = 0.5° = {{ex5_5.delta_rad:.5f}} rad about $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_c$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_c$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_c$ in turn. Find the position error of the grasp point.

The distance from the grasp point to the optical centre of the camera is {{ex5_5.lever:.4f}} m. Program 5.5.1 computes the error exactly from its definition and compares it with the first-order estimate (5.5.3); the results are listed in Table 5.5.2.

| Error axis | Exact value / mm | First-order estimate δρ / mm |

An error about $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_c$ or $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_c$ displaces the grasp point by nearly 8 mm, whereas an error of the same size about the optical axis causes a miss of only about 0.5 mm, because the grasp point lies almost on the optical axis and its distance from the optical axis is very short. When a two-finger gripper grips a gear blank of 80 mm outer diameter, the clearance of the open fingers is usually only a few millimetres, and a miss of 8 mm is enough to make the gripper crash into the workpiece. If the miss must not exceed 1 mm, the mounting angle error of the camera about the x and y axes must be less than about 1/900 rad, that is, about 0.06°. Such accuracy cannot be reached by aligning by eye during installation; only calibration can achieve it.

If the same error of 0.5° occurs in the mounting orientation of the robot base (about $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_s$), the lever is the distance from the grasp point to the $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_s$ axis, {{ex5_5.r_base:.4f}} m, and the miss is {{ex5_5.base_mm:.2f}} mm. Position errors, on the other hand, are not magnified: if the base is mounted 1 mm off on the table top, the grasp point is 1 mm off (Exercise 5.5.4).

This analysis also yields an important design principle: **the shorter the chain of frames, the fewer the sources of error.** In Eq. (5.5.2), $T_{ws}$ and $T_{wc}$ both take the worktable as their reference, so dimensional errors of the table itself enter both. If the pose $T_{sc}$ of the camera relative to the robot base is calibrated directly, the closure equation becomes $T_{sb}^{*} = T_{sc}\,T_{co}\,T_{og}\,T_{tb}$; the worktable disappears from the chain, and its errors no longer play any part. This is exactly what hand-eye calibration (Chapter 50) does.

### 5.5.5 Eye-to-hand and eye-in-hand

The camera in this section is fixed on a stand and looks at the robot and the workpiece from outside; this configuration is called **eye-to-hand**. Another common configuration mounts the camera on the robot's wrist, so that it moves together with the robot; this is called **eye-in-hand**. The camera then hangs below the flange, the edge in the frame tree is $T_{bc}$ (camera relative to the flange, fixed), and the closure equation becomes

$$
T_{sb}^{*} = T_{sb}(\theta_k)\,T_{bc}\,T_{co}\,T_{og}\,T_{tb} , \tag{5.5.4}
$$

where $\theta_k$ are the joint angles at the moment $t_k$ when the image is taken, and $T_{co}$ is the workpiece pose given by that image. The moment of the image appears in the equation, and this is precisely why Section 5.4.5 stresses timestamps: if the robot keeps moving after the image is taken, the joint angles at the moment of the image must be used. An eye-in-hand camera can take images close to the workpiece, giving high resolution, and is not blocked by the arm; an eye-to-hand camera has a fixed field of view and, once calibrated, can be used for a long time, but it may be blocked by the arm.

How is $T_{bc}$ obtained? Let the robot carry the camera and take images of the same fixed calibration board {k} from two different poses, 1 and 2. The pose of the calibration board in the base frame does not change, and each of the two images gives a chain of frames:

$$
T_{sb_1}\,X\,T_{c_1k} = T_{sb_2}\,X\,T_{c_2k} , \qquad X = T_{bc} .
$$

Premultiplying by $T_{sb_2}^{-1}$ and postmultiplying by $T_{c_1k}^{-1}$, the subscript cancellation rule gives

$$
A\,X = X\,B , \qquad A = T_{b_2b_1} = T_{sb_2}^{-1}\,T_{sb_1} , \quad B = T_{c_2c_1} = T_{c_2k}\,T_{c_1k}^{-1} , \tag{5.5.5}
$$

where A is computed from the joint angles of the two poses, B from the two images of the calibration board, and only X is unknown. This is the hand-eye calibration equation. In 1989 Y. C. Shiu and S. Ahmad, and R. Y. Tsai and R. K. Lenz, independently proposed methods for solving it; its solution, and the corresponding equation for the eye-to-hand case, are discussed in Chapter 50.

### 5.5.6 An everyday example

Many cars have a reversing camera. The coloured guide lines on the screen that show the path of the rear of the car and the distances are computed from a chain of frames: a point on the ground is transferred first into the car body frame, then into the frame of the camera mounted on the tailgate, and finally projected onto the image. The mounting pose of the camera is calibrated at the factory, with the car standing in front of a calibration pattern on the ground. If the car has been hit from behind and the tailgate repaired, and the camera is now mounted 1° askew, then at 3 m the guide lines are off by about 5 cm (3 m × π/180), and the driver reversing into a parking space may scrape the car alongside. Pitch errors matter even more: with the camera 1.0 m high, a pitch error of 1° makes the distance to a traffic cone at 3 m wrong by about 18 cm (the everyday scene of Lab 5.5). After a repair the camera must be recalibrated, for exactly the same reason as in the grasping problem of this section.

Starting from "the coordinates of the same point in two frames", this chapter has built a complete set of tools: poses, homogeneous transformations, composition and inversion, and frame trees, and has used them to solve a real problem of vision-guided grasping. Chapter 6 will study SE(3) from another angle: every rigid-body displacement is a screw motion, and a pose can be written as a matrix exponential; this is the main thread of the kinematics and dynamics of the whole book.

**Engineer's notes**

- To solve a vision-guided problem, first write down the closure equation (5.5.1), then solve for the unknown with the subscript cancellation rule, and finally check the data source and the frame conventions of each factor one by one. Do not start by multiplying matrices in code.
- In an error budget, an angular error is multiplied by the length of the lever. The farther away the camera is mounted, and the farther the workpiece is from the robot base, the higher the demand on angular accuracy.
- Calibrate directly whatever pose can be calibrated directly, to shorten the chain of frames. In an eye-to-hand system, calibrating $T_{sc}$ directly is more accurate than calibrating $T_{ws}$ and $T_{wc}$ separately.
- Before grasping, go to the pre-grasp pose first, then descend in a straight line along the approach direction of the tool (postmultiplying by a translation). Then even with a miss of a few millimetres, the gripper touches the workpiece from directly above rather than crashing into it from the side.
- After installation, verify the whole chain with an independent check loop: for example, have the tip of the gripper touch a marker point seen by the camera; a miss larger than the budget means that some edge is wrong.
- To compare poses, compare rotation matrices (or use the error angle of Section 4.8); do not compare the numbers of rotation vectors or Euler angles directly; near 180° the sign of the rotation vector jumps.
