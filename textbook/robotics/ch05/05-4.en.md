### 5.4.1 The problem

Count the frames in the workstation of Figure 5.1.1: the worktable, the robot base, the robot's base link and six links, the flange, the gripper, the camera, the tray, the gear blank and the grasp point already make fifteen or sixteen. If the shop floor also has an AGV bringing parts and a second camera, the number doubles. At any moment the control program may ask for the pose between any two of them: where, in the base frame, is the workpiece the camera sees? How far is the gripper from the edge of the tray? Where is the tote on the AGV relative to the robot base?

A naive approach is to store the pose between every two frames. Fifteen frames make 105 pairs, and as soon as one joint moves, the dozens of poses that involve the links beyond that joint all have to be updated. Worse, the same pose can be derived from different data: "camera to base" can be stored directly as one value, or obtained by multiplying "camera to worktable" and "worktable to base". Measured data always contain errors, the two routes will not give exactly the same result, and the program does not know which one to trust.

The approach of this section is that each frame records only one pose: its pose relative to the frame it "hangs" from. A robot link hangs from the previous link, the gripper hangs from the flange, the camera hangs from the worktable to which its bracket is fixed, and the workpiece the camera sees hangs from the camera. The structure organized in this way is a tree. The pose between any two frames is obtained by multiplying along the one and only path between them in the tree, by the subscript cancellation rule of Section 5.3.

### 5.4.2 Definition of the frame tree

**Definition 5.4.1 (Frame tree)** A set of frames is called a **frame tree** if:

1. exactly one of the frames has no "parent frame"; it is called the root;
2. every other frame {c} has exactly one parent frame {p}, and the pose $T_{pc}$ of {c} relative to {p} is recorded;
3. starting from any frame and going up from parent to parent, one always reaches the root.

{c} is called a child frame of {p}. A record between a parent and a child is called an edge of the tree.

Condition 3 guarantees that the tree has no loops: if there were a loop, a frame on it going up from parent to parent would go round in circles and never reach the root. In graph theory, a connected graph without cycles (an acyclic connected graph) is called a tree, and a frame tree is exactly such a structure. Figure 5.4.1 is the frame tree of the UR5e workstation; its root is the worktable frame {w}.

Every edge of the tree corresponds to a physical fact, and so has a definite source of data. By their source, the edges fall into three kinds:

- **Fixed edges**: mounting relations, for example the base relative to the worktable, the camera relative to the worktable, the gripper relative to the flange. They come from drawings or from calibration, and in general do not change with time;
- **Edges set by joint variables**: for example link i relative to link i − 1, which is a function of the joint angle $\theta_i$ and is computed at every moment from the encoder readings;
- **Measured edges**: for example the workpiece seen by the camera relative to the camera, which comes from the vision system, is updated each time an image is taken, and carries the time of the measurement.

When poses are hung on the tree according to their source, every number has exactly one origin. When a joint moves, only the corresponding edge is updated; when the workpiece is moved, only the edge from the camera to the workpiece is updated.

### 5.4.3 The pose between any two frames

**Theorem 5.4.1** In a frame tree there is exactly one path between any two frames {a} and {b} that does not pass through the same frame twice. Let this path pass in turn through $\{a\} = \{a_0\}, \{a_1\}, \ldots, \{a_n\} = \{b\}$. Then

$$
T_{ab} = T_{a_0a_1}\,T_{a_1a_2}\cdots T_{a_{n-1}a_n} , \tag{5.4.1}
$$

where, for each step that goes from a child frame to its parent (upwards), the factor $T_{a_ka_{k+1}}$ is the inverse of the pose recorded on that edge, and for each step that goes from a parent to its child (downwards), it is the recorded pose itself.

*Proof*　Going up from {a} to the root, the frames passed form a list; starting from {b} gives another list. Both lists end at the root, so they have frames in common; call the common frame that appears first in the list of {a} {m}, the **lowest common ancestor** of {a} and {b}. Going up from {a} to {m} and then down from {m} to {b} is a path that repeats no frame. If there were another, different path that repeats no frame, the two paths together would contain a loop, contradicting the definition of a tree; so the path is unique. Eq. (5.4.1) follows from Corollary 5.3.1; for an upward step, $T_{a_ka_{k+1}} = T_{a_{k+1}a_k}^{-1}$ follows from Eq. (5.3.3).　∎

Theorem 5.4.1 gives a general algorithm that a computer can apply, without distinction, to any two frames:

1. go up from {a} and from {b} to the root, and find the lowest common ancestor {m};
2. for each step from {a} to {m} multiply by the inverse of the edge, and for each step from {m} to {b} multiply by the edge itself.

Another, equivalent formulation is more convenient for programming. Let {r} be the root, and first compute the pose $T_{ra}$ of every frame relative to the root (multiplying all the way down from the root). Then

$$
T_{ab} = T_{ra}^{-1}\,T_{rb} . \tag{5.4.2}
$$

By the subscript cancellation rule, $T_{ra}^{-1}T_{rb} = T_{ar}T_{rb} = T_{ab}$, so the result is clearly correct. The two approaches give the same result. The first does fewer multiplications when the tree is deep and {a} and {b} are close together; with the second, when many pairs of frames have to be queried again and again, all the $T_{ra}$ can be computed once and stored, after which each query takes only one inversion and one multiplication.

**Why a tree and not an arbitrary network.** Loops do exist in practice. For example, a camera may see both the workpiece and a marker on the robot's flange, so "worktable–camera–flange" and "worktable–base–links–flange" are two paths, and they form a loop. The frame tree does not deny that such loops exist; it lays down that the tree records only one of the paths, and the data of the other path are not stored as an edge but used as a constraint. The product once round the loop should equal the identity matrix:

$$
T_{a_0a_1}\,T_{a_1a_2}\cdots T_{a_{k}a_0} = I_4 . \tag{5.4.3}
$$

Measured data do not satisfy Eq. (5.4.3) exactly, and the size of the deviation reflects the size of the errors; the hand-eye calibration of Chapter 50 finds the mounting pose of the camera from exactly a set of such loop equations. The links of a parallel mechanism form loops in themselves, and Chapter 18 will describe them with constraint equations rather than a tree.

### 5.4.4 The robot itself is a chain of frames

The model file of a robot is a frame tree. In robot description formats such as URDF (Chapter 34) and MJCF, each link has a frame and each joint is an edge, which records three things: the position xyz of the child link's frame relative to the parent link when the joint angle is zero, the relative orientation rpy, and the direction of the joint axis. When the joint has turned through $\theta$, the pose of this edge is

$$
T_{p,c}(\theta) = \mathrm{Trans}(xyz)\ \begin{pmatrix} R_{\mathrm{rpy}} & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\ \begin{pmatrix} \mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega, \theta) & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{5.4.4}
$$

where $R_{\mathrm{rpy}}$ is the roll–pitch–yaw rotation matrix of Eq. (4.5.2): the three numbers of rpy in the file are, in order, the roll, pitch and yaw angles, that is, rotations in turn about the fixed axes x, y and z of the parent frame, and the matrix is in the order "yaw × pitch × roll" (here we do not use the letter θ of Section 4.5 for the pitch angle, to avoid confusion with the joint angle θ); $\hat\omega$ is the direction of the joint axis in the child link's frame, and $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega, \theta)$ is given by Rodrigues' formula. For a prismatic joint, the last factor is replaced by a translation of θ along $\hat\omega$.

The UR5e is a tree without branches, that is, a chain. Table 5.4.1 lists its eight edges in the model of the parts library. The frames of the base link and of links 1 to 6 are written {0}, {1}, …, {6} in turn. Forward kinematics, finding the flange pose from the joint angles, is going down this chain from {s} to {b} and multiplying the eight edges together by Eq. (5.4.1). Alongside the product-of-exponentials formula of Chapter 12, this is another algorithm for forward kinematics; the DH parameter method of Chapter 13 also belongs to this class, except that it adds a convention for choosing each link frame, so that each edge needs only four parameters.

**Example 5.4.1** Find the flange pose of the UR5e at the joint angles $\theta_A = (0^\circ, -90^\circ, 90^\circ, -90^\circ, -90^\circ, 0^\circ)$, and check it with three algorithms.

Writing down the eight edges from Table 5.4.1 and Eq. (5.4.4) and multiplying them in turn going down from {s} gives

$$
T_{sb}(\theta_A) = {{ex5_4.TsbA}} .
$$

Reading this matrix: the centre of the flange is at $(-0.492,\ -0.134,\ 0.488)$ m, and the second column, the flange normal $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_b = (0, 0, -1)$, points vertically downwards. With the gripper fitted, its fingertips point downwards; this is the usual ready posture for grasping a workpiece on the table from above. Figure 5.4.2 shows the position and direction of every link frame along the way.

Program 5.4.1 computes the same $T_{sb}$ by three mutually independent algorithms: (1) multiplying edge by edge by Eq. (5.4.4), using the numbers in the joint table entry.json of the model; (2) multiplying the matrices of the link nodes in the web 3D model default.glb, which is the algorithm used to display the robot in the 3D labs; (3) the product-of-exponentials formula of Chapter 12. The results of (2) and (3) differ by ${{ex5_4.d23:sci1}}$, which is just floating-point rounding. Between (1) and the other two, the rotation matrix entries differ by at most ${{ex5_4.d12_R:sci1}}$, and the flange positions by ${{ex5_4.d12_p:sci1}}$ m. The reason is that the joint table records π/2 as {{ex5_4.rpy_file:.6f}}, which is off by ${{ex5_4.rpy_err:sci1}}$ rad. Two edges in the table carry this angle, and since the axes of joints 2, 3 and 4 are parallel, the two errors add up about the same direction, so the orientations differ by about twice ${{ex5_4.rpy_err:sci1}}$ rad; the position difference is the result of each angle error times the lever arm beyond it, combined according to direction. Such differences are far smaller than the pose repeatability of the UR5e, ±0.03 mm, and do not matter in use; but they remind us that the numbers in a model file are data with a precision too.

**Example 5.4.2** In the frame tree of Figure 5.4.1, with the joint angles of the UR5e at $\theta_A$, find the pose $T_{ct}$ of the tool as seen by the camera.

The path from {c} to {t} is: one step up to {w}, then down through {s}, the base link, links 1 to 6 and {b} to {t}, {{ex5_4.n_edges}} edges in all: {{ex5_4.n_up}} upward and {{ex5_4.n_down}} downward. By Theorem 5.4.1,

$$
T_{ct} = T_{wc}^{-1}\,T_{ws}\,T_{s0}\,T_{01}\cdots T_{56}\,T_{6b}\,T_{bt} = {{ex5_4.Tct}} .
$$

The tool centre point is at ${{ex5_4.pct}}$ m in the camera frame, {{ex5_4.dist_ct:.4f}} m from the optical centre. The z component in the camera frame is positive, which says that the gripper is in front of the camera; the ratios of the x and y components to the z component are about 0.2, which says that it is near the centre of the field of view and can be seen by the camera. Program 5.4.1 computes this in two ways, by the general tree path algorithm and by the hand-written chain of frames $T_{wc}^{-1}T_{ws}T_{sb}T_{bt}$ (with $T_{sb}$ from the product-of-exponentials formula), and the results agree. It also makes two checks: the product once round the loop {w} → {c} → {t} → {w}, $T_{wc}T_{ct}T_{tw}$, equals the identity matrix; and after joint 1 is turned to 30°, $T_{wc}$ and $T_{ws}$ are unchanged while $T_{ct}$ changes. The latter shows that the whole subtree below the edge of joint 1 moves with it, while the rest of the tree is unaffected.

### 5.4.5 The frame tree in software

The frame tree is part of the infrastructure of robot software. The tf library of the Robot Operating System ROS (Tully Foote, published 2013) is organized in just the way described in this section: for each frame, a publisher regularly reports its pose relative to its parent frame, tf maintains a tree, and any program can query the pose between any two frames at any time. The corresponding library in ROS 2 is called tf2, and Chapter 52 will describe it in detail. Mobile robots usually use the chain map → odom → base_link (the ROS convention REP 105): base_link is the frame of the vehicle body, odom is given by odometry and map by the localization algorithm, and this division of labour corresponds exactly to the different sources of the three kinds of edges above.

tf records a timestamp for every edge, which is particularly important for measured edges. Suppose the camera takes an image at time $t_1$ and the vision algorithm gives the workpiece pose only at $t_1 + 0.2$ s. If the arm is still moving during those 0.2 s, converting the workpiece pose into the base frame must use the joint angles at time $t_1$, not the current ones. For this purpose tf keeps the history of every edge over the most recent stretch of time and interpolates in time when queried.

### 5.4.6 Everyday examples

The turntable (lazy Susan) on a dining table is a small frame tree: the table stands fixed on the floor, the turntable hangs from the table, and the dishes hang from the turntable. Turning the turntable changes only one edge, "turntable relative to table", and all the dishes turn with it; not one of their positions relative to the turntable needs changing. To ask "how far is the fish from me now", go up from "me" to the table, then down through the turntable to the plate with the fish, and multiply the poses along the way. A home address is a tree as well: country, province, city, street, house number, each level saying only where it is within the level above. A parcel is delivered by looking things up level by level down the tree, and nobody ever needs a big table of "the distance between any two households".

We now have all the tools for describing and computing the pose between any frames. The next section applies them to a complete problem: starting from the camera's reading, compute the pose the arm should reach to grasp a workpiece, and analyse how errors propagate along the chain of frames.

**Engineer's notes**

- Drawing a frame tree for a workstation, with the data source and update rate of every edge marked, is the most effective first step in debugging vision-guided or multi-robot programs. Many "the robot picks off-centre" problems can be traced, once the tree is drawn, to one edge whose data are wrong or out of date.
- Each frame can have only one parent frame. If two modules both publish a parent edge for the same frame, software such as tf will report an error or behave strangely; this is a common configuration error in ROS systems.
- Measured edges must carry timestamps. While the robot moves, the time of an image and the time of the joint angles differ by tens of milliseconds, which at an end-effector speed of 0.5 m/s amounts to an error of 10–20 mm.
- Do not throw away the loop data in a tree; they are the basis for checking and calibration. Computing the loop error with Eq. (5.4.3) at regular intervals is a simple way to find out whether a camera or a fixture has been knocked.
- The link frames in a robot model file are chosen by whoever made the model, and are not necessarily the same as the frames in the controller or in the manufacturer's DH parameters. Before using a model for simulation, first check the end-effector pose of the model against the real robot at a few joint angles (the method of Section 12.3 in Chapter 12).
