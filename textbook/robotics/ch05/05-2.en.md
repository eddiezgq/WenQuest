### 5.2.1 The problem

In the workstation of Figure 5.1.1, the camera measures the coordinates $p_c$ of a point on the gear blank in the camera frame {c}, and these must be converted to the robot base frame {s}. What we know is the pose of the camera relative to the worktable, $(R_{wc},\ p_{wc})$, and the pose of the base relative to the worktable, $(R_{ws},\ p_{ws})$. Using Theorem 5.1.1 in two steps, we first obtain $p_w = R_{wc}\,p_c + p_{wc}$ from Eq. (5.1.2), and then from Eq. (5.1.3)

$$
p_s = R_{ws}^{\mathsf T}\big(R_{wc}\,p_c + p_{wc} - p_{ws}\big) .
$$

Two conversions already call for an expression with brackets. In a robot controller, the way from the camera to the fingertips of the gripper passes through a dozen or so frames, the camera, the worktable, the base, six links, the flange and the tool, and each step involves one rotation and one translation. Nesting the steps like this makes the expression long-winded, and the order of the rotations and translations is also easy to get wrong.

Recall the case in which the origins coincide: two conversions are simply the product of two rotation matrices, $R_{ac} = R_{ab}R_{bc}$ (Eq. (3.3.6)), and any number of conversions is a chain of matrix products whose order is fixed naturally by the subscripts. We would like conversions with translations to work the same way. The difficulty is that Eq. (5.1.2) is an affine transformation, not a linear one, and cannot be represented by a 3×3 matrix. In this section we shall see that the problem is solved at once simply by giving every point an extra component equal to 1.

### 5.2.2 Homogeneous coordinates

Rewrite Eq. (5.1.2) in the form of block matrices:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} p_a \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} =
\begin{pmatrix} R_{ab} & p_{ab} \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} p_b \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{5.2.1}
$$

Check it block by block using the multiplication of block matrices: the first block row gives $R_{ab}\,p_b + p_{ab} \cdot 1$, which is exactly the right-hand side of Eq. (5.1.2); the second block row gives $0 \cdot p_b + 1 \cdot 1 = 1$, so the two sides agree. The 0 here is the 1×3 zero row vector. The added 1 serves to "carry" the translation: it is precisely by being multiplied by this 1 that the fourth column of the matrix, $p_{ab}$, gets added to the result. In this way an affine transformation becomes a linear transformation in four dimensions.

**Definition 5.2.1 (Homogeneous coordinates)** If the coordinates of a point P in {a} are $p_a$, the four-dimensional column vector $(p_a,\ 1)^{\mathsf T}$ is called the **homogeneous coordinates** of P in {a}. If the components of a free vector **v** in {a} are $v_a$, we write $(v_a,\ 0)^{\mathsf T}$.

For brevity, this book often shortens $(p_a,\ 1)^{\mathsf T}$ to $(p_a,\ 1)$, and where no confusion can arise it simply writes $T\,p$, meaning: multiply using homogeneous coordinates, then drop the final 1.

Homogeneous coordinates come from projective geometry. August Ferdinand Möbius introduced them in 1827 in *Der barycentrische Calcul* (The Barycentric Calculus). In projective geometry, $(x, y, z, w)$ and any non-zero multiple of it represent the same point; when $w \ne 0$ it is the ordinary point $(x/w,\ y/w,\ z/w)$, and when $w = 0$ it represents a "point at infinity" in some direction. This book uses only the two cases w = 1 and w = 0: w = 1 represents a point and w = 0 a direction. The language of projective geometry gives the second convention an intuitive meaning: a direction is a point at infinity, and no translation, however large, can reach infinity, so directions are not affected by translations.

In the theory of mechanisms, the use of 4×4 matrices to represent the relative positions of rigid bodies began with the 1955 paper of Jacques Denavit and Richard S. Hartenberg, who used such matrices to describe the relation between neighbouring links; Chapter 13 covers this in detail. In a 1965 report from the MIT Lincoln Laboratory, Lawrence G. Roberts applied homogeneous coordinates and 4×4 matrices systematically to computer graphics, using them to handle translation, rotation and perspective projection in a uniform way, and this has been standard practice in graphics ever since. Richard P. Paul's *Robot Manipulators: Mathematics, Programming, and Control* of 1981 made the homogeneous transformation the common language of robotics textbooks.

### 5.2.3 The homogeneous transformation matrix

**Definition 5.2.2 (Homogeneous transformation matrix)** A 4×4 matrix of the form

$$
T = \begin{pmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad R \in \mathrm{SO}(3),\quad p \in \mathbb{R}^3 \tag{5.2.2}
$$

is called a **homogeneous transformation matrix**, or a **homogeneous transformation** for short. The pose $(R_{ab},\ p_{ab})$ of frame {b} relative to {a}, written as a homogeneous transformation matrix, is denoted

$$
T_{ab} = \begin{pmatrix} R_{ab} & p_{ab} \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{5.2.3}
$$

The book also often abbreviates it as $T = (R,\ p)$.

**Theorem 5.2.1** Let the pose of {b} relative to {a} be $T_{ab}$. Then for any point P and any free vector **v**,

$$
\begin{pmatrix} p_a \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = T_{ab} \begin{pmatrix} p_b \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{5.2.4}
$$

$$
\begin{pmatrix} v_a \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = T_{ab} \begin{pmatrix} v_b \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{5.2.5}
$$

*Proof*　Eq. (5.2.4) is just Eq. (5.2.1), which has been proved by Theorem 5.1.1. For Eq. (5.2.5), by block multiplication the first block row is $R_{ab}\,v_b + p_{ab} \cdot 0 = R_{ab}\,v_b$, which equals $v_a$ by Eq. (5.1.4); the second block row is 0.　∎

Theorem 5.2.1 shows that the same matrix $T_{ab}$ tells points and directions apart automatically: a point, whose last entry is 1, is rotated and translated, while a direction, whose last entry is 0, is only rotated (Figure 5.2.2). The last entry also does a kind of bookkeeping. Subtracting the homogeneous coordinates of two points, $(p,\ 1) - (q,\ 1) = (p - q,\ 0)$, gives a direction, which is exactly "the difference of two points is a free vector"; a point plus a direction still has last entry 1 and is still a point; the sum of two points has last entry 2 and has no geometric meaning. If a program finds a last entry that is neither 0 nor 1, then somewhere points and directions have been mixed up.

**What the columns of a homogeneous transformation matrix mean.** Substituting the x direction of {b}, $(1, 0, 0, 0)^{\mathsf T}$, into Eq. (5.2.5) gives the first column of $T_{ab}$, that is, the components of the x axis of {b} in {a} (with last entry 0); in the same way the second and third columns are the y axis and the z axis of {b}. Substituting the origin of {b}, $(0, 0, 0, 1)^{\mathsf T}$, into Eq. (5.2.4) gives the fourth column, that is, the position of the origin of {b} in {a} (with last entry 1). This extends the view of Section 4.1.4 that "the columns of a rotation matrix are the turned coordinate axes": **to read a homogeneous transformation matrix is to read where the three axes and the origin of {b} are in {a}** (Figure 5.2.1).

### 5.2.4 The special Euclidean group SE(3)

**Definition 5.2.3 (Special Euclidean group)** The set of all 4×4 matrices of the form (5.2.2), written

$$
\mathrm{SE}(3) = \left\{ \begin{pmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} :\ R \in \mathrm{SO}(3),\ p \in \mathbb{R}^3 \right\} , \tag{5.2.6}
$$

is called the **special Euclidean group**. Its counterpart in the plane consists of the 3×3 matrices $\begin{pmatrix} R(\theta) & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, where R(θ) is the plane rotation matrix of Section 4.1 and $p \in \mathbb{R}^2$; the set of all of them is written SE(2), and it is used to describe the pose of a mobile robot on the ground (Chapter 19).

The word "Euclidean" in the name is there because these transformations preserve the properties studied by Euclidean geometry, such as length, angle and area. In his Erlangen programme of 1872, Felix Klein proposed that a geometry is the study of the properties left unchanged by some group of transformations; Euclidean geometry belongs to the group of all isometries (distance-preserving transformations), and the orientation-preserving part of that group is the group of rigid motions, which is SE(3) here. "Special" has the same meaning as in SO(3) (Section 4.2): the determinant of the rotation matrix is +1, which rules out mirror reflections.

**Theorem 5.2.2** SE(3) forms a group under matrix multiplication, that is:

1. Closure: if $T_1 = (R_1,\ p_1)$, $T_2 = (R_2,\ p_2) \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$, then

$$
T_1 T_2 = \begin{pmatrix} R_1 R_2 & R_1 p_2 + p_1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{SE}(3) ; \tag{5.2.7}
$$

2. Associativity: $(T_1 T_2) T_3 = T_1 (T_2 T_3)$;
3. Identity: the 4×4 identity matrix $I_4 \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$;
4. Inverses: every $T = (R,\ p) \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$ is invertible, and

$$
T^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} R^{\mathsf T} & -R^{\mathsf T} p \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{SE}(3) . \tag{5.2.8}
$$

*Proof*　(1) By block multiplication, the upper-left block is $R_1R_2$, the upper-right block is $R_1 p_2 + p_1 \cdot 1$, the lower-left block is $0 \cdot R_2 + 1 \cdot 0 = 0$, and the lower-right block is $0 \cdot p_2 + 1 = 1$. By Theorem 4.2.1, $R_1R_2 \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$, so the product again has the form (5.2.2). (2) Matrix multiplication is associative. (3) $I_4 = (I,\ 0)$, where $I \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$. (4) By direct verification: by Eq. (5.2.7),

$$
T\,T^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} RR^{\mathsf T} & R(-R^{\mathsf T}p) + p \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} ,
\qquad
T^{-1}T = \begin{pmatrix} R^{\mathsf T}R & R^{\mathsf T}p - R^{\mathsf T}p \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I_4 ,
$$

and $R^{\mathsf T} \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ (Theorem 4.2.1), so $T^{-1} \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$.　∎

The geometric meaning of Eqs. (5.2.7) and (5.2.8), namely "composition" and "inversion", is the subject of Section 5.3. Here we point out just two things. First, the orientation part of the product, $R_1R_2$, depends only on the two orientations, but the position part, $R_1 p_2 + p_1$, also depends on $R_1$: the second translation must first pass through the first rotation before it can be added to the first translation. Second, as in SO(3), multiplication in SE(3) is not commutative in general. Although two pure translations, or two rotations about the same axis, can be swapped, a rotation and a translation in general cannot (Exercise 5.2.2).

An element of SE(3) has 6 independent parameters, which agrees with the count in Section 5.1.4. SE(3) is both a group and a smooth six-dimensional "surface" (a manifold); a group of this kind is called a Lie group, and Chapter 6 studies SE(3) from this point of view.

### 5.2.5 Rigid-body transformations

Why do we say that SE(3) describes rigid bodies? A rigid body is characterized by the fact that the distance between any two of its points does not change (Section 3.6), and that it does not turn into its mirror image. Below, these two properties are written as a definition, and we prove that they characterize SE(3) exactly.

**Definition 5.2.4 (Rigid-body transformation)** A mapping $g:\ \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ is called a **rigid-body transformation** if it satisfies:

1. it preserves distances: for any two points p and q, $|g(p) - g(q)| = |p - q|$;
2. it preserves orientation: for any four points o, p, q and r, the scalar triple product (Section 3.2) is unchanged, that is,

$$
\big(g(p) - g(o)\big) \cdot \Big[\big(g(q) - g(o)\big) \times \big(g(r) - g(o)\big)\Big] = (p - o) \cdot \big[(q - o) \times (r - o)\big] .
$$

The scalar triple product is the signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the three edges, and its sign says whether the three edges form a right-handed or a left-handed system (Theorem 3.2.6). The second condition says that if three edges starting from the same point form a right-handed system, they still form a right-handed system after the transformation, so a body does not turn into its own mirror image. The first condition already ensures that the absolute value of the volume is unchanged; the second only rules out a change of sign.

**Theorem 5.2.3** Every $T = (R,\ p) \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$, acting on points by $g(x) = Rx + p$, is a rigid-body transformation; conversely, every rigid-body transformation can be written in this form.

*Proof*　First we prove the first part. $|g(x) - g(y)| = |R(x - y)| = |x - y|$, because a rotation matrix preserves length (Eq. (3.3.7)). For the second condition, the difference of two points is only multiplied by R: $g(p) - g(o) = R(p - o)$, and likewise for the other two edges. By Theorem 3.2.6, the scalar triple product equals the determinant whose columns are the components of the three edges, so the triple product after the transformation is $\det\big(R\,(p - o,\ q - o,\ r - o)\big) = \det R \cdot \det(p - o,\ q - o,\ r - o)$; and since $\det R = 1$, the triple product is unchanged.

Next we prove the second part. Let g be a rigid-body transformation, and let $h(x) = g(x) - g(0)$. h preserves distances, and $h(0) = 0$, so $|h(x)| = |x|$, that is, h preserves length. By the identity $x \cdot y = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(|x|^2 + |y|^2 - |x - y|^2\right)$, h also preserves dot products. We now prove that h is linear. For any x, y and numbers k, l, expand

$$
\big|h(kx + ly) - k\,h(x) - l\,h(y)\big|^2 ;
$$

it consists of the dot products of $h(kx + ly)$, $h(x)$ and $h(y)$ with one another. Replacing all these dot products by the dot products of $kx + ly$, x and y with one another (h preserves dot products), the result is $|kx + ly - kx - ly|^2 = 0$. So $h(kx + ly) = k\,h(x) + l\,h(y)$, h is a linear transformation, and it can be written $h(x) = Qx$. Preserving dot products means $Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I$, that is, Q is an orthogonal matrix, and $\det Q = \pm 1$. Finally, the second condition fixes the sign. Take o = 0 ($h$ and g differ only by a constant translation, so the differences of two points are the same), and let p, q, r be the components $e_1$, $e_2$, $e_3$ of $\hat x$, $\hat y$, $\hat z$ in turn. The triple product on the right is 1; the left-hand side is the determinant whose columns are $Qe_1$, $Qe_2$, $Qe_3$, that is, the three columns of Q, which by Theorem 3.2.6 equals $\det Q$. So $\det Q = +1$. Hence $Q \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$, and $g(x) = Qx + g(0)$ is exactly of the form (5.2.2).　∎

Theorem 5.2.3 tells us that "a change of position of a rigid body" and "a matrix in SE(3)" are one and the same thing. The change of a rigid body from one pose to another is called a **rigid-body displacement**; it is completely determined by one element of SE(3) and does not depend on the path taken in between. A rigid body changing its pose continuously over time is called a **rigid-body motion**, and it corresponds to a curve T(t) in SE(3). Chapter 6 will prove that any rigid-body displacement can be achieved by a screw motion about some axis (Chasles' theorem).

### 5.2.6 Three uses of a homogeneous transformation

Like the rotation matrix in Section 4.2.4, a homogeneous transformation matrix is used in three ways in robotics.

**First, as a pose.** $T_{ab}$ says where {b} is and which way it faces relative to {a}. The end-effector pose reported by a controller and the workpiece pose reported by a vision system are both examples of this use.

**Second, as a change of coordinates.** The point stays still and is described in a different frame, as in Eq. (5.2.4): $(p_a, 1) = T_{ab}\,(p_b, 1)$. Example 5.2.1 uses it in this way.

**Third, as a displacement operator.** The frame stays still and a body is moved from one place to another; the new and old coordinates of every point of the body are written in the same frame:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} p' \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = D \begin{pmatrix} p \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad D \in \mathrm{SE}(3) . \tag{5.2.9}
$$

This is the active rotation of Section 4.1 generalized. The rotation part of D takes place about the origin of the frame. To rotate by R about another point c, we can first translate c to the origin, rotate, and then translate back:

$$
\mathrm{Trans}(c)\begin{pmatrix} R & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\mathrm{Trans}(-c) = \begin{pmatrix} R & c - Rc \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{5.2.10}
$$

where $\mathrm{Trans}(c) = (I,\ c)$ denotes a translation by c. Eq. (12.1.2) of Chapter 12 has exactly this form: when a joint turns, the part beyond the joint turns about a point q on the joint axis.

**Example 5.2.2** On the worktable lies a rectangular block of 120 mm × 80 mm, centred at $c = (0.80,\ 0.45,\ 0)^{\mathsf T}$ m with its long side along $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_w$. The robot turns it through 90° about its own centre and then translates it by $(-0.10,\ 0.20,\ 0)^{\mathsf T}$ m. Find the matrix of this displacement and the new positions of the corners of the block.

First write down the rotation about the centre using Eq. (5.2.10), then premultiply by the translation (what is done later is written on the left, as in Section 4.2.5):

$$
D = \mathrm{Trans}(-0.10,\ 0.20,\ 0)\ \mathrm{Trans}(c)\ \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, 90^\circ)\ \mathrm{Trans}(-c) = {{ex5_2.D}} .
$$

Premultiplying the homogeneous coordinates of the four corners $(0.86,\ 0.49,\ 0)$, $(0.74,\ 0.49,\ 0)$, $(0.74,\ 0.41,\ 0)$ and $(0.86,\ 0.41,\ 0)$ by D in turn gives their new positions (one corner per column):

$$
{{ex5_2.moved}}\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

After the rotation the long side is along $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_w$, and the centre has moved to $(0.70,\ 0.65,\ 0)$ m. Program 5.2.1 checks that the distance between any two corners is the same before and after the displacement, and verifies Theorems 5.2.2 and 5.2.3 for 200 randomly generated homogeneous transformations.

The second and third uses employ the same matrix, but their meanings are opposite: in a change of coordinates the point stays still and the frame moves; with a displacement operator the frame stays still and the point moves. This is the distinction between the passive and active rotations of Section 4.1.6 carried over to SE(3). Before writing down a line $T\,p$, be clear which of the two it is.

### 5.2.7 An everyday example

When you drag or rotate a picture on a web page, what the browser does inside is a planar homogeneous transformation. In the standards for Cascading Style Sheets (CSS) and Scalable Vector Graphics (SVG), a transformation is written `matrix(a, b, c, d, e, f)`, which stands for the 3×3 matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & c & e \\ b & d & f \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$; the last row is fixed at (0, 0, 1), and e and f are the translation. "Rotate and then move" and "rotate about a point" in graphics software are all products of such matrices; the frames and arrows in the figures of this book are recorded in this way in the SVG files too. Three-dimensional games and animation software use 4×4 homogeneous matrices, exactly as in this section, except that for perspective projection the last row is not always (0, 0, 0, 1).

A homogeneous transformation turns one conversion into one matrix multiplication. The next section studies how several conversions are multiplied in a chain and how the conversion in the opposite direction is found by inversion, and gives the subscript cancellation rule used again and again throughout the book.

**Engineer's notes**

- The last row of a homogeneous transformation matrix must be exactly (0, 0, 0, 1). Before using a matrix read from a file or over a network, first check its last row and whether its upper-left 3×3 block is a valid rotation matrix (the three checks of Section 4.1).
- Poses are stored in different formats in different systems: ROS messages use a translation plus a unit quaternion, stored as (x, y, z, w) with the real part last; ABB's RAPID also uses a translation plus a quaternion (q1, q2, q3, q4), but q1 is the real part and the identity quaternion is written [1, 0, 0, 0]; UR controllers use (x, y, z, rx, ry, rz), where the last three numbers are the rotation vector $\hat\omega\theta$, i.e. the exponential coordinates of Section 4.4; KUKA uses (X, Y, Z, A, B, C), with the orientation $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, A)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat y, B)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, C)$, the ZYX Euler angles of Section 4.5, A being yaw and C roll. Most manufacturers give positions in millimetres and angles in degrees. When exchanging data, converting to a 4×4 matrix first and then converting back is the least error-prone way; whether the real part of a quaternion comes first or last is the most common single source of mistakes.
- When transforming a large number of points (a point cloud, for example), computing $Rp + p_0$ directly takes only 9 multiplications per point, whereas padding every point to four dimensions and multiplying by the 4×4 matrix takes 16 (the last row and the final 1 are wasted work); the two give exactly the same result.
- When transforming a direction vector, its last entry must be 0. If the direction of a normal, a velocity or a force is mistakenly given a 1, a translation gets added to it.
