### 5.1.1 The problem

In the speed-reducer workshop of the WenQuest digital factory, a UR5e collaborative arm is mounted on a worktable, and its job is to take gear blanks out of a parts tray. The position of the tray is given by the fixture drawing: taking the front-left corner of the table top as the origin, the centre of the bottom face of a gear blank lies 0.80 m along the long side of the table top and 0.45 m along the short side, 0.02 m above the table top (the thickness of the tray). The robot controller, however, accepts only coordinates expressed in its own base frame: the origin is at the centre of the mounting face of the base, and the directions of the three coordinate axes are fixed by the manufacturer. The robot base is mounted on the table top at (0.30, 0.40) m from the front-left corner, and so that its cable could be led out on the left side of the table, it was turned through 180° about the vertical axis when it was installed. Before the robot can be sent to take the gear blank, we must first answer this question: what are the coordinates of the centre of the gear blank in the robot base frame?

Chapters 3 and 4 have already solved half of this problem. There the origins of the two frames coincided and only the directions of the coordinate axes differed, and the components of the same vector in the two frames were related by the direction cosine matrix, $p_a = R_{ab}\,p_b$ (Eq. (3.3.3)). Now the two frames have different origins. As Section 3.1 pointed out, a position vector depends on the choice of origin: when the origin changes, the arrow describing the same point is itself a different arrow, not merely the same arrow read against a different set of axes. So Eq. (3.3.3) cannot be applied to this problem directly; the translation between the two origins must be taken into account.

Conversions like this are found everywhere in a robot system. Figure 5.1.1 shows the example that runs through this chapter, a UR5e workstation. The worktable, the robot base, the flange, the gripper, the camera and the workpiece each have their own frame; every sensor reports its numbers in its own frame, and every motion command must be written in a frame that the controller accepts. The task of this chapter is to set up a notation and a set of operations that make the conversions between these frames hard to get wrong and easy for a computer to handle. This section first explains what frames and poses are, and solves the problem of converting the coordinates of a point between two frames with different origins.

### 5.1.2 Defining and naming frames

To give the position of a point by numbers, we must first agree where to measure from and along which directions. Representing the points of a plane by coordinates goes back to René Descartes's *La Géométrie* (Geometry), published in 1637; three-dimensional coordinates in space came into general use in the eighteenth century through the work of Alexis Claude Clairaut, Euler and others. In robotics, "where to measure from and along which directions" is summed up as one point and three directions.

**Definition 5.1.1 (Coordinate frame)** A **coordinate frame** {a} consists of a point $O_a$ and three unit vectors $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a$. $O_a$ is called the origin of {a}; the three unit vectors are called the coordinate axes of {a}. They are mutually perpendicular and form a right-handed system: $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a \times \hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a = \hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a$.

This book uses only right-handed Cartesian frames of this kind (Section 3.2), and lengths are always in metres. A frame is always attached to some body: a frame attached to the ground or to a machine base stays still, and a frame attached to a moving body moves with the body. Since the distance between any two points of a rigid body never changes, the coordinates of every point of a rigid body in a frame attached to that body never change either. This is exactly why a frame is attached to every rigid body: the relations among the points of the body are written down once and for all, and afterwards we need only keep track of where the frame itself is and which way it faces.

A frame and a reference frame are related but different ideas. A reference frame is a body that we agree to regard as being at rest and use to describe motion, for example the floor of the workshop; many frames can be attached to the same reference frame, and they differ from one another only by a fixed translation and rotation. Chapter 10 discusses the case in which the reference frame itself moves. All the frames in this chapter take the workshop floor as their reference frame, and they differ only in the choice of origin and coordinate axes.

Following the notation conventions, a frame attached to the ground or to a machine base is called a **space frame** and is often written {s}; a frame attached to a moving rigid body is called a **body frame** and is often written {b}. Industrial robotics also has a set of customary names: the reference shared by the whole work cell is called the **world frame**; the one on the robot base is the **base frame**; the one on the end face of the last link (the flange) is the **flange frame**; the one whose origin is at the tool centre point (Section 2.5) is the **tool frame**; and a frame set up on a workpiece or a fixture, that is, the user frame of Section 2.7, is also called a workpiece frame. Table 5.1.1 lists the six frames of Figure 5.1.1, and all the sections of this chapter use these names.

### 5.1.3 Coordinates of a point when the origins differ

Let {a} and {b} be two frames with origins $O_a$ and $O_b$, and let P be a point in space (Figure 5.1.2). First let us make the notation clear.

- The components in {a} of the position vector $\overrightarrow{O_aP}$ from $O_a$ to P are written $p_a$ and are called the coordinates of P in {a};
- the components in {b} of the position vector $\overrightarrow{O_bP}$ from $O_b$ to P are written $p_b$ and are called the coordinates of P in {b};
- the components in {a} of the vector $\overrightarrow{O_aO_b}$ from $O_a$ to $O_b$ are written $p_{ab}$, read "the position of the origin of {b} relative to {a}".

Note carefully here that $p_a$ and $p_b$ are the components of two different arrows. The $p_a$ and $p_b$ of Chapter 3 were readings of the same arrow on two sets of axes, and they differed only by a rotation; the $p_a$ and $p_b$ of this section start from different points, so the arrows themselves are different. The two subscripts of $p_{ab}$ are read in the same way as those of $R_{ab}$: the first subscript says "in which frame it is read", and the second says "what is being read".

The three arrows join head to tail and form the triangle $O_aO_bP$. Vectors add by the parallelogram rule, which does not depend on any frame (Section 3.1), so

$$
\overrightarrow{O_aP} = \overrightarrow{O_aO_b} + \overrightarrow{O_bP} . \tag{5.1.1}
$$

This is a vector equation: it holds for the arrows themselves, and no frame has been chosen yet. To obtain a relation between numbers, we need only write both sides of the equation as components in {a}.

caption: Two frames {a} and {b} with different origins and the same point P: the three position vectors form a triangle, and the vector equation does not depend on any frame
:::

**Theorem 5.1.1 (Transformation of the coordinates of a point)** Let the orientation of {b} relative to {a} be $R_{ab}$ and the position of the origin of {b} relative to {a} be $p_{ab}$. Then the coordinates of any point P in the two frames satisfy

$$
p_a = R_{ab}\,p_b + p_{ab} , \tag{5.1.2}
$$

$$
p_b = R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}\,(p_a - p_{ab}) . \tag{5.1.3}
$$

*Proof*　Take the components in {a} of Eq. (5.1.1) term by term. The components of $\overrightarrow{O_aP}$ on the left are $p_a$ by definition; the components of the first term on the right are $p_{ab}$ by definition. The components of the second term on the right, $\overrightarrow{O_bP}$, in {b} are $p_b$, and by Theorem 3.3.1 the components of the same vector in {a} are $R_{ab}\,p_b$. Adding the three terms gives Eq. (5.1.2). Moving a term across in Eq. (5.1.2) gives $R_{ab}\,p_b = p_a - p_{ab}$; premultiplying both sides by $R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}$ and using $R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}R_{ab} = I$ (Theorem 3.3.2) gives Eq. (5.1.3).　∎

Eq. (5.1.2) can be read column by column, which makes its geometric meaning easier to remember. Writing the three components of $p_b$ as $(u, v, w)$, we have

$$
p_a = p_{ab} + u\,(\hat x_b)_a + v\,(\hat y_b)_a + w\,(\hat z_b)_a ,
$$

where $(\hat x_b)_a$ and so on are the components of the three axes of {b} in {a}, that is, the three columns of $R_{ab}$. In other words: starting from the origin of {b}, go u along the x axis of {b}, v along its y axis and w along its z axis, and you arrive at P; Eq. (5.1.2) is simply this journey tallied in the components of {a}.

Eq. (5.1.2) differs from Eq. (3.3.3) of Chapter 3 only by the constant term $p_{ab}$, but this term changes the nature of the conversion. A linear transformation always maps zero to zero; in Eq. (5.1.2), however, $p_b = 0$ (that is, P is exactly $O_b$) corresponds to $p_a = p_{ab}$, which in general is not zero. A mapping that "first applies a linear transformation and then adds a constant" is called an **affine transformation**. Precisely because of this constant term, a 3×3 matrix alone cannot represent the conversion between frames with different origins. Section 5.2 solves this problem with a 4×4 matrix.

**Example 5.1.1** Find the coordinates of the centre of the gear blank of Section 5.1.1 in the robot base frame {s}.

The worktable frame {w} and the base frame {s} are related as follows: the origin of {s} is at $p_{ws} = (0.30,\ 0.40,\ 0)^{\mathsf T}$ m in {w}; {s} is turned through 180° about the vertical axis relative to {w}, so $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_s$ is opposite to $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_w$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_s$ is opposite to $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_w$, and $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_s$ points the same way as $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_w$. Filling in the matrix column by column following "the columns are the components of the three axes of {s} in {w}",

$$
R_{ws} = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, 180^\circ) = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The coordinates of the centre P of the gear blank in {w} are $p_w = (0.80,\ 0.45,\ 0.02)^{\mathsf T}$ m. By Eq. (5.1.3), we first find the difference of the two points, $p_w - p_{ws} = ({{ex5_1.dx:.2f}},\ {{ex5_1.dy:.2f}},\ 0.02)^{\mathsf T}$ m, and then premultiply by $R_{ws}^{\mathsf T}$:

$$
p_s = R_{ws}^{\mathsf T}\,(p_w - p_{ws}) = ({{ex5_1.ps_x:.2f}},\ {{ex5_1.ps_y:.2f}},\ {{ex5_1.ps_z:.2f}})^{\mathsf T}\ \mathrm{m}.
$$

The result can also be obtained directly by geometric reasoning: seen from the base origin, the gear blank lies 0.50 m in the direction of $+\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_w$, and $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_s = -\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_w$, so its x coordinate is −0.50 m; in the same way its y coordinate is −0.05 m; the z axes point the same way, so the height is unchanged. Program 5.1.1 computes the result both ways and checks it by substituting it back into Eq. (5.1.2).

Coordinates change with the origin, but the distance between two points does not change with the frame. The distance from the centre of the gear blank to the base origin is $|p_s| = {{ex5_1.dist_s:.3f}}$ m when computed from the coordinates in {s}, and $|p_w - p_{ws}|$ when computed from the coordinates in {w}; the results are the same. On the other hand, $|p_w| = {{ex5_1.dist_w:.3f}}$ m is its distance to the front-left corner of the table top, which is the distance between a different pair of points, so naturally its value is different. Take also the locating pin Q on the tray, $q_w = (0.80,\ 0.25,\ 0.02)^{\mathsf T}$ m; converted to {s} it becomes $q_s = ({{ex5_1.qs_x:.2f}},\ {{ex5_1.qs_y:.2f}},\ 0.02)^{\mathsf T}$ m. The distance between P and Q comes out as {{ex5_1.dist_pq:.2f}} m in both frames.

### 5.1.4 Pose

Theorem 5.1.1 shows that to convert the coordinates of any point between {a} and {b}, we need two things and only two: where the three axes of {b} point relative to {a}, that is, $R_{ab}$; and where the origin of {b} is relative to {a}, that is, $p_{ab}$. Together these two things say completely where frame {b} is and which way it faces relative to {a}.

**Definition 5.1.2 (Pose)** The **pose** of frame {b} relative to {a} is the ordered pair $(R_{ab},\ p_{ab})$, where $R_{ab} \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ is the orientation of {b} relative to {a} and $p_{ab} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the position of the origin of {b} relative to {a}. The pose of a rigid body relative to {a} means the pose relative to {a} of the frame attached to that body.

"Pose" is the combined name for "position" and "orientation". A position needs 3 numbers; an orientation is described by a rotation matrix with 9 entries, but only 3 of them are independent (Section 3.3). So 6 independent numbers in all are needed to fix the pose of one frame relative to another, which agrees with Theorem 1.4.1: a rigid body moving freely in space has 6 degrees of freedom. Chapter 10 will show this again, starting from the kinematics of points.

The two parts of a pose have different properties. $p_{ab}$ can be any three numbers in $\mathbb{R}^3$; $R_{ab}$, however, must satisfy $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ and $\det R = 1$. For this reason the set of all poses is not an ordinary six-dimensional space; as Section 5.2 will show, it forms a group.

The same pair of numbers $(R_{ab},\ p_{ab})$ both describes "where {b} is and which way it faces" and is the formula for "how coordinates in {b} convert to {a}". This is exactly like the two uses of the rotation matrix in Chapter 4, "describing an orientation" and "converting components". Beginners often ask: is the "end-effector pose" reported by a robot controller where the end-effector is, or a formula for converting coordinates? The answer is that it is both; they are two ways of saying the same thing.

### 5.1.5 Points and free vectors

Theorem 5.1.1 applies only to the coordinates of points. Quantities such as velocities, forces and directions are free vectors (Section 3.1): they have only a magnitude and a direction, no starting point, and they do not depend on the choice of origin. For a free vector, the conversion involves only the directions of the coordinate axes, and Eq. (3.3.3) of Chapter 3 still holds:

$$
v_a = R_{ab}\,v_b . \tag{5.1.4}
$$

Eqs. (5.1.2) and (5.1.4) differ only in the constant term $p_{ab}$, and this term must never be added to a free vector. The difference of two points is a free vector: writing Eq. (5.1.2) for two points P and Q and subtracting, $p_{ab}$ cancels exactly, giving $p_a - q_a = R_{ab}\,(p_b - q_b)$, which agrees with Eq. (5.1.4). This also shows why the distance between two points does not change with the frame: since $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ (Eq. (4.2.2)), a rotation matrix preserves length.

**Example 5.1.2** The origin of the workshop map frame {m} is at the south-west corner of the workshop, with x pointing east and y pointing north. The frame {v} of an AGV has its origin at the centre of the vehicle, with the x axis pointing towards the front. The AGV is parked at $p_{mv} = (12.0,\ 5.0)^{\mathsf T}$ m with a heading of 30° (the front of the vehicle turned 30° anticlockwise from due east). The coordinates of the shelf station Q on the map are $q_m = (14.5,\ 8.0)^{\mathsf T}$ m. Find the coordinates of Q in the AGV frame, and convert the velocity of the AGV between the two frames.

This is a planar problem, so the rotation matrix takes the 2×2 form of Section 4.1, $R_{mv} = R(30^\circ)$. By Eq. (5.1.3),

$$
q_v = R_{mv}^{\mathsf T}\,(q_m - p_{mv}) =
\begin{pmatrix} \cos 30^\circ & \sin 30^\circ \\ -\sin 30^\circ & \cos 30^\circ \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} 2.5 \\ 3.0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} {{ex5_1.qv_x:.3f}} \\ {{ex5_1.qv_y:.3f}} \end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{m},
$$

that is, the station is {{ex5_1.qv_x:.3f}} m straight ahead of the front of the vehicle and {{ex5_1.qv_y:.3f}} m to the left. Let us check this another way: the distance from the AGV to Q is $\sqrt{2.5^2 + 3.0^2} = {{ex5_1.rng:.3f}}$ m, and its bearing as seen on the map is $\arctan(3.0/2.5) = {{ex5_1.bear_m_deg:.2f}}^\circ$; subtracting the heading of 30° gives a bearing of {{ex5_1.bear_v_deg:.2f}}° relative to the front of the vehicle, and converting from polar to Cartesian coordinates gives the same result.

The AGV drives forward at 1.0 m/s. Its velocity in {v} is $(1.0,\ 0)^{\mathsf T}$ m/s, and by Eq. (5.1.4) in {m} it is $({{ex5_1.vm_x:.3f}},\ {{ex5_1.vm_y:.3f}})^{\mathsf T}$ m/s, that is, {{ex5_1.vm_x:.3f}} m/s towards the east and {{ex5_1.vm_y:.3f}} m/s towards the north. If the velocity were mistakenly treated as a point and converted back to {v} with Eq. (5.1.3), we would get a physically meaningless result such as $({{ex5_1.wrong_x:.2f}},\ {{ex5_1.wrong_y:.2f}})^{\mathsf T}$ m/s.

### 5.1.6 An everyday example

When you ask the way, you often hear two kinds of answer: "come out of exit B of the metro station and walk 200 metres north" and "walk 50 metres east from the main gate of the school". The two sentences may point to the same building; they use two frames with different origins. To turn one description into the other, you need to know the distance and bearing between the two origins, which corresponds to $p_{ab}$; and if someone says "turn left when you come out of the door", you also need to know which way they were facing when they came out, which corresponds to $R_{ab}$. When a phone map shows "turn right in 300 metres", it is using exactly a body frame with the walker as its origin and the direction of travel as its x axis; what is stored in the map database, on the other hand, is latitude and longitude, which is another frame. At every moment the navigation software uses Eq. (5.1.3) to convert the latter into the former.

As soon as a frame is used to describe a point, two things are involved at once: the origin and the directions. The next section writes these two things together as one matrix, so that converting a point becomes a single matrix multiplication.

**Engineer's notes**

- In programs, state the frame for every coordinate and every pose, for example with variable names such as `p_w`, `p_s`, `R_ws`, `T_ws`. When two subscripts do not match, the mistake can often be seen at a glance; write only `p` and `R`, and the mistake hides in the numbers.
- Keep units consistent. The teach pendants of many controllers display positions in millimetres, while script interfaces and most program libraries use metres; camera manufacturers' software may also use millimetres. Check the units before converting.
- Different fields have different habits for the coordinate axes: robot bases and vehicle bodies usually have z up and x forward, while the optical frame of a camera has z along the optical axis and y down (ROS REP 103 specifies both). When someone hands you coordinates, first ask which frame and which convention they are in.
- The frames in a model file are not necessarily the same as the controller's frames. In the UR5e model of Chapter 12, the base link is turned through 180° relative to the base frame. The most reliable check is to make the real robot move a short distance along one axis and see how the readings change.
- Velocities, forces and directions are transformed by the rotation matrix only, with no translation. Adding a translation to a direction vector is one of the most common mistakes in converting coordinates.
