title: Chapter Overview
---

A robot workstation contains many frames: the worktable, the robot base, the flange, the gripper, the camera, the workpiece and the parts tray each have their own origin and coordinate axes. The position of a part reported by the camera is written in the camera frame, the dimensions on the fixture drawing are written in the worktable frame, and the robot controller accepts only targets written in its own base frame. Whether the robot can pick up the part accurately depends first of all on whether the conversions between these frames have been worked out correctly. This chapter builds the mathematical tools for describing these frames and converting between them: the pose and the homogeneous transformation.

Chapter 3 dealt with converting the components of a vector between two frames whose origins coincide, and Chapter 4 described the rotation of a rigid body by a rotation matrix. This chapter adds translation to them. The position and orientation of one frame relative to another are together called its pose, which consists of a rotation matrix and a position vector. Once the two are put together into a 4×4 homogeneous transformation matrix, converting the coordinates of a point becomes a single matrix multiplication, several conversions in a row become a product of matrices, and the conversion in the opposite direction is found by taking the inverse. All homogeneous transformation matrices together form the special Euclidean group SE(3). The subscript cancellation rule, used again and again throughout the book, is stated and proved as a theorem in this chapter.

The main line of the chapter is as follows:

- Section 5.1 explains what frames and poses are, derives how the coordinates of a point convert between two frames with different origins, and distinguishes points from free vectors;
- Section 5.2 introduces homogeneous coordinates and the homogeneous transformation matrix, explains what the columns of a homogeneous transformation matrix mean and its three uses, and proves that SE(3) is a group and that rigid-body transformations preserve distances and orientation;
- Section 5.3 studies the composition and inversion of homogeneous transformations, gives the subscript cancellation rule and the formula for the inverse, and explains the difference between premultiplication and postmultiplication;
- Section 5.4 organizes all the frames of a workstation into a frame tree, gives a general algorithm for the pose between any two frames, and shows that a robot model file is itself a frame tree;
- Section 5.5 takes a UR5e workstation as its example, starts from a camera reading, computes through a chain of frames the pose the arm should reach, and analyses how calibration errors propagate along the chain.

The chapter uses the matrix operations of Chapter 2, the vectors, cross products and direction cosine matrices of Chapter 3, and the rotation matrices and SO(3) of Chapter 4. Its results are the foundation of the chapters that follow: the screws and the exponential map of Chapter 6 take SE(3) as their stage; the product-of-exponentials formula of Chapter 12 and the DH parameter method of Chapter 13 both write forward kinematics as a product of homogeneous transformations; the numerical inverse kinematics of Chapter 15, the hand-eye calibration of Chapter 50 and the tf2 coordinate-transform library of ROS 2 in Chapter 52 all use the frame trees and chains of frames of this chapter directly.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. set up frames for the devices in a workstation, write down the pose of one frame relative to another, and convert the coordinates of a point between frames with different origins;
2. write down a homogeneous transformation matrix and read its columns, use it to transform points and directions, and explain how points and free vectors differ under a transformation;
3. write the composition and inversion of frames with the subscript cancellation rule, and decide correctly whether a motion should be premultiplied or postmultiplied;
4. organize the frames of a workstation into a frame tree and find the pose between any two frames;
5. compute the target pose of the arm from a camera reading, and estimate the effect of calibration errors on grasping accuracy.
