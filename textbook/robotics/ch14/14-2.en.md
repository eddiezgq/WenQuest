### 14.2.1 The problem: the UR5e seen from the side

Seen from the side, the axes of joints 2, 3 and 4 of the UR5e are parallel to one another, and the upper arm, the forearm and the first segment of the wrist pitch in one vertical plane, just like a planar three-link arm (Section 12.1.6). Many motions on an assembly line use only this plane: lift a part off the conveyor, carry it forwards, and lower it vertically into a tray. In such motions joint 1 and the wrist joints stay still, and the inverse kinematics reduces to a planar problem: given the position of the end-effector in this plane, and sometimes also the orientation of the tool, find the angles of these few joints.

Planar problems are the foundation of spatial ones. When Section 14.5 finds all the inverse solutions of the UR5e, the last step is precisely the planar three-link arm of this section. This section first derives the inverse solution of the planar 2R arm by the most direct geometric method, then derives it again by an algebraic method, then deals with the numerical details near the boundaries, and finally extends the results to the planar 3R arm.

The notation is the same as in Section 14.1.4: link lengths $l_1 = 0.425$ m and $l_2 = 0.392$ m, joint angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ positive anticlockwise, and the end-effector position given by Eq. (14.1.2). The coordinates of the target point P are written (x, y), and its distance from the base is $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$.

### 14.2.2 The geometric method: the law of cosines

Look at the triangle OEP in Figure 14.2.1. Its three sides, $l_1$, $l_2$ and r, are all known, so its three interior angles are all determined. The angle through which the forearm has turned relative to the extension of the upper arm is $\theta_2$, so the interior angle of the triangle at the elbow point E is $\pi - \theta_2$. By the law of cosines,

$$
r^2 = l_1^2 + l_2^2 - 2l_1l_2\cos(\pi - \theta_2) = l_1^2 + l_2^2 + 2l_1l_2\cos\theta_2 , \tag{14.2.1}
$$

that is,

$$
\cos\theta_2 = \frac{x^2 + y^2 - l_1^2 - l_2^2}{2l_1l_2} . \tag{14.2.2}
$$

Eq. (14.2.1) can also be obtained directly from Eq. (14.1.2): square the x and y equations and add them, combining the cross terms with the angle-addition formula. It says that the distance from the end-effector to the base is determined by $\theta_2$ alone and does not depend on $\theta_1$. This agrees with intuition: turning joint 1 swings the whole arm about the base, and the distance from the end-effector to the base does not change.

Once $\cos\theta_2$ has been obtained from Eq. (14.2.2), $\theta_2$ has two values, one positive and one negative, corresponding to the elbow-down and elbow-up configurations:

$$
\theta_2 = \pm\arccos\frac{x^2 + y^2 - l_1^2 - l_2^2}{2l_1l_2} . \tag{14.2.3}
$$

Next we find $\theta_1$. Let β be the angle between OP and the x-axis, and ψ the angle between the upper arm OE and OP (Figure 14.2.1), both measured anticlockwise. $\theta_1$ is the direction angle of the upper arm, so $\theta_1 = \beta - \psi$. β is given directly by the coordinates of the target: $\beta = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x)$. The size of ψ could be found by using the law of cosines once more, but its sign then depends on which side the elbow is on, and this is easy to get wrong. A better way is to look at the end-effector from the direction of the upper arm: taking OE as the horizontal axis, the coordinates of the end-effector relative to O are $(l_1 + l_2\cos\theta_2,\ l_2\sin\theta_2)$, and the direction angle of this point is ψ with its sign. Hence

$$
\theta_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x) - \operatorname{atan2}(l_2\sin\theta_2,\ l_1 + l_2\cos\theta_2) . \tag{14.2.4}
$$

This formula holds for both configurations: whichever sign $\theta_2$ takes, ψ automatically takes the corresponding sign, with no separate decision needed.

**Example 14.2.1** Use the geometric method to find the two solutions with which the 2R arm reaches (0.45, 0.35) m.

r = {{ex14_2.r:.4f}} m, and by Eq. (14.2.2), $\cos\theta_2 = {{ex14_2.c2:.5f}}$, so $\theta_2 = \pm{{ex14_2.t2_deg:.2f}}^\circ$. β = atan2(0.35, 0.45) = {{ex14_2.beta_deg:.2f}}°. Taking $\theta_2 > 0$, the second term of Eq. (14.2.4) is ψ = {{ex14_2.psi_deg:.2f}}°, giving $\theta_1 = {{ex14_2.t1d:.2f}}^\circ$; taking $\theta_2 < 0$, ψ changes sign and $\theta_1 = {{ex14_2.t1u:.2f}}^\circ$. The two solutions are {{ex14_2.geo1}} (elbow down) and {{ex14_2.geo2}} (elbow up), the same as in Example 14.1.1.

### 14.2.3 The algebraic method: treating cos θ₁ and sin θ₁ as unknowns

The geometric method depends on seeing the figure clearly. A completely algebraic derivation gives the same result more systematically, and prepares the way for more complicated mechanisms later.

With $\theta_2$ already found from Eq. (14.2.2), expand $\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$ and $\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$ in Eq. (14.1.2) by the angle-addition formulas and collect the terms as combinations of $\cos\theta_1$ and $\sin\theta_1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= k_1\cos\theta_1 - k_2\sin\theta_1 , \\
y &= k_2\cos\theta_1 + k_1\sin\theta_1 ,
\end{aligned}
\qquad k_1 = l_1 + l_2\cos\theta_2,\quad k_2 = l_2\sin\theta_2 . \tag{14.2.5}
$$

$k_1$ and $k_2$ are now known numbers. Regarding $\cos\theta_1$ and $\sin\theta_1$ as two unknowns, Eq. (14.2.5) is a system of linear equations whose coefficient matrix $\begin{pmatrix} k_1 & -k_2 \\ k_2 & k_1 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $k_1^2 + k_2^2$. It is easy to verify from Eq. (14.2.1) that $k_1^2 + k_2^2 = r^2$, so as long as the target is not at the base, the system has a unique solution:

$$
\cos\theta_1 = \frac{k_1x + k_2y}{r^2}, \qquad \sin\theta_1 = \frac{k_1y - k_2x}{r^2}, \qquad \theta_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(k_1y - k_2x,\ k_1x + k_2y) .
$$

Here the denominator $r^2 > 0$ is the same for both components and can be cancelled inside atan2. For the elbow-down solution of Example 14.2.1, $k_1 = {{ex14_2.k1:.4f}}$ m and $k_2 = {{ex14_2.k2:.4f}}$ m, and the $\theta_1$ computed is the same as by the geometric method. In fact the formula above and Eq. (14.2.4) are just two ways of writing the same angle: the coefficient matrix is the planar rotation "scale up by r and turn through ψ" (Section 4.1), and solving the system amounts to turning the direction of P back through ψ.

The geometric and algebraic methods split the work into the same two steps: first find $\theta_2$ from "the distance from the end-effector to the base", then find $\theta_1$ from "the direction of the end-effector". This order is no accident. The first step uses an invariant: turning joint 1 does not change the distance from the end-effector to the base, so this distance depends only on $\theta_2$; the second step turns a known point about the axis of joint 1 onto the target. Section 14.3 will show that these two steps are the planar special cases of Paden–Kahan subproblem 3 and subproblem 1, and this idea of "first find an invariant and eliminate some of the joints" is the key to solving the inverse kinematics of spatial arms.

### 14.2.4 Numerical details near the boundaries

On paper Eqs. (14.2.3) and (14.2.4) are already complete, but a program that follows them literally runs into trouble where the arm is nearly straight or folded back. These two configurations are not rare in practice: when a robot stretches its arm out to a distant parts rack, it is nearly straight.

**Problem 1: rounding turns a solvable case into an unsolvable one.** When the target lies exactly on the outer boundary, $r = l_1 + l_2$ and the right-hand side of Eq. (14.2.2) is exactly 1. But the computer has rounding errors when it computes $x^2 + y^2 - l_1^2 - l_2^2$, and the result may be {{ex14_2.c_bad}}, and the inverse cosine is undefined for numbers greater than 1. Program 14.2.1 takes {{ex14_2.N}} target points evenly spread on the outer boundary and computes them directly by Eq. (14.2.3): {{ex14_2.fail_naive}} of them ({{ex14_2.fail_pct:.1f}}%) are judged "out of reach". When a point recorded during teaching with the arm pulled straight is reported unreachable on playback, the cause is often just this.

The remedy is to avoid subtraction near 1. From Eq. (14.2.2) and the difference of two squares,

$$
1 - \cos\theta_2 = \frac{(l_1 + l_2 - r)(l_1 + l_2 + r)}{2l_1l_2}, \qquad
1 + \cos\theta_2 = \frac{(r - |l_1 - l_2|)(r + |l_1 - l_2|)}{2l_1l_2} . \tag{14.2.6}
$$

Both expressions are products computed directly from geometric quantities, with no subtraction of two nearly equal numbers. $1 - \cos\theta_2$ measures exactly how far the target is from the outer boundary, and $1 + \cos\theta_2$ how far it is from the inner boundary. With them, $\sin\theta_2 = \pm\sqrt{(1 - \cos\theta_2)(1 + \cos\theta_2)}$, and the inverse cosine is replaced by the form of Lemma 14.1.1, $\theta_2 = \operatorname{atan2}(\sin\theta_2, \cos\theta_2)$. To decide whether there is a solution, check whether the two products in Eq. (14.2.6) are non-negative, allowing a tolerance comparable to the rounding error. Program 14.2.1 treats the same {{ex14_2.N}} boundary points in this way and misses none of them.

**Problem 2: a tiny change in the target causes a large rotation of a joint.** Differentiating both sides of Eq. (14.2.1) gives $2r\,\mathrm dr = -2l_1l_2\sin\theta_2\,\mathrm d\theta_2$, and so

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\theta_2}{\mathrm dr} = -\frac{r}{l_1l_2\sin\theta_2} . \tag{14.2.7}
$$

As $\theta_2$ approaches 0 or π, the denominator tends to zero and this derivative tends to infinity (Figure 14.2.2). Take $\theta_2 = 1^\circ$ as an example: the target is then {{ex14_2.rS:.4f}} m from the base, and moving the target 1 μm radially changes $\theta_2$ by {{ex14_2.d_ik_deg:.4f}}°; the same 1 μm move at $\theta_2 = 90^\circ$ changes it by only {{ex14_2.d_mid_deg:.6f}}°, so the former is {{ex14_2.d_ratio:.0f}} times the latter. Program 14.2.1 checks Eq. (14.2.7) with the difference quotient of two inverse solutions.

::: Figure 14.2.2
src: fig14_2_2
caption: |dθ₂/dr| as a function of θ₂ (Eq. (14.2.7), logarithmic vertical axis): when the arm is nearly straight or folded back, θ₂ is extremely sensitive to the target position

Problem 2 is different in nature from Problem 1. Problem 1 is a defect of the algorithm, removed by writing the formula differently; Problem 2 is a property of the problem itself, and no algorithm can do anything about it: if the target position itself is accurate only to a micrometre, then near the straight configuration the joint angle can be accurate only to a hundredth of a degree. Its consequences in motion are more direct: when the end-effector moves radially at constant speed through the region where the arm is nearly straight, the speed of joint 2 rises sharply and may exceed what the motor can do. This is the singularity to be discussed in Chapter 16. The engineering remedy is to lay out the workstations so that frequently used target points keep some distance from the boundary of the workspace.

### 14.2.5 The planar three-link arm: fix the wrist point first

Add a tool of length $l_3$ to the 2R arm and it becomes a planar 3R arm. Besides its position (x, y), the end-effector now has an orientation: the angle φ between the tool and the x-axis, positive anticlockwise. The sum of the three joint angles is the orientation of the tool,

$$
\theta_1 + \theta_2 + \theta_3 = \varphi . \tag{14.2.8}
$$

Given (x, y, φ), there are three unknowns and three equations. The key to the solution is one observation: the direction of the tool segment is already known, so the position of the third joint (the wrist joint) is known too; it lies at the point reached by going back a distance $l_3$ from the end-effector against the direction of the tool:

$$
w = \begin{pmatrix} x - l_3\cos\varphi \\ y - l_3\sin\varphi \end{pmatrix} . \tag{14.2.9}
$$

The wrist point w is determined by the first two joints alone, so finding $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ is the inverse kinematics of the 2R arm reaching w, which has two solutions; finally Eq. (14.2.8) gives $\theta_3 = \varphi - \theta_1 - \theta_2$. This approach, "first fix the wrist point from the orientation, then find the earlier joints from the wrist point", is precisely the planar version of the decoupling of six-axis arms in Section 14.4.

**Example 14.2.2** A planar 3R arm with $l_3 = 0.1$ m is to bring its end-effector to (0.6, 0.05) m with the tool pointing vertically down (φ = −90°).

By Eq. (14.2.9), the wrist point is $w = ({{ex14_2.wx:.2f}},\ {{ex14_2.wy:.2f}})$ m. Solving the 2R inverse kinematics for w and then finding $\theta_3$ from Eq. (14.2.8) gives two solutions, $(\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3)$ = {{ex14_2.s3a}} and {{ex14_2.s3b}}; see Figure 14.2.3a. The tool segments of the two solutions coincide exactly; they differ only in the upper arm and the forearm.

When only the position of the end-effector is given and not the orientation, there are three unknowns but only two equations, and the solutions are no longer isolated points. For each φ, two solutions can be found by the method above, provided the wrist point w lies within the ring of the 2R arm. So the solutions form a family, with φ as parameter. Which values can φ take? w lies on the circle with centre P and radius $l_3$, and its distance from the base satisfies $|w|^2 = r^2 + l_3^2 - 2rl_3\cos(\varphi - \beta)$, where β is again the direction angle of OP. The requirement $|w| \le l_1 + l_2$ gives

$$
\cos(\varphi - \beta) \ge \frac{r^2 + l_3^2 - (l_1 + l_2)^2}{2rl_3} . \tag{14.2.10}
$$

(The inner boundary $|l_1 - l_2|$ also gives a condition, but it has no effect for the dimensions and target of this example.) Take the target (0.75, 0.10) m as an example: r = {{ex14_2.rB:.4f}} m and β = {{ex14_2.bB_deg:.2f}}°. By Eq. (14.2.10), φ can only lie between {{ex14_2.phi_lo:.1f}}° and {{ex14_2.phi_hi:.1f}}°, a range of {{ex14_2.phi_span:.1f}}° in all; when the tool points roughly towards the base (φ close to β + 180°), the wrist point goes outside the outer circle. Program 14.2.1 takes 7201 values of φ round one full turn and solves the inverse kinematics for each; the range with solutions agrees exactly with Eq. (14.2.10). This is the same thing as Theorem 11.4.2 of Section 11.4: P is not in the dexterous workspace, so it can be reached with only some orientations.

### 14.2.6 Back to the UR5e: the planar three-link arm seen from the side

We now show how joints 2, 3 and 4 of the UR5e form a planar 3R arm. Let joints 1, 5 and 6 all be zero. By Table 12.1.1, the axes of joints 2, 3 and 4 all lie along the −y direction, and the arm moves in a vertical plane of constant y. Looking along the −y direction (from one side of the robot), take the horizontal coordinate u = −x (the direction in which the arm reaches out at the zero position) and the height z. Since the joint axes point along −y, from this side the positive direction of the joint angles is clockwise, and the angle turned in the plane is $-\theta_i$. The shoulder height is $H_1 = 0.163$ m, the first segment has length $L_1 = 0.425$ m and the second $L_2 = 0.392$ m; the third segment is the distance $H_2 = 0.1$ m from the axis of joint 4 to the wrist point P56 (the point where the axes of joints 5 and 6 meet), which points vertically down at the zero position. The coordinates of P56 in this plane are therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
u &= L_1\cos a_1 + L_2\cos a_2 + H_2\cos a_3 , \\
z &= H_1 + L_1\sin a_1 + L_2\sin a_2 + H_2\sin a_3 ,
\end{aligned}
\qquad a_1 = -\theta_2,\ a_2 = -\theta_2 - \theta_3,\ a_3 = -\theta_2 - \theta_3 - \theta_4 - \tfrac{\pi}{2} . \tag{14.2.11}
$$

Program 14.2.1 takes 1000 random sets of $(\theta_2, \theta_3, \theta_4)$, computes P56 with the product-of-exponentials formula and compares it with Eq. (14.2.11): the largest difference is ${{ex14_2.ur_planar:sci1}}$ m, mere rounding error. So once joints 1, 5 and 6 of the UR5e have been fixed, finding the remaining three joints is the inverse kinematics of a planar 3R arm. Section 14.5 will take the same step in the language of screws, and there joints 5 and 6 need not be assumed zero.

### 14.2.7 An everyday example

A common folding desk lamp has a base, two lamp arms and a shade, and the axes of its hinges are parallel to one another: it is exactly a planar 3R arm. To make the shade stop directly above a book with the light shining vertically down is to prescribe both the position and the orientation of the end-effector: once the direction of the shade is fixed, the position of the hinge that joins the shade (the wrist point) is fixed too; the remaining two lamp arms must bring this hinge into place, and they can arch upwards or sag downwards, either of which will do. People almost always make the lamp arms arch upwards, because the sagging arrangement would hit the desk. This is exactly what Section 14.1.3 described: first find all the solutions, then choose according to the constraints.

The next section extends these two planar steps into space: "turn a point about an axis onto a target" and "turn about an axis so that the distance from a point to another point takes a given value". These are the Paden–Kahan subproblems.

**Engineer's notes**

- To find $\theta_2$, use the factorization of Eq. (14.2.6) and atan2; do not take the inverse cosine of Eq. (14.2.2) directly. When deciding whether there is a solution, allow a tolerance comparable to the rounding error (for example $10^{-12}$), and treat a target exactly on the boundary as having one solution.
- To find $\theta_1$, use the difference of the two atan2 terms in Eq. (14.2.4); it holds for both the elbow-up and the elbow-down configuration, and there is no need to decide the quadrant separately.
- When the arm is nearly straight, $\theta_2$ is extremely sensitive to the target position (Eq. (14.2.7)). Work stations visited often should leave a margin from the outer boundary of the workspace, for example keeping $\theta_2$ no smaller than about 10°; motions that pass through the straight region along a straight line should have their speed limited.
- For a planar 3R arm with given orientation, first find the wrist point from Eq. (14.2.9). When only the position is given there are infinitely many solutions, and an extra condition is needed to fix one (for example, making the tool point down, or keeping the joints as far as possible from their limits).
