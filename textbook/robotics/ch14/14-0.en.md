title: Chapter Overview
---

Chapters 12 and 13 solved forward kinematics: given how far each joint has turned, compute where the end-effector is and which way it points. When a robot is actually at work, the question is almost always posed the other way round: the vision system tells the controller where the part is and how the gripper should be oriented, and the controller must work out the angle to which each joint should turn. Finding the joint variables from the pose of the end-effector is inverse kinematics. It is much harder than forward kinematics: the equations are non-linear, a solution may not exist, or there may be several, and one and the same pose often corresponds to several arm postures of entirely different configuration. This chapter discusses inverse solutions that can be written as formulas, that is, analytical solutions; Chapter 15 then discusses iterative numerical solutions.

The main thread of this chapter is one idea: use the geometric invariants of rotation to break a high-dimensional system of non-linear equations into several small problems, each containing only one or two unknowns. When a body turns about an axis, points on the axis do not move, the distance to a point on the axis does not change, and the component along the axis does not change. With a firm grip on these three facts, the joints can be eliminated one by one, until every step is just a simple geometric problem.

- Section 14.1 explains how the inverse kinematics problem is posed, the conditions for a solution to exist and the phenomenon of multiple solutions, starting from the elbow-up and elbow-down solutions of the planar 2R arm; it introduces the difference between analytical and numerical solutions, and the historic result that a general six-axis arm has at most 16 solutions;
- Section 14.2 derives the inverse solutions of planar two-link and three-link arms, and deals with the numerical details when the arm is nearly straight;
- Section 14.3 gives the three basic tools of the screw method, the Paden–Kahan subproblems;
- Section 14.4 discusses Pieper's criterion: when three adjacent joint axes meet in a point or are parallel to one another, the inverse solution can be written as formulas; taking a six-axis arm with a spherical wrist as the example, it uses the wrist centre to solve for position and orientation separately;
- Section 14.5 finds all the inverse solutions of the UR5e in full, at most 8 of them, checks each one against the 3D model of the parts library, and discusses how to choose among them, as well as the three kinds of singular configuration;
- Section 14.6 finds the inverse solutions of the SCARA and the Delta, explains the left-handed and right-handed configurations of the SCARA, and regards the Delta formula of Section 11.5 as Subproblem 3.

This chapter uses the rotation matrices and Rodrigues' formula of Chapter 4; the screw axes and the product-of-exponentials formula of Chapter 12; the DH parameters of Chapter 13 (used for cross-checking in Section 14.5); the workspaces and closed chains of Chapter 11; and the results of Chapters 7 and 8, where the inverse solutions of the 2R arm and the UR5e were found by numerical methods. This chapter uses the same UR5e and the same parts-library model as Chapter 12, and every inverse solution in the book has been substituted back into the parts-library model as a check. The results of this chapter will be used again and again later: the numerical inverse solutions of Chapter 15 take the analytical solutions of this chapter as a reference, Chapter 16 explains the singular configurations met in this chapter through the Jacobian matrix, and the trajectory planning of Chapter 22 must choose one branch of the inverse solution in joint space.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. state how the inverse kinematics problem is posed, judge whether a given target has a solution and roughly how many solutions it has, and distinguish the uses of analytical and numerical solutions;
2. derive the inverse solutions of planar 2R and 3R arms, tell the elbow-up and elbow-down configurations apart correctly, and write programs that are numerically robust near the boundaries;
3. state and solve the three Paden–Kahan subproblems, and reduce an inverse solution to subproblems by the technique of "eliminating the last joints and eliminating the first joints";
4. explain Pieper's criterion, and find all the inverse solutions of a six-axis arm with a spherical wrist by wrist-centre decoupling;
5. write down the 8 inverse solutions of the UR5e, check them with the 3D model, choose a solution by the principle "nearest, continuous, collision-free", and name the three kinds of singular configuration: shoulder, elbow and wrist;
6. find the inverse solutions of the SCARA and the Delta, and handle the choice of configuration under joint limits.
