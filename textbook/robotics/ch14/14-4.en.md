### 14.4.1 The problem: why industrial arms mostly have spherical wrists

Walk into a car-body welding shop and you will see rows of six-axis industrial robots. They all look much alike: a turntable, an upper arm, a forearm, and at the front of the forearm a short wrist whose three joint axes meet at one point. A wrist of this kind is called a **spherical wrist**, because it acts like a ball joint: the end-effector can turn in any direction about the centre of the wrist while the centre itself stays still. Most six-axis industrial robots use this structure.

A spherical wrist is mechanically compact, but its more important advantage is kinematic: it allows the inverse solution to be split into two small problems that do not interfere with each other. The first three joints are responsible for bringing the wrist centre to the right position, and the last three for turning the end-effector to the right orientation. Section 14.1.6 noted that a six-axis arm of general geometry requires solving a polynomial of degree 16; a six-axis arm with a spherical wrist requires solving at most one quartic equation, and in common industrial robots the first three joints also have a special geometry, so that every step is just one Paden–Kahan subproblem. This section discusses this decoupling and the general result behind it: Pieper's criterion.

### 14.4.2 The wrist centre: separating position from orientation

Let the axes of joints 4, 5 and 6 of a six-axis arm meet, in the zero position, at a point $p_{w0}$. This point is called the **wrist centre**.

**Lemma 14.4.1** If the axes of joints 4, 5 and 6 meet at $p_{w0}$, then for any $\theta_4$, $\theta_5$ and $\theta_6$,

$$
e^{[\mathcal S_4]\theta_4}\,e^{[\mathcal S_5]\theta_5}\,e^{[\mathcal S_6]\theta_6}\,p_{w0} = p_{w0} .
$$

*Proof* $p_{w0}$ lies on axis 6, so by Lemma 14.3.1 (1), $e^{[\mathcal S_6]\theta_6}p_{w0} = p_{w0}$; in the same way it does not move under the rotations about axes 5 and 4 either.　∎

Note that the axes used here are those of the zero position: the screw axes $\mathcal S_i$ in the product of exponentials formula are all the axes in the zero position (Section 12.2), so the condition is that the three axes meet at one point in the zero position. After the joints turn, the three axes move with the arm and still meet at one point, and this point of intersection is the current wrist centre. The current positions of axes 4, 5 and 6 are obtained by applying to the zero-position axes $e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}\cdots e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}$, then that product further multiplied by $e^{[\mathcal S_4]\theta_4}$, and then further multiplied by $e^{[\mathcal S_5]\theta_5}$; the two extra rotations do not move $p_{w0}$, so all three axes pass through the same point $e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}p_{w0}$.

Postmultiplying both sides of Eq. (14.1.1) by $M^{-1}$ and applying them to $p_{w0}$, the last three joints disappear by Lemma 14.4.1:

$$
e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}\,e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}\,e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}\,p_{w0} = T_dM^{-1}p_{w0} =: p_w . \tag{14.4.1}
$$

The right-hand side is known: it is where the wrist centre must be for the target pose. Eq. (14.4.1) is three equations in three unknowns, and involves only the first three joints. Once $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$ and $\theta_3$ have been found, only the last three joints remain in Eq. (14.1.1):

$$
e^{[\mathcal S_4]\theta_4}\,e^{[\mathcal S_5]\theta_5}\,e^{[\mathcal S_6]\theta_6} = \big(e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}\big)^{-1}\,T_dM^{-1} . \tag{14.4.2}
$$

Again the right-hand side is known. Since all three rotations on the left are about $p_{w0}$, Eq. (14.4.2) is really just an equation about a rotation: the last three joints must achieve a given orientation. This is the decoupling of position and orientation.

The position of the wrist centre can also be read directly from the end-effector. In the example of this section, the wrist centre lies a distance $D_6$ back from the centre of the flange along the approach direction (the $x_b$-axis of the end-effector), so $p_w = p_d - D_6\,R_d\hat x$, where $p_d$ and $R_d$ are the position and orientation parts of the target pose. This is the same idea as "first find the wrist point from the direction of the tool" for the planar 3R arm in Section 14.2.5.

### 14.4.3 Pieper's criterion

The wrist-centre method depends on "three adjacent axes meeting at one point". In his 1968 doctoral thesis Donald L. Pieper studied systematically when a six-axis arm has an analytical solution, and arrived at the following result.

**Theorem 14.4.1 (Pieper's criterion)** In a six-axis serial arm (all of whose joints are revolute), if three adjacent joint axes meet at one point, the inverse kinematics can be reduced to a polynomial equation in one variable of degree at most four, and hence has an analytical solution. The same conclusion holds when three adjacent joint axes are parallel to one another.

The parallel case can be regarded as "meeting at a point at infinity", and later textbooks usually call both cases together **Pieper's criterion**. The criterion is a sufficient condition, not a necessary one: some arms that do not satisfy it have analytical solutions all the same; but for an arm that does satisfy it, the inverse solutions can always be written as formulas.

The full proof of the theorem has to treat several cases separately: the three intersecting (or parallel) axes can be axes 1–3, 2–4, 3–5 or 4–6. The case Pieper discussed in detail is that in which the last three axes meet at one point: the wrist-centre equation (14.4.1) then involves only the first three joints, and whatever the geometry of the first three axes, it can be reduced by elimination to a quartic equation in one variable. This book does not repeat this general elimination; it discusses only the two concrete structures most common in engineering. In both, the first three axes themselves also have a special geometry, so that every step is one Paden–Kahan subproblem: one is the six-axis arm with a spherical wrist of this section (axes 4, 5 and 6 meet at one point; in addition axes 1 and 2 intersect and axes 2 and 3 are parallel), the other is the UR5e of Section 14.5 (axes 2, 3 and 4 are parallel). The case in which the first three axes meet at one point becomes the case in which the last three axes do by "looking at the arm the other way round" (taking the end-effector as the base and exchanging the roles of the two sides of Eq. (14.1.1)).

Figure 14.4.2 compares the two structures. On the left is a spherical wrist, whose three wrist axes meet at one point; on the right is the UR5e, in which the axes of joints 2, 3 and 4 are parallel to one another and the three wrist axes do not meet at one point: axes 4 and 5 intersect, and so do axes 5 and 6, but axes 4 and 6 are parallel and $H_2$ apart (Table 12.1.1).

### 14.4.4 The eight solutions of a six-axis arm with a spherical wrist

For comparison with the UR5e, we take as our example "a UR5e with its sideways offsets removed": shoulder height $H_1 = 0.163$ m, upper arm $L_1 = 0.425$ m, forearm $L_2 = 0.392$ m, distance from the wrist centre to the centre of the flange $D_6 = 0.1$ m, all joint axes in the same vertical plane, and the three wrist axes meeting at the wrist centre. In the zero position the arm reaches out horizontally along +x. The axes of the joints are listed in Table 14.4.1, and the screw axes are computed from them by Eq. (12.1.3); the zero-position pose M has the identity matrix as its orientation and $(L_1 + L_2 + D_6,\ 0,\ H_1)$ as its position.

The axes of joints 2 and 3 are taken along −y, so that the upper arm rises when $\theta_2 > 0$. Using the two devices based on Lemma 14.3.1, the inverse solution takes four steps.

**Step 1: the wrist centre.** Compute the target wrist centre $p_w$ from Eq. (14.4.1).

**Step 2: $\theta_3$, Subproblem 3.** Axes 1 and 2 meet at the shoulder point $q_2$. By Lemma 14.3.1 (2), rotations about these two axes do not change the distance to $q_2$, so taking the distance of both sides of Eq. (14.4.1) to $q_2$ gives

$$
\big\|e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}p_{w0} - q_2\big\| = \|p_w - q_2\| . \tag{14.4.3}
$$

This is exactly Subproblem 3: turn the wrist centre about axis 3 so that its distance to the shoulder point equals the distance from the target wrist centre to the shoulder point. In general there are two solutions, elbow up and elbow down.

**Step 3: $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, Subproblem 2.** Write $a = e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}p_{w0}$; Eq. (14.4.1) becomes $e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}a = p_w$, and the two axes meet at $q_2$, so this is Subproblem 2, which in general has two solutions. Geometrically, in one the turntable faces the direction of the wrist centre and the upper arm leans forwards; in the other the turntable turns a further 180° and the upper arm leans back over the top, reaching the same point from behind.

**Step 4: $\theta_4$, $\theta_5$ and $\theta_6$, Subproblems 2 and 1.** Call the right-hand side of Eq. (14.4.2) h; it is known. Take a point on axis 6, $p_6 = p_{w0} + (0.1, 0, 0)$, which is not affected by $\theta_6$; Eq. (14.4.2) gives $e^{[\mathcal S_4]\theta_4}e^{[\mathcal S_5]\theta_5}p_6 = h\,p_6$, and since axes 4 and 5 meet at the wrist centre this is again Subproblem 2, with two solutions. Finally, take a point not on axis 6 and use Subproblem 1 to find $\theta_6$.

Each of the three steps offers two choices, so in general there are 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 solutions. For this arm, this verifies Theorem 14.4.1 constructively: every step is an analytical subproblem, and Subproblems 3 and 2 come down in the end to solving only quadratic equations (Eqs. (14.3.8) and (14.3.10)), whose degree does not exceed the four of the theorem. Note that Steps 2 and 3 make use of an extra structural feature, namely that axes 1 and 2 intersect (the shoulder point $q_2$ lies on both axes); when the first three axes lack such a structure (for example when the shoulder has a forward offset, Exercise 14.4.3), Steps 2 and 3 must be replaced by Pieper's quartic equation, while Step 4 is not affected at all.

### 14.4.5 The wrist singularity and Euler angles

The Subproblem 2 of Step 4 degenerates in one situation: $\sin\theta_5 = 0$. When $\theta_5 = 0$, axis 4 and axis 6 coincide (in Table 14.4.1 both run along x through the wrist centre in the zero position); turning through some angle about axis 4 and through the same angle the opposite way about axis 6 leaves the orientation of the end-effector unchanged, and only $\theta_4 + \theta_6$ is determined. When $\theta_5 = \pm 180^\circ$ the two axes coincide in opposite directions and only $\theta_4 - \theta_6$ is determined (the tool is then folded back onto the forearm, which cannot be reached in practice). This is the wrist singularity.

It is the same thing as the gimbal lock of Section 4.5. The three wrist joints turn in sequence about "roll–pitch–roll" axes, and the three joint angles are precisely a set of Euler angles of the end-effector orientation whose first and last axes are the same (of the same family as ZYZ Euler angles; here of X–Y–X type, apart from differences in positive sense). The gimbal lock of Section 4.5 says that when the middle rotation brings the first and third axes of rotation into line, only a combination of the three angles can be determined. For ZYX Euler angles this happens when the pitch angle is ±90°; for Euler angles whose first and last axes are the same, it happens when the middle angle is 0 or ±180°. So Step 4 of the inverse solution of a spherical wrist is "finding Euler angles from a rotation matrix", and the wrist singularity is the singularity of these Euler angles. If an industrial robot passes near $\theta_5 = 0$ while moving along a straight line, joints 4 and 6 suddenly turn in opposite directions at very high speed; many controllers raise an alarm or slow down automatically when approaching such a configuration.

### 14.4.6 Arms that do not satisfy Pieper's criterion

Not every six-axis arm satisfies Pieper's criterion. Some arms have an offset in the wrist, so that the three wrist axes do not meet at one point, and no three adjacent axes are parallel either. The inverse solutions of such arms can be found only by the methods for a general 6R arm: either reduce the problem to a polynomial of degree 16 as in Section 14.1.6 and find its roots numerically, or solve it iteratively with the numerical methods of Chapter 15. A redundant arm with seven joints is another case again: its solutions are not isolated points, and one usually fixes one parameter first (for example the angle through which the elbow turns about the line from shoulder to wrist), reducing the problem to the six-axis case; Chapter 17 will discuss this.

So Pieper's criterion is not only a mathematical result but also a constraint in the design of arms. When deciding on the arrangement of the joints, designers often deliberately make three axes intersect or be parallel, in exchange for an inverse solution that is simple, fast and reliable. The UR5e satisfies the "three parallel axes" case; its wrist has offsets and is not a spherical wrist. The next section will show that it too has at most eight solutions, each of which can be written as a formula.

### 14.4.7 Everyday examples

When walking with a glass of water in your hand, you unconsciously treat position and orientation separately: the shoulder and elbow bring the hand to a suitable position in front of you, and the wrist keeps the glass level. To pour the water into another glass, the hand stays still above the rim and only the wrist turns, tilting the glass. The axes of rotation of the wrist meet roughly at the centre of the wrist, and the wrist has little effect on the position of the hand; this is an intuitive version of wrist-centre decoupling.

The human shoulder joint, by contrast, is a true ball joint. It can be regarded as three axes of rotation meeting at the centre of the shoulder, which corresponds to the case "the first three axes meet at one point" of Theorem 14.4.1.

**Engineer's notes**

- When choosing a robot, take a look at the structure of the wrist: for an arm with a spherical wrist the controller uses the analytical solution and can list all configurations for any pose in microseconds; an arm with a wrist offset can often be solved only numerically, and most of the "inverse kinematics failed" messages seen in offline programming software come from robots of this kind.
- In the inverse solution of a spherical wrist, a wrist flip changes only $\theta_4$, $\theta_5$ and $\theta_6$. When choosing among solutions, avoid having different wrist configurations at two adjacent points of a taught program; otherwise the robot will flip the whole wrist over between the two points.
- The wrist singularity ($\theta_5 = 0$) lies inside the workspace and cannot be avoided by "keeping away from the boundary". When planning trajectories that move along a path, such as welding or applying adhesive, choose tool orientations that keep $\theta_5$ from passing through zero, or deliberately keep $\theta_5$ of one sign.
- Once the inverse solution is written in the two steps "wrist centre + orientation", the joint limits of the first three joints and of the last three can be checked separately, which makes it easy to tell whether the position is out of reach or the orientation cannot be achieved.
