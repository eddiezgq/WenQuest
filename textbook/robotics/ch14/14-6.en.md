### 14.6.1 The problem: two fast workers on the assembly line

On the electronics assembly line of the digital factory, a SCARA robot drives screws into circuit boards: the position (x, y) and height z of every screw hole are given by the drawing, and the screwdriver bit must also turn to a suitable angle to line up with the slot in the screw head. Above the conveyor next to it, a Delta robot uses a suction cup to pick parts one by one into bins, more than a hundred times a minute. The controllers of both robots have to do the same thing in every control cycle: convert the end-effector target into joint commands.

The inverse solutions of both these robots are simpler than those of six-axis arms. The four joints of the SCARA have clearly divided tasks, with the horizontal position, the height and the angle each handled separately; the three legs of the Delta do not interact, and each has only one unknown. But simple does not mean there is no choice: the SCARA has right-handed and left-handed configurations, and each leg of the Delta has two solutions. This section derives their inverse solutions, shows that they are the same thing as the methods of the previous sections, and discusses how to choose a solution under joint limits.

### 14.6.2 SCARA: a planar 2R plus a lift and a rotation

The SCARA of the parts library (Section 12.3.5, Table 12.3.2) has four joints: joints 1 and 2 rotate about vertical axes, and the two horizontal arms are $L_1 = 0.35$ m and $L_2 = 0.25$ m long; joint 3 is a vertical lead screw, positive downwards, with a stroke of 0–0.15 m; joint 4 rotates the tool about a vertical axis. At the zero position the two arms are stretched out along the x-axis, the tool point (the origin of the tool link) is at (0.6, 0, $z_0$) with $z_0 = {{ex14_6.z0:.3f}}$ m, and the x-axis of the tool lies along the x direction.

By the analysis of Section 12.3.5, the axes of the three revolute joints are all vertical, so the sum of their angles is the angle through which the tool has turned about the vertical; the lead screw changes only the height. So the forward kinematics can be written directly as

$$
\begin{aligned}
x &= L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2), & y &= L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2), \\
z &= z_0 - \theta_3, & \varphi &= \theta_1 + \theta_2 + \theta_4 ,
\end{aligned} \tag{14.6.1}
$$

where φ is the angle between the tool x-axis and the base x-axis, positive anticlockwise. Section 12.3 has already checked this result against the parts-library model. Of the four equations in Eq. (14.6.1), x and y contain only $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, z contains only $\theta_3$, and φ adds $\theta_4$. The inverse solution therefore has three steps:

1. find $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ from (x, y): this is exactly the planar 2R arm of Section 14.2, using Eqs. (14.2.6) and (14.2.4);
2. find the lead-screw displacement from the height: $\theta_3 = z_0 - z$;
3. find joint 4 from the tool angle: $\theta_4 = \varphi - \theta_1 - \theta_2$.

In the language of the Paden–Kahan subproblems, step 1 is "Subproblem 3 + Subproblem 1" (Section 14.3.6), step 3 is one Subproblem 1, and step 2 is a subtraction for a prismatic joint. Four joints and four equations: in general there are two solutions, differing only in step 1.

Seen from above, when $\theta_2 > 0$ the elbow is to the right of the line from the base to the target, and the forearm folds back to the left (anticlockwise) to the target, like a person's right arm reaching across a desk; when $\theta_2 < 0$ it is the other way round. SCARA users borrow this image and call the former the **right-handed configuration** and the latter the **left-handed configuration**. Like "elbow up" and "elbow down" in Section 14.1.4, these two names describe the shape; manufacturers differ in their conventions for the positive direction of the joints, so the same shape may have $\theta_2$ of opposite sign.

**Example 14.6.1** The tool point is to reach (0.40, 0.20, 0.15) m with the tool x-axis at 30° to the x-axis. Find the joint variables of both configurations, and check the joint limits (±140° for joint 1, ±145° for joint 2).

The three steps give, for the right-handed configuration, $(\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3, \theta_4)$ = {{ex14_6.sr}}, and for the left-handed configuration {{ex14_6.sl}}. The two have the same lead-screw displacement, their values of $\theta_2$ are negatives of each other, and $\theta_4$ makes up for the different sums of the first two joint angles, so that the tool points the same way. Both are within the limits. Program 14.6.1 substitutes them into the parts-library model, and the largest difference between the end-effector pose and the target is ${{ex14_6.worst:sci1}}$ (Figure 14.6.1a).

**Joint limits and configurations.** The two configurations cannot always both be used. Take Q = (−0.30, 0.35) m as an example: in the right-handed configuration the first two joints are {{ex14_6.only_t}}, within the limits; in the left-handed configuration they are {{ex14_6.other_t}}, and joint 1 is beyond 140°. Figure 14.6.1b shows the situation over the whole horizontal workspace: where both configurations can reach, the choice depends on other conditions; in the two regions behind the base on either side, only one configuration can be used. Program 14.6.1 counts on a 2.5 mm grid: of the region that can be reached once the limits are taken into account, both configurations are usable in {{ex14_6.both_pct:.1f}}%, the right-handed configuration in {{ex14_6.r_pct:.1f}}% and the left-handed configuration in {{ex14_6.l_pct:.1f}}%. The last two are equal because the limits are symmetric about zero and the two configurations are mirror images of each other.

**No change of hand along a path.** Let the tool move along the line x = 0.45 m with y going from −0.3 m to 0.3 m. Program 14.6.1 finds the inverse solution every 1 mm along the way, and the joint angles of both configurations change continuously: joint 1 of the right-handed configuration goes from {{ex14_6.r1_lo:.1f}}° to {{ex14_6.r1_hi:.1f}}°, and that of the left-handed configuration from {{ex14_6.l1_lo:.1f}}° to {{ex14_6.l1_hi:.1f}}°. To change from one configuration to the other on the way, $\theta_2$ would have to pass through zero, that is, the arm would have to be straight with the tool at the outer boundary, which cannot happen in the middle of the line. So a SCARA point usually carries a left-handed or right-handed flag, set when programming and unchanged during motion; to change hands, a dedicated motion through the straight position is arranged.

### 14.6.3 Delta: each leg is one Subproblem 3

Section 11.5.4 has already derived the inverse kinematics of the Delta: given the centre p of the moving platform, the angle $\theta_k$ of the active arm of leg k satisfies the loop-closure condition (11.5.6), which, after being put into the form $A_k\cos\theta_k + B_k\sin\theta_k = C_k$, is solved by Eq. (11.5.7); each leg has two solutions, and the three legs do not interact. Rather than repeat the derivation, we look at this result again from the point of view of subproblems, and on that basis discuss choosing a solution and following a path.

The notation is that of Section 11.5: the distance from the centre of the base to each hinge is $R_b = 0.15$ m, the distance from the centre of the moving platform to its spherical joints is $R_p = 0.04$ m, the active arms are $L_a = 0.22$ m long and the passive rods $L_b = 0.5$ m; the horizontal unit vector of the vertical plane containing leg k is $e_k$, the axis of the active arm lies along $t_k = \hat z \times e_k$, and the angle $\theta_k$ is measured from the horizontal, positive downwards.

At $\theta_k = 0$ the end of the active arm is at $A_k = (R_b + L_a)\,e_k$. Rotating through $\theta_k$ about the hinge axis (through $R_be_k$, with direction $t_k$), it arrives at the elbow point $E_k = e^{[\mathcal S_k]\theta_k}A_k$. The loop-closure condition (11.5.6) says that the distance from the elbow point to the corresponding point $P'_k = p + R_pe_k$ on the moving platform equals $L_b$:

$$
\big\|P'_k - e^{[\mathcal S_k]\theta_k}A_k\big\| = L_b . \tag{14.6.2}
$$

This is exactly Subproblem 3 (Definition 14.3.1): rotate a point about an axis so that its distance to another point has a given value. Following Eq. (14.3.9), first take out the distance along the axis: $P'_k$ and $A_k$ differ by $t_k^{\mathsf T}p$ in the direction $t_k$, which is the distance of the platform centre from the plane of leg k, the tangential component $v_k$ of Section 11.5. So within the plane of the leg, the distance from the elbow point to the projection of $P'_k$ must be

$$
L_b' = \sqrt{L_b^2 - v_k^2} . \tag{14.6.3}
$$

This gives the formula of Section 11.5 an intuitive meaning: when the platform is off the plane of the leg, part of the length of the passive rod is "used up" outside the plane, and the effective length within the plane shrinks to $L_b'$. Then, by Eq. (14.3.10), intersecting two circles in the plane of the leg gives the two solutions of Eq. (11.5.7). Program 14.6.1 takes {{ex14_6.N}} random points in the workspace and, for each leg, solves both with Subproblem 3 and with Eq. (11.5.7), comparing {{ex14_6.n_cmp}} solutions in all; the two methods agree completely.

**Eight combinations, only one usable.** With two solutions per leg, the three legs combine in $2^3 = 8$ ways. At the rest position p = (0, 0, −0.42) m of the parts-library model, the two solutions of each leg are {{ex14_6.th_out:.2f}}° (elbow out) and {{ex14_6.th_in:.2f}}° (elbow in: the active arm is flipped up and over, pointing almost horizontally towards the centre). All 8 combinations satisfy the three loop-closure conditions, but the limits of the active-arm angle are −40° to 90°, and the number of combinations within the limits is only {{ex14_6.n_ok}}: the one with all three legs elbow out (Figure 14.6.2a). Unlike the SCARA, a Delta in practice never switches between the two solutions: once the mechanism has been assembled it stays in the elbow-out assembly mode (Definition 11.5.1), and the controller simply always takes the same sign in Eq. (11.5.7).

**The boundary of the workspace.** As the moving platform descends along the central axis, all three legs tend towards being straight. When the active arm and the passive rod are in a straight line, the distance from the hinge to $P'_k$ equals $L_a + L_b$, the two solutions of Eq. (14.6.2) merge; below that there is no solution. From the geometry, the height of the platform centre is then $z = -\sqrt{(L_a + L_b)^2 - (R_b - R_p)^2} = {{ex14_6.z_str:.4f}}$ m. The straightened leg points from the hinge to $P'_k$, and $P'_k$ is closer to the centre than the hinge ($R_p < R_b$), so the active arm points inwards and obliquely down, at an angle of {{ex14_6.t_str:.1f}}°, already beyond the 90° limit. On the way down, the platform first reaches the limit at the position where the active arm points straight down ($\theta_k = 90^\circ$): the elbow point is then $L_a$ directly below the hinge, and the length of the passive rod gives $z = -L_a - \sqrt{L_b^2 - (R_b - R_p)^2} = {{ex14_6.z_lim:.4f}}$ m. So the lower boundary of the workspace along the central axis is set by the joint limit, not by the legs becoming straight. The lower boundary of −0.70 m computed on a 1 cm grid in Section 11.5 agrees with this.

**Along the pick path.** The demonstration path defined for this Delta in the parts library is a horizontal circle of radius 0.12 m at height −0.42 m. Program 14.6.1 finds the inverse solution every 1° along this circle, then goes back from the three angles to the platform centre by intersecting three spheres (Eq. (11.5.8)); the largest difference from the original point is ${{ex14_6.path_err:sci1}}$ m. The angles of all three active arms vary between {{ex14_6.th1_lo:.1f}}° and {{ex14_6.th1_hi:.1f}}°, with curves of the same shape shifted by 120° from one another (Figure 14.6.2b), a direct result of the three legs being spaced evenly at 120°. Whichever leg the platform is close to, the active arm of that leg is raised high.

### 14.6.4 Serial and parallel: easy and hard exactly reversed

Section 11.5.4 pointed out that for parallel mechanisms the inverse kinematics is easy and the forward kinematics hard, while for serial mechanisms it is exactly the other way round. The results of this chapter make this more concrete:

- The forward kinematics of the UR5e is a product of six matrix exponentials, done in one step; the inverse solution takes three steps and has up to 8 solutions.
- Both the forward kinematics and the inverse solution of the SCARA are very simple, because its joint axes are all parallel and its four joints look after different degrees of freedom.
- The inverse solution of the Delta is three independent instances of Subproblem 3; the forward kinematics has to solve the three legs together, by intersecting three spheres. For the Delta the three-sphere intersection still has a formula, but for general parallel mechanisms (such as the Stewart platform) the forward kinematics can only be solved numerically (Chapter 18).

This duality comes from the structure. In a serial mechanism each joint affects all the links beyond it, so going forward one simply multiplies along the chain, while going backward one has to separate the effects of the joints; in a parallel mechanism each leg connects to the moving platform independently, so if the platform is known each leg can be computed separately, while if the legs are known one has to consider how they jointly determine the platform.

### 14.6.5 Everyday examples

When you sit at a desk writing, seen from above, the elbow of your right arm is out to the right, and the forearm folds back to the left to the pen tip; when a left-hander writes, the elbow is out to the left and the forearm folds back to the right. The pen tip can be in exactly the same place on the paper, yet the arms are mirror images of each other. The "right-handed" and "left-handed" configurations of the SCARA borrow exactly this way of speaking. A right-handed person feels awkward reaching for something far to the right with the left hand; this is the same reason as "only one configuration is usable behind the base" in Figure 14.6.1b: the joints have limited ranges of rotation.

A marionette is an everyday example of a parallel mechanism. Several strings hang the puppet's body from a control bar above; when the bar moves, each string keeps its length and the puppet is pulled to a definite position. To bring the puppet to a certain place, how each string must be pulled can be worked out one string at a time; conversely, to say where the puppet is just by watching the control bar, all the strings must be considered together. This is an intuitive version of the Delta's easy inverse and hard forward solutions.

**Engineer's notes**

- SCARA points should carry a left-handed or right-handed flag. Reaching the same point with different hands can make joint 1 differ by more than a hundred degrees, with completely different cycle times and collision risks. When two points in a program have different configuration flags, the controller changes hands by passing through the straight position; this motion is often slow and can easily hit surrounding equipment.
- The regions of Figure 14.6.1b where only one configuration is usable are suitable for trays placed "behind"; frequently used stations should be placed where both configurations are usable, to leave room for adjustment.
- The Delta controller always takes the elbow-out branch. At power-up initialization, first make sure that the active arms really are on the outer side (for example by returning to the mechanical zero); otherwise Eq. (11.5.7) takes the wrong sign, and the angles it gives will jam the mechanism.
- The three legs of the Delta can be computed in parallel, and the computational cost of the inverse solution is about that of one forward-kinematics step of a six-axis arm, so it can be solved in real time in control cycles running at more than a thousand per second.
