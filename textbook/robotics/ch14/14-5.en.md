### 14.5.1 The problem: one pose, eight arms

Return to the problem of Section 14.1.1: the UR5e is to bring its flange to the pose of Example 12.3.1. Chapter 8 solved position-only inverse kinematics for the UR5e by the LM method, and what it obtained was the one solution nearest the initial value. What the controller really needs is all the solutions: from them it must pick the one that is nearest the current configuration, stays within the joint limits and does not hit the worktable.

The UR5e does not satisfy the conditions for a spherical wrist: its three wrist axes do not meet at one point. Axes 4 and 5 intersect, and axes 5 and 6 meet at $P_{56}$, but axes 4 and 6 are parallel and $H_2$ apart (Table 12.1.1). But its joints 2, 3 and 4 are parallel to one another, which is the other case of Pieper's criterion. This section uses the Paden–Kahan subproblems to give all the inverse solutions of the UR5e, in three steps: first find $\theta_1$, then $\theta_5$ and $\theta_6$, and finally $\theta_2$, $\theta_3$ and $\theta_4$. Each step uses a geometric invariant to eliminate some of the joints. We shall see that the UR5e has at most 8 solutions for a given pose, that each of them has a clear geometric meaning, and each is checked one by one against the 3D model of the parts library.

The screw axes, the points $q_i$ on the axes and the zero-position pose M used here are all taken from Table 12.1.1. The few geometric facts we need can also be read off that table:

- axis 1 is vertical and passes through the origin;
- axes 2, 3 and 4 are parallel to one another, all with direction $\hat\omega_2 = (0, -1, 0)$ at the zero position;
- axis 5 is perpendicular to axis 4; axis 6 is perpendicular to axis 5, and axes 5 and 6 meet at a point, the $q_6$ of the table, written $P_{56}$ below and called the wrist point of the UR5e;
- the flange centre lies $W_4 = 0.1$ m out from $P_{56}$ along the flange normal (the end-effector $y_b$-axis);
- the coordinate of $P_{56}$ along $\hat\omega_2$ is $d_4 = W_1 - W_2 + W_3 = {{ex14_5.d4:.3f}}$ m; this is the distance from the base axis to the vertical plane that contains the wrist point and is perpendicular to $\hat\omega_2$, and it is written $d_4$ among the DH parameters (Section 13.3).

### 14.5.2 Step 1: θ₁

**The wrist point is affected only by the first four joints.** $P_{56}$ lies on axis 5 and on axis 6, so by Lemma 14.3.1 (1) neither $\theta_5$ nor $\theta_6$ moves it. Multiplying both sides of Eq. (14.1.1) on the right by $M^{-1}$ and applying them to $P_{56}$ at the zero position gives

$$
e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}\,e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}\,e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}\,e^{[\mathcal S_4]\theta_4}\,P_{56} = p_w , \qquad p_w = p_d - W_4\,R_d\,\hat y . \tag{14.5.1}
$$

The $p_w$ on the right is where the wrist point must be in the target pose: start from the target flange centre $p_d$ and move back $W_4$ along the target normal $R_d\hat y$ (the second column of $R_d$).

**Eliminating joints 2, 3 and 4.** Multiply both sides of Eq. (14.5.1) on the left by $e^{-[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}$. What remains on the left is three rotations about axes 2, 3 and 4, all parallel to $\hat\omega_2$. By Lemma 14.3.1 (3), a rotation about an axis parallel to $\hat\omega_2$ does not change the component of a point along $\hat\omega_2$ (the three rotations have different points r on their axes, but $\hat\omega_2^{\mathsf T}r$ cancels between the two sides of the equation). So the component of the left-hand side along $\hat\omega_2$ equals the component $d_4$ of $P_{56}$ at the zero position:

$$
\hat\omega_2^{\mathsf T}\,e^{-[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}\,p_w = \hat\omega_2^{\mathsf T}P_{56} = d_4 . \tag{14.5.2}
$$

Joints 2, 3 and 4 have all disappeared from the equation. Axis 1 is the z-axis through the origin, so $e^{-[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}p_w$ simply rotates $p_w = (x, y, z)$ through $-\theta_1$ about the z-axis, and its y component is $-x\sin\theta_1 + y\cos\theta_1$; with $\hat\omega_2 = (0, -1, 0)$, Eq. (14.5.2) becomes

$$
x\sin\theta_1 - y\cos\theta_1 = d_4 . \tag{14.5.3}
$$

This is exactly the form of Lemma 14.1.1, with A = −y, B = x, C = $d_4$. By Eq. (14.1.4),

$$
\theta_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(x, -y) \pm \operatorname{atan2}\!\Big(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 - d_4^2},\ d_4\Big) . \tag{14.5.4}
$$

The condition for a solution is $\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \ge d_4$: the wrist point must be no closer than $d_4$ to the base axis. This is where the thin unreachable cylinder around the base axis in Section 11.4.4 comes from.

Geometrically, Eq. (14.5.2) says that as joints 2, 3 and 4 turn, the wrist point always stays in a vertical plane perpendicular to $\hat\omega_2$ (called the arm plane below for short); this plane is always at distance $d_4$ from the base axis, so seen from above it is tangent to the circle of radius $d_4$; the wrist point must lie in this plane. Through a point outside a circle there are two tangents to it, so $\theta_1$ has two solutions (Figure 14.5.1). We call the branch of Eq. (14.5.4) with the plus sign shoulder A, and the branch with the minus sign shoulder B.

### 14.5.3 Step 2: θ₅ and θ₆

**Orientation only.** The orientation part of Eq. (14.1.1) is the product of the orientation parts of the matrix exponentials. Write $R_i = e^{[\hat\omega_i]\theta_i}$ and let $R_M$ be the orientation part of M; then $R_1R_2R_3R_4R_5R_6R_M = R_d$. Axes 2, 3 and 4 have the same direction, so their three rotations combine into one rotation through $\theta_2 + \theta_3 + \theta_4$ about that direction. Since $\theta_1$ is already known, write

$$
R_g = R_1^{\mathsf T}R_dR_M^{\mathsf T} = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega_2,\ \theta_2 + \theta_3 + \theta_4)\,R_5R_6 , \tag{14.5.5}
$$

whose left-hand side is known. Transpose both sides and apply them to $\hat\omega_2$: $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat\omega_2, \cdot)$ does not change $\hat\omega_2$, so

$$
R_5R_6\,v = \hat\omega_2, \qquad v = R_g^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_2 . \tag{14.5.6}
$$

Once again joints 2, 3 and 4 have all disappeared. Eq. (14.5.6) is Subproblem 2 applied to a direction: rotate the direction v about axes 5 and 6 (which meet at $P_{56}$) so that it becomes $\hat\omega_2$, exactly as in Example 14.3.2. Because $\hat\omega_6 = \hat\omega_2$ and $\hat\omega_5 \perp \hat\omega_2$, the result can be written very simply: write Eq. (14.5.6) as $R_6v = R_5^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_2$ and take the dot product of both sides with $\hat\omega_6$. On the left, the rotation about $\hat\omega_6$ does not change the component along it; on the right is the component of $\hat\omega_2$ along itself after it has been rotated through $-\theta_5$ about an axis perpendicular to it, which equals $\cos\theta_5$. So

$$
\cos\theta_5 = \hat\omega_2^{\mathsf T}v = \big(R_1\hat\omega_2\big)^{\mathsf T}\big(R_d\hat y\big) . \tag{14.5.7}
$$

The second equality uses $R_M^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_2 = \hat y$ (checked directly from the M given below Table 12.1.1). It has a clear geometric meaning: $R_1\hat\omega_2$ is the current direction of the axes of joints 2, 3 and 4, $R_d\hat y$ is the target flange normal, and $\theta_5$ is just the angle between these two directions. $\theta_5$ takes a positive and a negative value, and Subproblem 1 then gives $\theta_6$ from Eq. (14.5.6). Program 14.5.1 confirms that between the two solutions $\theta_5$ changes sign and $\theta_6$ differs by 180°; this is the **wrist flip**. The solution with $\theta_5 > 0$ is called wrist not flipped, and the one with $\theta_5 < 0$ wrist flipped.

When $\sin\theta_5 = 0$, the flange normal is parallel to axes 2, 3 and 4, the v in Eq. (14.5.6) lies exactly on axis 6, Subproblem 1 degenerates, and $\theta_6$ can be chosen freely as long as $\theta_2 + \theta_3 + \theta_4$ is adjusted to match. This is the wrist singularity of the UR5e, discussed further in Section 14.5.7.

### 14.5.4 Step 3: θ₃, θ₂, θ₄

With $\theta_1$, $\theta_5$ and $\theta_6$ known, move them to the right-hand side of Eq. (14.1.1):

$$
e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}\,e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}\,e^{[\mathcal S_4]\theta_4} = g, \qquad g = e^{-[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}\,T_dM^{-1}\,e^{-[\mathcal S_6]\theta_6}\,e^{-[\mathcal S_5]\theta_5} . \tag{14.5.8}
$$

The left-hand side has three parallel axes, which is exactly the planar 3R arm of Section 14.2.6, and we use the techniques of Section 14.3:

- apply both sides to the point $q_4$ on axis 4 to eliminate $\theta_4$: $e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}q_4 = g\,q_4 =: p_4$;
- take the distance to the point $q_2$ on axis 2 to eliminate $\theta_2$:

$$
\big\|e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}q_4 - q_2\big\| = \|p_4 - q_2\| =: \delta . \tag{14.5.9}
$$

This is Subproblem 3, as in Example 14.3.3, and its two solutions correspond to elbow up and elbow down. Once $\theta_3$ is known, Subproblem 1 gives $\theta_2$ from $e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}(e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}q_4) = p_4$. Finally, Subproblem 1 gives $\theta_4$ from $e^{[\mathcal S_4]\theta_4}x = e^{-[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}e^{-[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}g\,x$, where x must be chosen as a point not on axis 4. The centre $q_5$ of joint 5 in Table 12.1.1 happens to lie on axis 4 (the two differ only by $W_3$ along the axis), so it cannot be used; the program uses $q_6$.

**Theorem 14.5.1** For a given end-effector pose, if the wrist point is not on the cylinder of radius $d_4$ around the base axis, $\sin\theta_5 \neq 0$, and the two solutions of Eq. (14.5.9) do not coincide, then the inverse kinematics of the UR5e has at most 8 solutions, each of which can be found analytically from Eqs. (14.5.4), (14.5.7) and (14.5.9) and Subproblem 1.

*Proof* Any solution must satisfy Eq. (14.5.1), and hence Eq. (14.5.3), so its $\theta_1$ is one of the two values of Eq. (14.5.4). Likewise, its $\theta_5$ and $\theta_6$ must satisfy Eq. (14.5.6), which for $\sin\theta_5 \neq 0$ allows at most two pairs for each $\theta_1$; its $\theta_3$ must satisfy Eq. (14.5.9), which allows at most two values for each $(\theta_1, \theta_5, \theta_6)$; $\theta_2$ and $\theta_4$ are then fixed uniquely by Subproblem 1 (the points used are not on the corresponding axes). So there are at most 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 solutions. Conversely, every set found by the three steps satisfies Eq. (14.5.8), and hence Eq. (14.1.1).　∎

### 14.5.5 Example: the 8 solutions checked against the model

**Example 14.5.1** For the UR5e take as target pose the end-effector pose of Example 12.3.1, that is, the pose computed from $\theta^* = (30^\circ, -60^\circ, 90^\circ, -120^\circ, -90^\circ, 45^\circ)$. Find all the inverse solutions, and check each of them against the 3D model of the parts library.

**Step 1.** The flange centre is $p_d = {{ex14_5.pd}}$ m; by Eq. (14.5.1) the wrist point is $p_w = {{ex14_5.pw}}$ m, at ρ = {{ex14_5.rho:.4f}} m from the base axis, greater than $d_4$. In Eq. (14.5.4), atan2(x, −y) = {{ex14_5.base:.2f}}° and the second term is {{ex14_5.gam:.2f}}°, giving shoulder A: $\theta_1 = {{ex14_5.t1a:.2f}}^\circ$, and shoulder B: $\theta_1 = {{ex14_5.t1b:.2f}}^\circ$.

**Step 2.** Taking shoulder A as an example, $v = R_g^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_2 = {{ex14_5.v}}$, and by Eq. (14.5.7) $\cos\theta_5 = {{ex14_5.c5:.4f}}$, $\theta_5 = \pm{{ex14_5.t5a:.2f}}^\circ$: the flange normal of this target happens to be perpendicular to axes 2, 3 and 4.

**Step 3.** Taking shoulder A with the wrist not flipped as an example, $p_4 = g\,q_4 = {{ex14_5.p4}}$ m, its distance from the shoulder point $q_2$ is δ = {{ex14_5.dl:.4f}} m, and Subproblem 3 gives two values of $\theta_3$, elbow up and elbow down.

Table 14.5.1 shows several regularities. In the two solutions related by a wrist flip (for example solutions 1 and 3), $\theta_5$ changes sign and $\theta_6$ differs by 180°; unlike a spherical wrist, the UR5e's $\theta_2$, $\theta_3$ and $\theta_4$ change as well, because the wrist has offsets: once the wrist has flipped over, the centre of joint 4 is in a different place, and the planar 3R arm has to reach a new point. ### 14.5.6 Checking the two methods against each other; the number of solutions

In a 2013 technical report Kelsey P. Hawkins wrote out the complete analytical inverse solution of the UR-5 and UR-10, which has since been widely cited. He used the manufacturer's standard DH parameters (Chapter 13) and transformations frame by frame along the links. Program 14.5.1 recomputes Example 14.5.1 with his formulas (using the dimensions of the parts-library model); the DH end-effector frame differs from this book's {b} only by 90° about the x-axis (Section 12.3.3), and after converting, the 8 solutions obtained are exactly those of Table 14.5.1. One derivation uses screws and subproblems, the other DH frames and trigonometric identities; each confirms the other.

Program 14.5.1 also uses the numerical method to be discussed in Chapter 15 to solve the same inverse problem from 200 random initial values. The {{ex14_5.n_conv}} initial values that converge arrive at exactly these 8 solutions, not one more and not one fewer. This agrees with Theorem 14.5.1: the analytical solution says "at most 8", and the numerical method finds no solution beyond the eight.

Eight is the upper limit, not the rule. Program 14.5.1 generates {{ex14_5.N}} random sets of joint angles and uses the poses computed from them as targets for the inverse solution. Poses with 8 solutions make up {{ex14_5.h8:.1f}}%, with 6 solutions {{ex14_5.h6:.1f}}%, with 4 solutions {{ex14_5.h4:.1f}}% and with 2 solutions {{ex14_5.h2:.1f}}%. The reason for fewer than 8 lies in step 3: after the wrist flips, the point the planar 3R arm has to reach changes and may be outside its workspace, so that Eq. (14.5.9) has no solution; the same can happen on the shoulder-B side. All these solutions have been checked against the parts-library model, with a largest position error of ${{ex14_5.wp:sci1}}$ m. If the position and orientation of the flange are given at random in a cube around the base with x and y from −0.6 m to 0.6 m and z from −0.3 m to 0.9 m (ignoring collisions), then {{ex14_5.a0:.1f}}% of the poses have no solution at all: they are outside the workspace, or the flange cannot take that orientation there.

### 14.5.7 Which one to choose: nearest, continuous, and singular

**Nearest.** The UR controller's script function get_inverse_kin returns "the solution nearest to the given reference joint angles", and when no reference is given it takes the solution nearest the current joint angles. Suppose the robot's current joint angles are ${{ex14_5.cur}}$ (in degrees). Comparing with the 8 solutions one by one by the square root of the sum of squares of the joint-angle differences, the nearest is solution {{ex14_5.near}}, at a distance of {{ex14_5.dmin:.2f}}°; the next nearest is at a distance of {{ex14_5.d2:.1f}}°. With such a large gap there is no doubt about the choice.

**No collision.** The chosen solution must still be checked for usability. In solutions {{ex14_5.below}} of Table 14.5.1, some joint or link corner lies below the base mounting surface (below the light grey plane in Figure 14.5.2); when the robot is mounted on a worktable these configurations would hit the tabletop, and they must be rejected.

**Equivalent joint angles.** Every joint of the UR5e has a range of ±360°. Every joint angle θ in the table is within (−180°, 180°], and both θ and θ + 360° (for θ ≤ 0) or θ − 360° (for θ > 0) are within range and represent the same geometric configuration. So for the controller a geometric solution generally corresponds to $2^6 = 64$ sets of joint angles (more when some angle is exactly 0, since then both +360° and −360° can be used), and the 8 solutions correspond to about 512 sets. The comparison "nearest the current joint angles" should be made among these equivalent joint angles; otherwise the robot may turn a joint through a full revolution to reach a point only a few degrees away.

**Continuous.** When the robot moves continuously along a path, the inverse solution should change continuously along the same branch. By the implicit function theorem (Section 11.5.3), wherever the Jacobian of the end-effector pose with respect to the joint angles is invertible, each branch of solutions is a smooth function of the end-effector pose and different branches are separated from one another; two branches can meet only where the Jacobian is singular. So as long as the path does not pass through a singular configuration, the robot stays on the branch it started on, and its configuration (the three labels shoulder, elbow, wrist) does not change. Conversely, to change from one configuration to another the robot must pass through a singular configuration. The UR5e has three singularities, corresponding exactly to the three two-way choices in the three steps:

- **Shoulder singularity**: the wrist point lies on the cylinder of radius $d_4$ around the base axis, the two solutions of Eq. (14.5.4) merge, and shoulder A meets shoulder B. Program 14.5.1 moves the target of Example 14.5.1 onto this cylinder, and the number of values of $\theta_1$ drops to {{ex14_5.n_s}}.
- **Elbow singularity**: $\theta_3 = 0$, the upper arm and forearm lie in a straight line, and the two solutions of Eq. (14.5.9) merge (the same happens at $\theta_3 = 180^\circ$, fully folded back, but then the forearm has already hit the upper arm). Computing the target from $\theta^*$ with $\theta_3$ changed to 0, on the shoulder-A, wrist-flipped branch elbow up and elbow down leave only {{ex14_5.n_e}} solution.
- **Wrist singularity**: $\sin\theta_5 = 0$, wrist flipped meets wrist not flipped, and $\theta_6$ can be chosen freely. With $\theta_5$ in $\theta^*$ changed to 0, wrist flipped and not flipped merge into one, and with the program taking $\theta_6 = 0$ only {{ex14_5.n_w}} solutions are obtained on the shoulder-A branch (one elbow up and one elbow down). They are not isolated: the program also takes five arbitrary values of $\theta_6$, finds $\theta_2$, $\theta_3$ and $\theta_4$ by step 3, and reaches the target exactly each time, confirming that this is a connected family of solutions with $\theta_6$ as parameter.

Chapter 16 will start from the Jacobian and prove that the singular configurations of the UR5e are exactly these three kinds.

### 14.5.8 An everyday example

When you reach out to press a light switch on the wall, the position of your fingertip and the direction of the press are fixed, yet your arm can be placed in several ways. The elbow can be raised or let hang; the wrist can bend upwards, or the forearm can be turned over and the wrist bent downwards; and by turning your body sideways you can even reach round from the other side. Each of these is a two-way choice, and together they give several postures. A person picks a comfortable one without thinking; a robot has to compare them one by one by the rules of Section 14.5.7. The 8 solutions of the UR5e are exactly all the combinations of the three two-way choices at the shoulder, the elbow and the wrist.

The next section turns to two other common kinds of robot: the SCARA and the Delta. Their inverse solutions are much simpler than those of six-axis arms, but multiple solutions and the choice among them are still an issue.

**Engineer's notes**

- The inverse solution of the UR5e proceeds in the three steps "θ₁ → (θ₅, θ₆) → (θ₃, θ₂, θ₄)", each with two branches, and a solution is marked with three labels (shoulder, wrist, elbow). If taught points store these three labels, playback will not land in a different configuration.
- If ρ is slightly smaller than $d_4$ when finding $\theta_1$ (the target is too close to the base axis), report "unreachable: too close to the base axis" and suggest moving the workpiece further away; do not treat it as a numerical error and force a solution.
- The wrist singularity $\theta_5 = 0$ occurs when the flange normal is horizontal and parallel to the axes of joints 2–4. With the flange facing down, $\theta_5 = \pm 90^\circ$, well clear of it; watch for it when planning paths for side-on work, when the tool faces horizontally along the joint-2 axis.
- Checking the inverse solutions one by one against the parts library or a CAD model is the most reliable way to catch errors in signs, zero positions and dimensions. The program in this section uses a 3D-model algorithm completely independent of the product of exponentials, and the errors are of the order of $10^{-15}$ m; if the errors are of the order of millimetres, the likely cause is that the manufacturer's DH parameters were used while the model uses another set of dimensions (Section 12.3.3).
- After the controller has found an inverse solution, it should substitute the result back into the forward kinematics and check that the error is below a threshold (URScript's get_inverse_kin has two threshold parameters, for the position and orientation errors). This step costs almost no time but catches programming errors and numerical problems near singularities.
