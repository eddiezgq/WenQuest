### 14.3.1 The problem: twelve equations, and where to begin

Substituting Table 12.1.1 of the UR5e into Eq. (14.1.1) gives a 4×4 matrix equation: the left-hand side is the product of six matrix exponentials and M, and each matrix exponential contains sines and cosines of a joint angle. Expanded element by element, every equation contains several joint angles at once, and as products of trigonometric functions. Direct elimination offers almost no foothold, and even if we force our way through, we arrive at the high-degree polynomial described in Section 14.1.6.

The planar 2R arm of Section 14.2 suggests another route. There we did not expand the equations. Instead we first found a quantity that depends only on $\theta_2$ (the distance from the end-effector to the base) and used it to find $\theta_2$; then we turned a known point about joint 1 onto the target to find $\theta_1$. Each step had only one or two unknowns, and each had a simple geometric meaning. Can a spatial arm be handled in the same way?

In the early 1980s William Kahan, a numerical analyst at the University of California, Berkeley, gathered computations of this kind, in lecture notes for a course on computational geometry, into a few "subproblems": rotate a point about one or two axes so that it satisfies some geometric condition. The notes were never formally published. Kahan received the 1989 Turing Award for his contributions to numerical analysis (among them the IEEE 754 standard for floating-point arithmetic). Bradley Paden, also at Berkeley, working under the supervision of S. Shankar Sastry, wrote these subproblems in the language of screws and the product of exponentials and used them to solve the inverse kinematics of arms systematically, as part of his 1985 doctoral thesis. In 1994 the textbook *A Mathematical Introduction to Robotic Manipulation* by Richard M. Murray, Zexiang Li and Sastry taught the method under the name "Paden–Kahan subproblems", and from then on it has been the standard tool for finding analytical inverse solutions by the screw method.

This section first gives three invariants of a rotation and then derives the solutions of the three subproblems, illustrating each with a concrete problem on the UR5e.

### 14.3.2 Three invariants of a rotation about an axis

Let an axis of rotation have the direction of the unit vector $\hat\omega$ and pass through the point r. By Eq. (12.1.2), a rotation through θ about it takes a point p to

$$
p' = r + R\,(p - r), \qquad R = e^{[\hat\omega]\theta} . \tag{14.3.1}
$$

Written with the screw axis $\mathcal S = (\hat\omega, -\hat\omega \times r)$, this is $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$ acting on the point p (the point is written in homogeneous coordinates by appending a 1; see Section 12.1.2). For brevity, in this section we write this action simply as $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}p$.

**Lemma 14.3.1** For a rotation $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$ about the axis $(\hat\omega, r)$, any point p and any point q on the axis:

1. points on the axis do not move: $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}q = q$;
2. the distance to a point on the axis does not change: $\|e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}p - q\| = \|p - q\|$;
3. the component along the axis does not change: $\hat\omega^{\mathsf T}(e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}p - r) = \hat\omega^{\mathsf T}(p - r)$.

*Proof* (1) q lies on the axis, so $q - r = k\hat\omega$. By Rodrigues' formula (4.4.5), $[\hat\omega]\hat\omega = \hat\omega \times \hat\omega = 0$, so $R\hat\omega = \hat\omega$, and hence $r + R(q - r) = r + k\hat\omega = q$.

(2) By (1), $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}p - q = e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}p - e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}q = R(p - q)$, and a rotation preserves length: by Eq. (4.2.2), $\|Rx\|^2 = x^{\mathsf T}R^{\mathsf T}Rx = \|x\|^2$.

(3) $\hat\omega^{\mathsf T}R = (R^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega)^{\mathsf T} = \hat\omega^{\mathsf T}$, because $R^{\mathsf T} = e^{-[\hat\omega]\theta}$ also leaves $\hat\omega$ fixed. Hence $\hat\omega^{\mathsf T}R(p - r) = \hat\omega^{\mathsf T}(p - r)$.　∎

Each of the three invariants has its use. (1) is used to "eliminate" a joint: apply both sides of the equation to a point on the axis of that joint, and the joint disappears from the equation. (2) is used to eliminate the joint at the other end: take the distance to a point on some axis, and rotation about that axis no longer has any effect. (3) says that a point rotating about an axis always stays in one plane perpendicular to the axis, so the problem can be projected onto that plane and solved there. The three subproblems below are all direct applications of these three facts.

**Definition 14.3.1 (Paden–Kahan subproblems)** The following three geometric problems built from rotations are called the **Paden–Kahan subproblems**:

- Subproblem 1: given one axis of rotation and two points p and q, find θ such that $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}p = q$;
- Subproblem 2: given two axes of rotation that intersect at one point and two points p and q, find $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ such that $e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}p = q$;
- Subproblem 3: given one axis of rotation, two points p and q, and a distance δ, find θ such that $\|q - e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}p\| = \delta$.

### 14.3.3 Subproblem 1: turning a point onto a target about one axis

Figure 14.3.1(a) shows Subproblem 1. Write $u = p - r$ and $v = q - r$ for the vectors of the two points relative to a point r on the axis. By Lemma 14.3.1 (3), a rotation does not change the component along the axis, so the first condition for a solution is $\hat\omega^{\mathsf T}u = \hat\omega^{\mathsf T}v$: p and q must lie in the same plane perpendicular to the axis. Projecting u and v onto the plane perpendicular to the axis gives

$$
u' = u - \hat\omega\hat\omega^{\mathsf T}u, \qquad v' = v - \hat\omega\hat\omega^{\mathsf T}v . \tag{14.3.2}
$$

$u'$ and $v'$ are the perpendicular vectors from the axis to p and q, both starting from the same point c on the axis (in the figure, c is the point where that plane meets the axis). By Lemma 14.3.1 (2), a rotation does not change the distance to c, so the second condition is $\|u'\| = \|v'\|$: p and q are equally far from the axis. Together the two conditions read

$$
\hat\omega^{\mathsf T}u = \hat\omega^{\mathsf T}v, \qquad \|u'\| = \|v'\| . \tag{14.3.3}
$$

When both conditions hold, the problem has become a rotation in a plane: $u'$ turned through θ about $\hat\omega$ becomes $v'$. The angle of a rotation in a plane is fixed by the dot product and the cross product: $u'^{\mathsf T}v' = \|u'\|\|v'\|\cos\theta$ and $u' \times v' = \|u'\|\|v'\|\sin\theta\,\hat\omega$ (the cross product lies along the axis of rotation, with the sign matching the sense of the rotation). Hence

$$
\theta = \operatorname{atan2}\!\big(\hat\omega^{\mathsf T}(u' \times v'),\ u'^{\mathsf T}v'\big) . \tag{14.3.4}
$$

When $u' \neq 0$ (p is not on the axis) the solution is unique; when $u' = 0$, p is on the axis, every θ satisfies the equation, and there are infinitely many solutions; when condition (14.3.3) does not hold, there is no solution.

**Example 14.3.1** In the zero position, the centre of the flange of the UR5e is at ${{ex14_3.p0}}$ m. Turning only joint 1, we want to bring it to the point q on the positive y-axis at the same height and the same distance from the axis. Find the angle of joint 1.

The axis of joint 1 is the z-axis, $\hat\omega = (0, 0, 1)$, and we take r at the origin. p and q are at the same height and both are {{ex14_3.rad:.4f}} m from the z-axis, so condition (14.3.3) holds, with $q = {{ex14_3.qt}}$ m. $u'$ is the horizontal component of p, with direction angle {{ex14_3.phi_p:.2f}}°; the direction angle of $v'$ is 90°. Eq. (14.3.4) gives $\theta_1 = {{ex14_3.t1_deg:.2f}}^\circ$, that is, a clockwise turn of about 106° seen from above rather than an anticlockwise turn of 254°: atan2 automatically gives the angle in (−180°, 180°]. Program 14.3.1 substitutes this angle into the forward kinematics, and the centre of the flange does reach q; when q is raised by 0.05 m, the first part of condition (14.3.3) fails and the program reports that there is no solution.

### 14.3.4 Subproblem 2: about two intersecting axes

Subproblem 2 has two unknowns: first turn through $\theta_2$ about axis 2, then through $\theta_1$ about axis 1, taking p to q. The two axes meet at a point r, have directions $\hat\omega_1$ and $\hat\omega_2$, and are not parallel.

The key is to introduce an intermediate point. Let c be the point that p reaches after turning through $\theta_2$ about axis 2:

$$
c = e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}p = e^{-[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}q . \tag{14.3.5}
$$

The second equality comes from premultiplying both sides of the original equation by $e^{-[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}$. Eq. (14.3.5) says that c lies both on the circle traced by p turning about axis 2 and on the circle traced by q turning backwards about axis 1. Both circles lie on the sphere with centre r and radius $\|p - r\|$ (by Lemma 14.3.1 (2)), and in general they meet at two points (Figure 14.3.2). Once c has been found, $\theta_2$ and $\theta_1$ are each one instance of Subproblem 1.

::: Figure 14.3.1

caption: (a) Subproblem 1: p and q lie in the same plane perpendicular to the axis and at the same distance from it; the angle of rotation is the angle from u′ to v′. (b) Subproblem 3: p turning about the axis traces a circle; the sphere with centre q and radius δ in general meets this circle at two points p₁ and p₂
:::

To find c, write $u = p - r$, $v = q - r$ and $z = c - r$. Since the two axes are not parallel, the three vectors $\hat\omega_1$, $\hat\omega_2$ and $\hat\omega_1 \times \hat\omega_2$ are linearly independent and can serve as a basis, in which z is written

$$
z = \alpha\hat\omega_1 + \beta\hat\omega_2 + \gamma\,(\hat\omega_1 \times \hat\omega_2) . \tag{14.3.6}
$$

The three coefficients are fixed by three conditions. c lies on the circle of p about axis 2, so by Lemma 14.3.1 (3), $\hat\omega_2^{\mathsf T}z = \hat\omega_2^{\mathsf T}u$; c lies on the circle of q about axis 1, so $\hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}z = \hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}v$. Substituting Eq. (14.3.6) and noting that $\hat\omega_1 \times \hat\omega_2$ is perpendicular to both axes gives

$$
\alpha + (\hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_2)\,\beta = \hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}v, \qquad (\hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_2)\,\alpha + \beta = \hat\omega_2^{\mathsf T}u .
$$

This is a system of linear equations in α and β with determinant $1 - (\hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_2)^2 > 0$ (the axes are not parallel), and its solution is

$$
\alpha = \frac{(\hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_2)\,\hat\omega_2^{\mathsf T}u - \hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}v}{(\hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_2)^2 - 1}, \qquad
\beta = \frac{(\hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_2)\,\hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}v - \hat\omega_2^{\mathsf T}u}{(\hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_2)^2 - 1} . \tag{14.3.7}
$$

The third condition is that c lies on the sphere, $\|z\|^2 = \|u\|^2$. By Eq. (14.3.6), $\|z\|^2 = \alpha^2 + \beta^2 + 2\alpha\beta\,\hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_2 + \gamma^2\|\hat\omega_1 \times \hat\omega_2\|^2$, so

$$
\gamma^2 = \frac{\|u\|^2 - \alpha^2 - \beta^2 - 2\alpha\beta\,\hat\omega_1^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_2}{\|\hat\omega_1 \times \hat\omega_2\|^2} . \tag{14.3.8}
$$

When the right-hand side is positive, γ takes two values, one positive and one negative, giving two intermediate points $c_1$ and $c_2$ and two solutions; when it is zero, there is one solution; when it is negative, the two circles do not meet and there is no solution. In addition we must have $\|u\| = \|v\|$ (p and q equally far from the point where the axes meet), or else no rotation can take p to q. Once c has been found, Subproblem 1 gives $\theta_2$ from $e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}p = c$ and $\theta_1$ from $e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}c = q$. There is also a degenerate case: when p lies on axis 2, $\theta_2$ has no effect and may be chosen freely; when c lies on axis 1, $\theta_1$ may be chosen freely. The solutions are then no longer isolated points; the wrist singularity of Section 14.4.5 is a case of this kind.

**Example 14.3.2** The UR5e is in the zero position. The axes of joints 5 and 6 meet at the wrist point P56 ($q_6$ in Table 12.1.1), with $\hat\omega_5 = (0, 0, -1)$ and $\hat\omega_6 = (0, -1, 0)$. Turning only these two joints, we want to bring the x-axis of the end-effector frame from (1, 0, 0) in the zero position to the direction $d = {{ex14_3.d}}$. Find $\theta_5$ and $\theta_6$.

This is Subproblem 2 applied to a direction: take $p = P_{56} + (1, 0, 0)$, $q = P_{56} + d$ and r = P56, so that both points are at distance 1 from r. The two axes are perpendicular, $\hat\omega_5^{\mathsf T}\hat\omega_6 = 0$, and Eq. (14.3.7) simplifies to $\alpha = \hat\omega_5^{\mathsf T}v = {{ex14_3.a2:.4f}}$, $\beta = \hat\omega_6^{\mathsf T}u = 0$; Eq. (14.3.8) gives $\gamma^2 = {{ex14_3.g2:.4f}}$, $\gamma = \pm{{ex14_3.g:.4f}}$. The vectors of the two intermediate points relative to P56 are ${{ex14_3.c1}}$ and ${{ex14_3.c2}}$. Applying Subproblem 1 twice for each gives two solutions, $(\theta_5, \theta_6)$ = {{ex14_3.s2a}} and {{ex14_3.s2b}}. Program 14.3.1 substitutes both solutions into the forward kinematics of the UR5e, and in each case the x-axis of the end-effector points exactly along d.

The values of $\theta_5$ in the two solutions differ by 180°: joint 5 turns to the opposite side and joint 6 compensates, and the x-axis of the end-effector points in exactly the same direction. Two intersecting wrist axes always have two ways of achieving the same direction, and this is where the "wrist flip" of Sections 14.4 and 14.5 comes from.

### 14.3.5 Subproblem 3: turning about an axis to a given distance from another point

Subproblem 3 asks that p, after turning through θ about an axis, be at distance δ from the point q. By Lemma 14.3.1 (3), the rotation does not change the component of p along the axis, so the distance between p and q along the axis, $|\hat\omega^{\mathsf T}(p - q)|$, stays the same throughout. By Pythagoras' theorem, once the problem is projected onto the plane perpendicular to the axis, the distance in that plane must be

$$
\delta'^2 = \delta^2 - \big(\hat\omega^{\mathsf T}(p - q)\big)^2 . \tag{14.3.9}
$$

In this plane the rotated p lies on the circle with centre c and radius $\|u'\|$, and the projection q′ of q is at distance $\|v'\|$ from the centre ($u'$ and $v'$ mean the same as in Eq. (14.3.2); Figure 14.3.1(b)). The three sides of the triangle c, q′, p′ are known, so by the law of cosines the angle φ between $u'$ after turning through θ and $v'$ satisfies $\delta'^2 = \|u'\|^2 + \|v'\|^2 - 2\|u'\|\|v'\|\cos\phi$. Let $\theta_0$ be the angle from $u'$ to $v'$ computed by Eq. (14.3.4); then $\theta = \theta_0 \pm \phi$:

$$
\theta = \theta_0 \pm \arccos\frac{\|u'\|^2 + \|v'\|^2 - \delta'^2}{2\|u'\|\|v'\|} . \tag{14.3.10}
$$

When the argument of the arccosine lies in (−1, 1) there are two solutions, when it equals ±1 there is one, and in all other cases, as well as when $\delta'^2 < 0$, there is none. Eq. (14.3.10) requires $\|u'\|$ and $\|v'\|$ both to be non-zero: when p or q lies on the axis of rotation, the distance does not depend on θ, and the condition holds either everywhere or nowhere (Exercise 14.3.4). In a program, use the atan2 form of Lemma 14.1.1 here as well.

**Example 14.3.3** The UR5e is in the zero position. Turning only joint 3, we want the centre $q_4$ of wrist joint 4 to be at distance 0.6 m from the centre $q_2$ of shoulder joint 2. Find $\theta_3$.

The axis of joint 3 lies along −y and passes through $q_3$. From Table 12.1.1, the component of $q_4 - q_2$ along the axis is the distance by which joint 3 is set back sideways relative to joint 2, $W_2 = 0.131$ m. By Eq. (14.3.9), $\delta' = {{ex14_3.dprime:.4f}}$ m. After projection, $\|u'\| = L_2$ (the forearm) and $\|v'\| = L_1$ (the upper arm); in the zero position the two point in opposite directions, so $\theta_0 = 180^\circ$. The argument of the arccosine in Eq. (14.3.10) is {{ex14_3.cos_d:.5f}} and the arccosine is {{ex14_3.acos_d:.2f}}°, so $\theta_3 = 180^\circ \pm {{ex14_3.acos_d:.2f}}^\circ$, which reduced to (−180°, 180°] is ${{ex14_3.s3a:.2f}}^\circ$ or ${{ex14_3.s3b:.2f}}^\circ$: the elbow bends by the same angle to either side, which is exactly elbow up and elbow down. This is the same as Eq. (14.2.1), obtained for the planar 2R arm from the law of cosines, except that the sideways offset along the axis has been taken out first.

### 14.3.6 Breaking inverse kinematics into subproblems

The solutions of all three subproblems use only dot products, cross products, square roots and atan2, so by Definition 14.1.2 they are all analytical. Program 14.3.1 tests them on ten thousand sets of randomly generated data: when p and q (or δ) are made from known angles, the solutions found by each of the three subproblems include the original angles; substituting every solution found back into the original equation gives a largest residual of ${{ex14_3.worst:sci1}}$. When the data are given arbitrarily (for Subproblem 2 it is ensured only that p and q are equally far from the point where the axes meet; for Subproblem 3, δ is drawn at random between 0 and 4), Subproblem 2 has no solution in {{ex14_3.sp2_none:.1f}}% of cases and Subproblem 3 in {{ex14_3.sp3_none:.1f}}%, so the programs for both of these subproblems must be able to report correctly that there is no solution.

To find an inverse solution with subproblems, Eq. (14.1.1) has to be reduced step by step to the form of the subproblems. Two devices are commonly used, both coming from Lemma 14.3.1:

- **Eliminating the joints at the outer end**: apply both sides of the equation to the common point of the last few axes. For example, when the axes of joints 4, 5 and 6 meet at a point $p_w$, $e^{[\mathcal S_4]\theta_4}e^{[\mathcal S_5]\theta_5}e^{[\mathcal S_6]\theta_6}p_w = p_w$, and all three joints vanish from the equation at a stroke.
- **Eliminating the joints at the base end**: take the distance to a point on the first axis. For example, when the axes of joints 1 and 2 meet at $q_{12}$, $\|e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}x - q_{12}\| = \|x - q_{12}\|$, and these two joints have no effect on the distance.

The planar 2R arm is a minimal example. The distance to the base of the end-effector turned about joint 2 must equal the distance from the target to the base; this is Subproblem 3, and it gives $\theta_2$. Then the end-effector, turned through $\theta_2$, is turned about joint 1 onto the target; this is Subproblem 1, and it gives $\theta_1$. Program 14.3.1 checks with a thousand sets of random joint angles that the solutions found in this way are exactly the same as those from the formulas of Section 14.2.

Not every arm can be broken up like this. To use the two devices above, several adjacent axes must meet at one point or be parallel to one another. The next section will show that this is exactly what Pieper's criterion requires; Section 14.5 then uses the three subproblems to write out all the inverse solutions of the UR5e in full. In 2022 Alexander J. Elias and John T. Wen extended the subproblems to six (the paper was formally published in 2025), covering more kinds of arm, but the basic idea is still that of Kahan and Paden: use the invariants of rotation to break a high-dimensional equation into low-dimensional geometric problems.

### 14.3.7 Everyday examples

Opening a door is Subproblem 1. The door turns about the axis of its hinges, and the handle moves on a horizontal circle. For the handle to reach a given position, that position must be at the same height as the handle (the same component along the axis) and the same distance from the hinge axis; when these two conditions hold, the angle of the door is fixed uniquely.

A pan–tilt head for a telescope or a camera on a tripod is Subproblem 2. The head has a vertical pan axis and a horizontal tilt axis, and the two axes meet at one point. To aim at a star in the sky there is, besides the solution "pan to the bearing of the star, then tilt up to its elevation", a second one: pan a further 180°, then tilt the tube backwards over the top, so that the camera points at the same star upside down. When an amateur astronomer's equatorial mount performs a "meridian flip", it is switching from one solution to the other in exactly this way.

Drawing an arc with a pair of compasses is Subproblem 3. One leg of the compasses stays fixed and the other turns about it; to find a position on the paper at exactly δ from a given point, the turning pencil leg will in general cross the circle of radius δ centred at that point in two places.

**Engineer's notes**

- Measured data (points found by vision, axes obtained by calibration) often do not satisfy the solvability conditions of a subproblem exactly; for example, condition (14.3.3) may be off by a few tenths of a millimetre. Eq. (14.3.4) then still gives the θ that minimizes $\|e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}p - q\|$, and it can be used directly; but check the residual, since a large residual means the data are wrong.
- Where two solutions merge into one (the right-hand side of Eq. (14.3.8) is zero, or the argument of the arccosine in Eq. (14.3.10) is ±1), Subproblems 2 and 3 are very sensitive to the data, for the same reason as in Section 14.2.4. When deciding whether there is a solution, allow a tolerance, and treat a slightly negative $\gamma^2$ as zero.
- When Subproblem 1 is used to find an angle, the chosen point must not lie on the axis of rotation, or the angle cannot be determined. In a program, choose a point clearly off the axis, such as a point on another joint axis; when Section 14.5 finds $\theta_4$ for the UR5e, it deliberately avoids the centre of joint 5, which lies exactly on axis 4.
- The programs for the subproblems are short, but if a single sign convention (the direction of $\hat\omega$, the positive sense of the angle, which axis turns first) is written the wrong way round, the results are wrong. Every time you finish writing a subproblem, test it by "making data from known angles and solving back for them".
