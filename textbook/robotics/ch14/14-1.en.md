### 14.1.1 The problem: what the teach pendant gives is a pose

In the gear-reducer workshop of the WenQuest digital factory, a UR5e takes gear blanks out of a fixture. Once the vision system has found a gear blank, what it tells the controller is the pose the gripper should reach: the position of the centre of the flange in the base frame, and which way the flange faces. Take the pose of Example 12.3.1: the centre of the flange is at ${{ex14_5.pd}}$ m, and the three axes of the end-effector frame are given by a definite rotation matrix. Yet all the controller can command is six joint motors. It must answer the question: to what angle must each of the six joints turn for the flange to arrive exactly at this pose?

Chapter 12 solved the opposite problem. Given the joint angles θ, the product-of-exponentials formula (12.2.3) computes the end-effector pose T(θ) directly: six matrix exponentials are multiplied in turn and then multiplied by the zero-position pose M; the amount of computation is fixed and the answer is unique. Now θ must be found from T. This is hard in two ways. First, the equations are non-linear: the joint angles appear inside sines and cosines, and the effects of the six joints are intertwined, so they cannot be solved for one at a time. Second, the answer need not be unique, nor need it exist. When the target is somewhere the arm cannot reach, no turning of the joints will get there; when the target is within reach, there are often several arm postures that all bring the flange to the same pose. The controller must know what each of these postures is in order to pick a suitable one.

This section first makes the problem clear: what inverse kinematics is, when it has a solution, how many solutions it has, and what "solving analytically" means. The later sections give the actual methods of solution.

### 14.1.2 Posing the inverse kinematics problem

Let a serial arm have n joints, with the end-effector pose T(θ) given by the space form of the product-of-exponentials formula. Given a desired end-effector pose $T_d \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$, the subscript d standing for "desired", we seek the joint variables θ such that

$$
T(\theta) = e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}\, e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2} \cdots e^{[\mathcal S_n]\theta_n}\, M = T_d . \tag{14.1.1}
$$

**Definition 14.1.1 (Inverse kinematics)** Given an end-effector pose $T_d$, the problem of finding all the joint variables θ that satisfy Eq. (14.1.1) is called the **inverse kinematics** problem of the arm; its solutions are called inverse solutions. When only the position of the end-effector is prescribed (and not its orientation), the problem is called position-only inverse kinematics.

The definition stresses "all", and the reason will become clear in Section 14.1.4: one pose often corresponds to several sets of joint angles, and in practice they are not equivalent. Some will hit the workbench, some lie outside the joint ranges, and some are far from the robot's current posture. Finding only one of them is often not enough.

Both sides of Eq. (14.1.1) are 4×4 matrices whose last row is always (0, 0, 0, 1), so there are 12 scalar equations. But they are not independent. The position part gives 3 equations; the orientation part says that two rotation matrices are equal, and the 9 elements of a rotation matrix must satisfy the 6 conditions of $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ (Section 4.2), leaving only 3 independent quantities, so the orientation part amounts to only 3 equations. Together there are 6 independent equations, which is exactly the number of degrees of freedom of a rigid body in space (Sections 10.7 and 11.3).

Comparing the number of equations with the number of unknowns gives a rough idea of what to expect:

- When n < 6, there are fewer unknowns than equations, a general $T_d$ has no solution, and only a lower-dimensional subset of the workspace can be reached. The SCARA has only 4 joints, and so it cannot tilt the tool arbitrarily (Section 11.4.5).
- When n = 6, there are as many unknowns as equations, and in the general case the solutions are isolated points, finite in number.
- When n > 6, there are more unknowns than equations, and the solutions generally come in continuous sets. The seven-joint Panda has infinitely many solutions for one pose, forming a curve; this is the subject of redundant robots in Chapter 17.

This judgement by "counting equations and counting unknowns" is only a rule of thumb, not a theorem. With equal numbers, a solution may still not exist (the target is too far away), or the solutions may join into a continuous set at special positions (the wrist singularity of Section 14.5). We look first at the conditions for a solution to exist.

### 14.1.3 Is there a solution? Workspace and joint limits

By the definition, $T_d$ has an inverse solution if and only if it belongs to the range of the forward kinematics map, that is, to the workspace described in Section 11.4. To be precise: when only the position is required, the reachable workspace is what matters; when both position and orientation are required, the question is whether this position can be reached with this orientation. Points in the dexterous workspace always can be; points at the edge of the reachable workspace can be reached with only some orientations.

Joint limits shrink the range further. Eq. (14.1.1) itself places no restriction on θ, and its solutions may fall outside the permitted ranges of the joints. For example, of the two solutions for each leg of the Delta in Section 11.5, the one with the elbow pointing inwards lies far outside the range of rotation of the active arm. An inverse kinematics program generally works in two steps: first find all the solutions of Eq. (14.1.1) without regard to limits; then check each one against the limits, collisions and other conditions, and discard those that cannot be used. All the sections of this chapter proceed in this order.

### 14.1.4 How many solutions? Starting from the planar 2R arm

Consider first the simplest example: the planar 2R arm. Its two links have lengths $l_1 = 0.425$ m and $l_2 = 0.392$ m, the same as the upper arm and forearm of the UR5e (the same arm was used in Chapters 7 and 8). The axes of both revolute joints are perpendicular to the page; $\theta_1$ is the angle between the upper arm and the x-axis, and $\theta_2$ is the angle through which the forearm has turned relative to the extension of the upper arm; both are positive anticlockwise and measured in radians. The position of the end-effector is

$$
p(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} l_1\cos\theta_1 + l_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \\ l_1\sin\theta_1 + l_2\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \end{pmatrix} . \tag{14.1.2}
$$

Given a target point P, how many solutions does position-only inverse kinematics have? Join the three points base O, elbow joint E and end-effector P: OE = $l_1$, EP = $l_2$, and OP = r is the distance from the target to the base. As long as r satisfies the triangle inequality, a triangle can be constructed with these three sides. The triangle can be placed on either side of the segment OP: both E and its mirror image E′ in the line OP meet the requirements, so there are two solutions (Figure 14.1.1).

**Theorem 14.1.1** Let both joints of a planar 2R arm be able to turn through a full revolution, and let the distance from the target point P to the base be r. Then the number of solutions of position-only inverse kinematics is:

1. two, when $|l_1 - l_2| < r < l_1 + l_2$;
2. one, when $r = l_1 + l_2$ or $r = |l_1 - l_2| > 0$;
3. infinitely many, when $r = 0$ and $l_1 = l_2$;
4. none in all other cases ($r > l_1 + l_2$ or $r < |l_1 - l_2|$).

*Proof* The joint angles θ are a solution if and only if the elbow point E is at distance $l_1$ from O and at distance $l_2$ from P; once E is fixed, $\theta_1$ is the direction angle of OE and $\theta_2$ is the angle through which EP has turned relative to OE, and both are uniquely determined. So the number of solutions equals the number of points E in the plane that satisfy both distance conditions at once, that is, the number of intersection points of the circle with centre O and radius $l_1$ and the circle with centre P and radius $l_2$.

When r > 0, the centres of the two circles do not coincide. The two circles meet in two points if and only if $|l_1 - l_2| < r < l_1 + l_2$, and the two intersection points are symmetric about the line of centres OP; when $r = l_1 + l_2$ the circles touch externally and when $r = |l_1 - l_2|$ they touch internally, each with one point of intersection; otherwise they do not meet. When r = 0 the two circles are concentric: if the radii are equal they coincide and have infinitely many points of intersection, and if the radii are unequal they have none.　∎

Case 3 is almost never met in practice, but it reminds us that the number of inverse solutions can be infinite, even when there are as many unknowns as equations. Case 2 is the two boundaries of the ring, where the two solutions merge into one and the arm is fully straight or fully folded back. Section 11.4.3 has already pointed out that such configurations are singular configurations; Chapter 16 will show, from the point of view of the Jacobian matrix, that the two solutions meet precisely at singular configurations.

**Example 14.1.1** Four target points for the planar 2R arm: A = (0.45, 0.35) m, B on the outer boundary ($r = l_1 + l_2$), C = (0.9, 0.3) m and D = (0.02, 0) m. Find how many inverse solutions each has.

The radius of the outer boundary is $l_1 + l_2 = {{ex14_1.r_out:.3f}}$ m and that of the inner boundary is $|l_1 - l_2| = {{ex14_1.r_in:.3f}}$ m. The distance from A to the base is r = {{ex14_1.rA:.4f}} m, which lies within the ring, so A has {{ex14_1.nA}} solutions: $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ = {{ex14_1.solA_deg}} and {{ex14_1.solB_deg}}. These are exactly the two solutions that Example 7.3.4 of Chapter 7 obtained by Newton's method from two different initial values, and Program 14.1.1 checks this by both methods. B has {{ex14_1.nB}} solution, with the arm straight and pointing at B; the distance of C, {{ex14_1.rC:.4f}} m, exceeds the outer boundary, and the distance of D, 0.02 m, is less than the inner boundary, so for each of them the number of solutions is {{ex14_1.nC}}.

The two solutions at A differ visibly: in the first, $\theta_2 > 0$ and the elbow point lies below and to the right of the line OA; in the second, $\theta_2 < 0$ and the elbow point lies above and to the left of the line. When the arm is mounted upright with the base at the bottom, these two configurations look like an elbow hanging down and an elbow raised up, and so they are customarily called the **elbow up / elbow down** configurations. With the convention of this book (joint axes pointing out of the page, anticlockwise positive), when the target is above and to the right of the base, $\theta_2 > 0$ is elbow down and $\theta_2 < 0$ is elbow up. The names describe the configuration, not the sign of $\theta_2$; once the positive direction of the axis is reversed, the signs reverse with it, which is something to watch for when reading the documentation of different manufacturers.

In general, a set of joint angles is called a configuration of the arm, and the phenomenon of one end-effector pose corresponding to several configurations is called **multiple solutions**. The proof of Theorem 14.1.1 also shows that the elbow points E and E′ of the two solutions are symmetric about OP, so that $\theta_1 + \theta_1' = 2\beta$ and $\theta_2' = -\theta_2$, where $\beta = {{ex14_1.beta_deg:.2f}}^\circ$ is the direction angle of OA. Program 14.1.1 checks these two relations.

Figure 14.1.1 also shows an important fact: between elbow up and elbow down the joint angles differ greatly; for the two solutions at A, $\theta_1$ differs by about 87°. A robot cannot change from one configuration to the other while its end-effector stays still: the arm must pass through a singular configuration (straight or folded back), and this needs a special motion of its own. So once the controller has chosen a configuration, it usually keeps it for the whole of one continuous task.

### 14.1.5 Analytical and numerical solutions

There are two kinds of method for finding inverse solutions.

The first kind writes down formulas. For the planar 2R arm, Section 14.2 will write down $\theta_2$ directly from the law of cosines, and then $\theta_1$. Such formulas contain only a finite number of arithmetic operations, square roots, trigonometric functions and inverse trigonometric functions; the amount of computation is fixed, it does not depend on an initial value, and all the solutions are given at once.

**Definition 14.1.2 (Analytical solution)** If all the solutions of the inverse kinematics can be expressed as functions of the end-effector pose using a finite number of arithmetic operations, square roots, trigonometric functions and inverse trigonometric functions (including atan2), the problem is said to have an **analytical solution**, also called a closed-form solution.

The second kind iterates. Chapter 7 used Newton's method, and Chapter 8 the Gauss–Newton and LM methods, to solve the inverse kinematics of the 2R arm and the UR5e: start from an initial value and correct it a little at each step until the error is small enough. Such methods work for any mechanism and do not rely on special geometry, but they give only one solution at a time, and which one they give depends on the initial value; with a badly chosen initial value they may even fail to converge. Chapter 15 will discuss such numerical methods systematically.

Each kind of method has its uses in engineering. For arms with special geometric structure, such as the UR5e, the SCARA and most six-axis industrial robots, controllers use analytical solutions: they finish in a fixed and very short time, never get stuck because an iteration fails to converge, and can list all the configurations to choose from. For seven-axis redundant arms, real robots with calibration errors, or mechanisms whose geometry has no special structure, numerical solutions are used.

The same kind of equation turns up again and again in analytical solutions: the sine and cosine of one angle appear linearly. We met it in Section 11.5 when solving the four-bar linkage and the Delta, where Eq. (11.5.3) was given. We now set it out as a lemma and give a numerically better way of writing it; it will be needed in all the later sections.

**Lemma 14.1.1** Let A, B and C be real numbers with $\rho = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2} > 0$. The equation

$$
A\cos\theta + B\sin\theta = C \tag{14.1.3}
$$

has exactly two solutions (within one period) when $|C| < \rho$, one solution when $|C| = \rho$, and none when $|C| > \rho$. When it has solutions,

$$
\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(B, A) \pm \operatorname{atan2}\!\Big(\sqrt{A^2 + B^2 - C^2},\ C\Big) . \tag{14.1.4}
$$

*Proof* Let $A = \rho\cos\psi$ and $B = \rho\sin\psi$, that is, $\psi = \operatorname{atan2}(B, A)$. By the angle-addition formula, Eq. (14.1.3) becomes $\rho\cos(\theta - \psi) = C$, that is, $\cos(\theta - \psi) = C/\rho$. The cosine takes values in [−1, 1], so there is no solution when $|C| > \rho$. When $|C| \le \rho$, let $\gamma \in [0, \pi]$ be the angle with $\cos\gamma = C/\rho$; then $\sin\gamma = \sqrt{\rho^2 - C^2}/\rho \ge 0$, and so $\gamma = \operatorname{atan2}(\sqrt{\rho^2 - C^2}, C)$. The solutions of $\cos(\theta - \psi) = \cos\gamma$ within one period are $\theta - \psi = \pm\gamma$; when γ = 0 ($|C| = \rho$ and C > 0) or γ = π (C < 0) the two solutions coincide.　∎

Eq. (14.1.4) is mathematically the same as Eq. (11.5.3), with the second term $\arccos(C/\rho)$ replaced by $\operatorname{atan2}(\sqrt{\rho^2 - C^2}, C)$. The advantage of writing it this way shows only where the two solutions are about to merge: there $C/\rho$ is very close to ±1, the slope of the inverse cosine tends to infinity near ±1, and a little rounding error in $C/\rho$ is magnified into a large error in the angle; rounding may even make $C/\rho$ slightly greater than 1, so that the inverse cosine is simply "undefined" and the program wrongly decides there is no solution. With atan2, as long as $\rho^2 - C^2$ is computed directly from geometric quantities (Section 14.2.4 shows how), neither problem arises.

There is a third way of writing it that is more common in theoretical analysis. Let $t = \tan(\theta/2)$; then

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{2t}{1 + t^2} , \tag{14.1.5}
$$

and substituting into Eq. (14.1.3) and multiplying both sides by $1 + t^2$ gives a quadratic equation in t:

$$
(C + A)\,t^2 - 2B\,t + (C - A) = 0 . \tag{14.1.6}
$$

Its discriminant is $4(B^2 - C^2 + A^2)$, and the condition for real roots is again $|C| \le \rho$. This substitution is called the half-angle substitution; it turns a trigonometric equation into a polynomial equation. For a single angle there is nothing to be gained by it; but for a system of equations containing several angles it is the standard first step in turning the problem into polynomials and then eliminating by algebraic methods, and this line of thought is about to be used below. Program 14.1.1 checks the three forms (11.5.3), (14.1.4) and (14.1.6) on ten thousand sets of random coefficients: all three give exactly the same solutions, and the relative residual on substituting back into Eq. (14.1.3) does not exceed ${{ex14_1.trig_worst:sci1}}$.

### 14.1.6 How many solutions does a general six-axis arm have?

The planar 2R arm has at most two solutions. What about a six-axis arm? In the history of robotics this question took more than twenty years to settle completely.

In 1968 Donald L. Pieper of Stanford University, studying computer-controlled arms in his doctoral thesis, found that as long as three adjacent joint axes meet in a point, the inverse kinematics of a six-axis arm can be reduced to polynomial equations of degree at most four, and so has an analytical solution (Section 14.4). For a general 6R arm without such special structure, the problem is much harder. In 1985 the American researchers Lung-Wen Tsai and Alexander P. Morgan used polynomial continuation to find all the solutions of general 6R arms numerically; in a large number of examples they never found more than 16, and they conjectured that 16 is the upper bound. In 1986 the British researcher Eric J. F. Primrose, starting from the displacement analysis of the 7R closed chain, which is equivalent to the 6R inverse kinematics, proved that the number of solutions is at most 16; in 1988 Hong-You Lee and Chong-Gao Liang reduced the displacement analysis of the 7R closed chain to a single polynomial equation of degree 16 in one unknown. In 1989 Rachid Manseur and Keith L. Doty constructed a 6R arm that has exactly 16 real solutions for a certain pose, showing that the bound cannot be lowered. Madhusudan Raghavan and Bernard Roth of Stanford University, first in the proceedings of the Fifth International Symposium on Robotics Research published in 1990, and again in 1993 in the ASME *Journal of Mechanical Design*, obtained this degree-16 polynomial directly from the inverse kinematics equations of the 6R arm using the half-angle substitution and elimination, giving a concise elimination procedure that can be implemented in a program. Taken together, these works give the following result.

**Theorem 14.1.2 (Number of inverse solutions of a general 6R arm)** For a 6R serial arm of general geometry and a given end-effector pose, the inverse kinematics has at most 16 solutions (counting complex solutions, with multiplicity).

The proof of the theorem needs a great deal of algebraic elimination and is beyond the scope of this book; here we only explain what it means. Among the roots of a polynomial of degree 16, complex roots come in pairs, so the number of real solutions is an even number between 0 and 16 (repeated roots counted with multiplicity); only real solutions can actually be used, and they must also lie within the joint limits and be free of collisions. General polynomials of degree five and higher have no formula for their roots in radicals (the Abel–Ruffini theorem), so a general 6R arm usually has no analytical solution in the sense of Definition 14.1.2: one can only first compute the coefficients of the polynomial and then find its roots numerically, or solve Eq. (14.1.1) directly by a numerical method.

So multiple solutions are the norm for six-axis arms. To find all the configurations quickly and reliably in one go, special structure must be given to the geometry at the design stage, so that the inverse solution can be broken into a few simple steps, and this is exactly how industrial arms are designed. Sections 14.3 and 14.4 discuss this decomposition; Section 14.5 will show that the special structure of the UR5e gives it at most 8 solutions, each of which can be written as formulas.

### 14.1.7 Everyday examples

Take a folding rule of two sections, press one end on the point O of a table, and let the other end touch the point P on the table. As long as OP is shorter than the sum of the two lengths and longer than their difference, there are two ways to fold it: the hinge bulges to the left, or to the right, and the two ways are symmetric about OP. Move P outwards and the two ways of folding come closer and closer together, until when OP exactly equals the sum of the two sections the rule can only be pulled straight; any further out, and it cannot reach. This is the whole content of Theorem 14.1.1.

The human arm has multiple solutions too. Keep your hand gripping a door handle without moving it, and your elbow can be raised or lowered, with the wrist and shoulder adjusting to follow. A human arm has seven degrees of freedom from shoulder to wrist, so such postures do not come in two kinds but form a continuous family, just like the case n > 6 described in Section 14.1.2.

**Engineer's notes**

- An inverse kinematics program must report "no solution" explicitly and say which condition is not met (distance out of range, joint over its limit, collision), rather than returning NaN or a wrong set of joint angles. When tracking down a "robot can't get there" problem on site, this information is what you rely on.
- With multiple solutions, the usual rules for choosing are: nearest to the current joint angles (this is what the URScript function get_inverse_kin of the UR controller does); keep the same configuration throughout a continuous task; avoid joint limits and collisions. Write the rules into the program explicitly; do not rely on "luck".
- When the permitted range of a joint exceeds ±180° (±360° for the UR5e), each joint angle of a solution can also have 360° added or subtracted, so the controller sees far more candidate joint angles than there are geometric solutions.
- A numerical method gives whichever solution is nearest the initial value, with no guarantee that it is the configuration wanted. When a particular configuration is needed, either use the analytical solution, or check the configuration after the iteration ends and recompute from a different initial value if necessary.
- For taught points, it is best to record the joint angles as well. A program that records only poses may, when the inverse kinematics is solved again on another robot of the same model or after the tool has been changed, land in a different configuration.
