Inverse kinematics finds the joint variables from the end-effector pose. Its fundamental difference from forward kinematics is that the answer is not unique: when the target is outside the workspace there is no solution, and when it is inside there are often several, so that the same pose can be reached by arm postures of completely different shapes. The task of inverse kinematics is therefore to find all the solutions and then choose among them according to the constraints. A six-axis arm of general geometry has at most 16 solutions and requires solving a polynomial of degree 16; industrial arms, by contrast, use special geometric structures to reduce the inverse solution to a few simple steps, each of which can be written as a formula.

The method of this chapter can be summed up in one sentence: use the invariants of rotation to eliminate joints. Under a rotation about an axis, points on the axis do not move, the distance to a point on the axis does not change, and the component along the axis does not change. Applying the equation to the common point of the last few axes makes the last joints disappear; taking the distance to the common point of the first few axes removes the effect of the first joints; when several axes are parallel, the component along their direction does not depend on any of those joints. What remains is the three Paden–Kahan subproblems: rotate a point about one axis onto a target, rotate a point about two intersecting axes onto a target, and rotate a point about one axis so that its distance to another point has a given value.

The planar 2R arm is the smallest example: the distance from the end-effector to the base gives the elbow angle, with a positive and a negative value, elbow up and elbow down; rotating the end-effector onto the target then gives the shoulder angle. Pieper's criterion shows that in a six-axis arm, as long as three adjacent axes meet at one point or are parallel to one another, the inverse solution reduces to equations of degree at most four; common industrial arms have still more special structure and can be broken directly into subproblems. In a spherical wrist the three wrist axes meet at the wrist centre, so the first three joints fix the position and the last three the orientation; in the UR5e, joints 2, 3 and 4 are parallel, and the inverse solution splits into the three steps θ₁, (θ₅, θ₆), (θ₃, θ₂, θ₄), each with two branches, giving at most 8 solutions. The SCARA is a planar 2R plus a lift and a rotation, with right-handed and left-handed configurations; each leg of the Delta is one Subproblem 3.

Once all the solutions have been found, choosing among them is just as important: nearest the current joint angles, staying on the same branch throughout a continuous motion, within the joint limits, and free of collisions. Two branches of solutions meet only at singular configurations, and the three singularities of the UR5e, at the shoulder, the elbow and the wrist, correspond exactly to the three two-way choices of the inverse solution. Near a singular configuration the joint angles are extremely sensitive to the target position; this is a property of the inverse problem itself, and Chapter 16 will discuss it systematically starting from the Jacobian.

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| Inverse kinematics | Finding all the joint variables from the end-effector pose |
| Multiple solutions | Several sets of joint variables (several configurations) for the same end-effector pose |
| Elbow up / elbow down | The two configurations with the elbow point above or below the shoulder–wrist (or base–end-effector) line |
| Wrist flip | Switching the wrist joints to the other solution while the end-effector orientation stays the same |
| Analytical solution | All the solutions can be written with a finite number of algebraic operations and trigonometric functions |
| Paden–Kahan subproblems | Three basic geometric problems built from rotations; the tool of the screw method for analytical inverse solutions |
| Pieper's criterion | If three adjacent joint axes meet at one point or are parallel to one another, there is an analytical inverse solution |
| Spherical wrist, wrist centre | The three wrist axes meet at one point; this point is determined by the first three joints alone |
| Right-handed configuration, left-handed configuration | The two SCARA configurations with the elbow, seen from above, to the right or to the left of the base–target line |

### Historical notes

| Year | Who | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| 1968 | Pieper | Doctoral thesis: a six-axis arm with three adjacent joint axes meeting at one point has an analytical inverse solution |
| 1983 | Kahan | Proposed several rotation subproblems in (unpublished) lecture notes on computational geometry |
| 1985 | Paden | Doctoral thesis: systematic analytical inverse solutions by subproblems, using screws and the product of exponentials |
| 1985 | Tsai, Morgan | Found all the inverse solutions of general 6R arms by polynomial continuation; in numerical experiments there were never more than 16 |
| 1986 | Primrose | Proved via the equivalent closed 7R chain that a general 6R arm has at most 16 inverse solutions |
| 1988 | Lee, Liang | Reduced the displacement analysis of the closed 7R chain to a univariate polynomial equation of degree 16 |
| 1989 | Manseur, Doty | Constructed a 6R arm with 16 real inverse solutions |
| 1990, 1993 | Raghavan, Roth | Obtained the degree-16 polynomial by elimination directly from the inverse kinematics equations of the 6R arm, giving a practical solution algorithm |
| 1994 | Murray, Li, Sastry | Taught the method systematically in a textbook under the name "Paden–Kahan subproblems" |
| 2013 | Hawkins | Wrote out the complete analytical inverse solution of the UR-5 and UR-10 |
| 2022, 2025 | Elias, Wen | Extended the subproblems to six, covering more kinds of arm (IK-Geo) |
