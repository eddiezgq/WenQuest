### 6.5.1 The problem

Mount a tool 0.15 m long, say a glue gun, on the flange of the UR5e. When gluing, the angle of the gun often has to be adjusted while the nozzle stays on the glue line, that is, the tool has to "turn about its own tip". This requirement is simplest to write in the tool frame {t}: turn about the x axis of {t}, with the tip still, and the twist is (0.5, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0). But the robot controller wants the twist in the base frame {s}, or in the flange frame {b}. When the same motion is described in another frame, how do the six numbers change?

We already met this problem in Chapter 12, when deriving the body form of the product-of-exponentials formula: the screw axes $\mathcal S_i$ in the space frame were converted to the $\mathcal B_i$ in the end-effector frame with a 6×6 matrix called the adjoint matrix, Eqs. (12.2.6) to (12.2.8). There only the formulas were given. This section derives the matrix from the definition of the twist, studies its properties, and makes clear what changes and what does not when the frame is changed.

### 6.5.2 Derivation: from a 4×4 matrix to a 6×6 matrix

Go back first to Section 6.3. When the pose of the body is $T = T_{sb}$, the spatial twist and the body twist satisfy $[\mathcal V_s] = \dot T T^{-1}$ and $[\mathcal V_b] = T^{-1}\dot T$. From the second, $\dot T = T[\mathcal V_b]$; substituting into the first:

$$
[\mathcal V_s] = T\,[\mathcal V_b]\,T^{-1} . \tag{6.5.1}
$$

So finding $\mathcal V_s$ from $\mathcal V_b$ means computing the "sandwich" $T[\mathcal V]T^{-1}$ of 4×4 matrices. Let T = (R, p) and $\mathcal V = (\omega, v)$; by Chapter 5, $T^{-1} = (R^{\mathsf T}, -R^{\mathsf T}p)$, and by block multiplication:

$$
T[\mathcal V]T^{-1}
= \begin{pmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} [\omega] & v \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} R^{\mathsf T} & -R^{\mathsf T}p \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} R[\omega]R^{\mathsf T} & -R[\omega]R^{\mathsf T}p + Rv \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{6.5.2}
$$

For the upper-left block, Eq. (3.4.6) gives $R[\omega]R^{\mathsf T} = [R\omega]$. The upper-right block is then $-[R\omega]p + Rv = -(R\omega)\times p + Rv = p \times (R\omega) + Rv$, that is,

$$
T[\mathcal V]T^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} [R\omega] & Rv + [p]R\omega \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{6.5.3}
$$

The right-hand side is again a matrix in se(3), and the corresponding six-dimensional vector is $(R\omega,\ [p]R\omega + Rv)$. It is a linear function of $\mathcal V$ and can be written as a 6×6 matrix times $\mathcal V$.

**Definition 6.5.1 (Adjoint matrix)** For $T = (R, p) \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$, the 6×6 matrix

$$
[\mathrm{Ad}_T] = \begin{pmatrix} R & 0 \\ [p]R & R \end{pmatrix} \tag{6.5.4}
$$

is called the **adjoint matrix** of T. The map $\mathcal V \mapsto [\mathrm{Ad}_T]\mathcal V$ is called the **adjoint transformation** of T, written $\mathrm{Ad}_T(\mathcal V)$.

Eq. (6.5.3) then reads

$$
T\,[\mathcal V]\,T^{-1} = \big[\,[\mathrm{Ad}_T]\,\mathcal V\,\big] . \tag{6.5.5}
$$

These are Eqs. (12.2.6) and (12.2.7) of Chapter 12. Eq. (6.5.1) becomes $\mathcal V_s = [\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{sb}}]\mathcal V_b$. In general, for any two frames {a} and {b}, the twists of the same motion in the two frames satisfy

$$
\mathcal V_a = [\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{ab}}]\,\mathcal V_b . \tag{6.5.6}
$$

This has exactly the same form as $p_a = R_{ab}\,p_b$ for rotation matrices, and the subscript cancellation rule applies just as well: in $[\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{ab}}]\mathcal V_b$ the two adjacent b's cancel, leaving a.

The reader can check that the second row of Eq. (6.5.4) is exactly $v_s = R v_b + p \times R\omega_b$ of Eq. (6.3.9) in Section 6.3. It has a clear physical meaning: the linear velocity $v_b$ of $\mathcal V_b$ is the velocity of the origin of {b} (with components in {b}), and $Rv_b$ converts it to {s}; but the linear velocity of $\mathcal V_s$ is the velocity of the point at the origin of {s}, and the two points are p apart, so the velocity difference caused by the rotation, $p \times \omega_s$ ($= -\omega_s \times p$), must be added. The upper-right block of the adjoint matrix is zero because the angular velocity does not depend on the reference point; the lower-left block is not zero because the linear velocity does.

### 6.5.3 Properties of the adjoint matrix

**Theorem 6.5.1** For any $T, T_1, T_2 \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$:

1. $[\mathrm{Ad}_{T_1}][\mathrm{Ad}_{T_2}] = [\mathrm{Ad}_{T_1T_2}]$;
2. $[\mathrm{Ad}_T]$ is invertible, and
$$
[\mathrm{Ad}_T]^{-1} = [\mathrm{Ad}_{T^{-1}}] = \begin{pmatrix} R^{\mathsf T} & 0 \\ -R^{\mathsf T}[p] & R^{\mathsf T} \end{pmatrix} ; \tag{6.5.7}
$$
3. for any screw axis $\mathcal S$ and angle θ,
$$
e^{[\mathrm{Ad}_T\mathcal S]\theta} = T\,e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}\,T^{-1} . \tag{6.5.8}
$$

*Proof*　(1) By Eq. (6.5.5), $[[\mathrm{Ad}_{T_1}][\mathrm{Ad}_{T_2}]\mathcal V] = T_1\,(T_2[\mathcal V]T_2^{-1})\,T_1^{-1} = (T_1T_2)[\mathcal V](T_1T_2)^{-1} = [[\mathrm{Ad}_{T_1T_2}]\mathcal V]$ for every $\mathcal V$. (2) Taking $T_1 = T$ and $T_2 = T^{-1}$ in (1) gives $[\mathrm{Ad}_T][\mathrm{Ad}_{T^{-1}}] = [\mathrm{Ad}_I] = I_6$. Substituting $T^{-1} = (R^{\mathsf T}, -R^{\mathsf T}p)$ into Eq. (6.5.4) and using $[R^{\mathsf T}p]R^{\mathsf T} = R^{\mathsf T}[p]RR^{\mathsf T} = R^{\mathsf T}[p]$ (Eq. (3.4.6)) gives the right-hand side of Eq. (6.5.7). (3) By Eq. (12.2.4) of Chapter 12, $P^{-1}e^{A}P = e^{P^{-1}AP}$; taking $P = T^{-1}$ and $A = [\mathcal S]\theta$ gives $Te^{[\mathcal S]\theta}T^{-1} = e^{T[\mathcal S]T^{-1}\theta}$, and then Eq. (6.5.5) applies.　∎

Property (1) says that changing frames twice in succession is the same as changing once directly: $[\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{ab}}][\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{bc}}] = [\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{ac}}]$, subscript cancellation once again. Taking T = M in property (2) gives Eq. (12.2.8) of Chapter 12. In mathematical language, the map $T \mapsto [\mathrm{Ad}_T]$ turns multiplication in the Lie group SE(3) into multiplication of 6×6 matrices; it is called the adjoint representation of SE(3), and this is where the word "adjoint" comes from.

The geometric meaning of property (3) is the one most worth taking in. $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$ is the screw motion along the screw axis $\mathcal S$. $T e^{[\mathcal S]\theta} T^{-1}$ means: first move the body away with $T^{-1}$, screw it along $\mathcal S$, then move it back with T. The result equals a screw along another axis, and that axis is $[\mathrm{Ad}_T]\mathcal S$: the axis obtained by carrying the original axis to a new place with the rigid-body transformation T. The following theorem makes this precise.

**Theorem 6.5.2** Let the screw axis $\mathcal S$ be determined by a point q on the axis, the direction $\hat s$ and the pitch h (Definition 6.3.3), and let T = (R, p). Then $[\mathrm{Ad}_T]\mathcal S$ is the screw axis through the point Rq + p, with direction $R\hat s$ and the same pitch h.

*Proof*　By Eq. (6.3.13), $\mathcal S = (\hat s,\ -\hat s \times q + h\hat s)$. By Eq. (6.5.4), the angular-velocity part of $[\mathrm{Ad}_T]\mathcal S$ is $R\hat s$, and the linear-velocity part is

$$
p \times R\hat s + R(-\hat s\times q + h\hat s) = p \times R\hat s - (R\hat s)\times(Rq) + hR\hat s = -(R\hat s) \times (Rq + p) + h\,R\hat s ,
$$

where we have used the fact that rotation preserves the cross product (Chapter 3): $R(\hat s \times q) = (R\hat s)\times(Rq)$. Comparing with Eq. (6.3.13) gives the result.　∎

This theorem can be read in two ways, corresponding to the two uses of a rotation matrix in Chapter 4. If T is a displacement, $[\mathrm{Ad}_T]\mathcal S$ is the axis after it has been moved by T. If $T = T_{ab}$ is the relative pose of two frames and $\mathcal S$ holds the coordinates of an axis in {b}, then $[\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{ab}}]\mathcal S$ holds the coordinates of the same axis in {a}: the axis has not moved; what has moved is "the person looking at it". Either way, we have the following conclusion.

**Corollary 6.5.1** The pitch h and the angle θ do not depend on the choice of frame. The magnitude |ω| of a twist (when ω ≠ 0) also does not depend on the frame; the magnitude |v| of the linear-velocity part, however, generally does.

This explains the phenomena of Exercise 6.3.3 in Section 6.3 and Exercise 6.4.4 in Section 6.4: the pitch, the angle and the axis itself are geometric attributes of the motion, and the six components are merely their "readings" in some frame.

### 6.5.4 Examples

**Example 6.5.1** The UR5e is at its zero position, with a tool 0.15 m long mounted on the flange and sticking out along the flange normal (the y axis of {b}). The tool frame {t} has the same orientation as {b}, with its origin at the tool tip. The tool is to turn about the x axis of {t} at 0.5 rad/s, with the tip still. Find the twists in {t}, {b} and {s}.

In {t} this is a pure rotation about the origin: $\mathcal V_t = {{ex6_5.Vt}}$. The pose of {t} relative to {b} and its adjoint matrix are

$$
T_{bt} = {{ex6_5.Tbt}} , \qquad [\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{bt}}] = {{ex6_5.AdTbt}} .
$$

By Eq. (6.5.6),

$$
\mathcal V_b = [\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{bt}}]\,\mathcal V_t = {{ex6_5.Vb}} .
$$

The linear-velocity part says that the flange centre moves at 0.075 m/s in the direction $-\hat z_b$: it is 0.15 m "behind" the tip and turns about the tip at 0.5 rad/s, with speed 0.15 × 0.5 = 0.075 m/s. At the zero position the z axis of {b} points down, so seen in {s} the flange centre moves upwards. Now change to {s}, with $T_{st} = M T_{bt}$:

$$
T_{st} = {{ex6_5.Tst}} , \qquad \mathcal V_s = [\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{st}}]\,\mathcal V_t = {{ex6_5.Vs}} .
$$

The angular-velocity parts of all three twists are (0.5, 0, 0), because at the zero position the x axes of the three frames point the same way; the linear-velocity parts all differ, because they are the velocities of three different points. Check: by Eq. (6.3.2), the velocity of the tool tip (at ${{ex6_5.p_tip}}$ m) is $v_s + \omega_s \times p_{\mathrm{tip}} = 0$, so it is indeed still; the velocity of the flange centre is ${{ex6_5.v_flange}}$ m/s, vertically upwards. Program 6.5.1 also computes $\mathcal V_s$ a second time as $[\mathrm{Ad}_M]\mathcal V_b$, and lets the tool actually turn for a very short time and finds the twist by numerically differentiating the pose; the three results agree.

This is exactly how a controller carries out the command "turn about the tool tip": it first writes the twist in the tool frame, then converts it with the adjoint matrix to the frame the controller uses, and finally finds the rates of the joints with the Jacobian matrix (Chapter 16).

Finally, we answer Exercise 6.4.4 of Section 6.4. The displacement of the flange in space, $D = T(\theta)M^{-1}$, and the displacement in the body frame, $M^{-1}T(\theta)$, are related by $M^{-1}T(\theta) = M^{-1}D\,M$, so by Eq. (6.5.8) their logarithms differ by an adjoint transformation:

$$
\log\big(M^{-1}T(\theta)\big) = \big[\,[\mathrm{Ad}_{M^{-1}}]\,\mathcal S\,\big]\,\theta .
$$

So the two have the same angle ({{ex6_5.th_deg:.2f}}°) and the same pitch (${{ex6_5.h_body:.4f}}$ m/rad). They are the same screw motion, merely written in {s} and in {b} at the zero position respectively.

### 6.5.5 An everyday example

Imagine riding a merry-go-round that turns at a steady rate. To someone on the ground, the horse is simply turning about the central pole: the spatial twist has only an angular velocity and no linear velocity (the points on the central pole do not move). But the rider on the horse feels that she is "moving forward" while "turning round": her body twist has both an angular velocity and a linear velocity, and the magnitude of the linear velocity is her distance from the central pole times the angular velocity. The two accounts describe the same motion and differ by an adjoint transformation. In the same way, in a moving car the passenger says "the car is driving forward", and a person by the road says "the car is turning about the centre of the bend"; both are right.

So far we have discussed only motion. A robot must also exert forces on the outside world: tightening screws, pressing on workpieces, lifting loads. The next section introduces the six-dimensional quantity that describes forces and moments, the wrench, and explains why it "pairs" exactly with the twist.

**Engineer's notes**

- In programming, the two most common mistakes with the adjoint matrix are writing $[p]R$ as $R[p]$, and mistaking $[\mathrm{Ad}_{T^{-1}}]$ for $[\mathrm{Ad}_T]^{\mathsf T}$. The first puts the shift of the reference point in the wrong frame; the second happens to be right only when p = 0. One numerical check of Eq. (6.5.5) with a random T and $\mathcal V$ catches both mistakes.
- When the tool frame (TCP) changes, you only need to recompute $[\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{bt}}]$ and premultiply each joint's body screw axis $\mathcal B_i$ by its inverse; there is no need to derive the kinematics again.
- Because $[\mathrm{Ad}_T]$ contains [p], the farther the origin of the frame is from the motion, the larger the linear-velocity part. In numerical computation, if a twist is converted to a very distant frame and then back, rounding errors are amplified; as far as possible, compute in a frame close to the motion.
