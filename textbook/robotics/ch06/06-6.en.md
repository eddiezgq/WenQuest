### 6.6.1 The problem

The rated payload of the UR5e is 5 kg. Suppose that in the zero position it holds a 5 kg workpiece whose centre of mass lies 0.05 m from the centre of the flange along the tool direction. What torques must the joint motors give to keep the arm still? A six-axis **force/torque sensor** is mounted on the flange: what are its six readings? If joint 2 turns slowly, what power do the motors deliver?

What these questions have in common is that they describe a "system of forces" acting on a rigid body: a force, where it acts, and how large a moment it produces. The previous sections built the six-dimensional twist for motion; this section builds the corresponding six-dimensional quantity for force, called the wrench. We shall see that wrenches and twists are a pair of "partners": their dot product is exactly the power; the rule by which a wrench changes frame is fixed uniquely by the adjoint transformation of twists; and the engineering question "which forces will not make a given joint turn" comes down to whether the reciprocal product of two screws is zero. This body of theory is the core of the screw theory built by the Irish scholar Ball in the second half of the nineteenth century.

### 6.6.2 Wrenches

First recall the moment of Chapter 3. A force **f** acting at a point P has the moment **m** = **r** × **f** about a point O, where **r** is the vector from O to P. In the frame {s}, with O taken as the origin, the moment is simply $m = p \times f$, where p holds the coordinates of the point of application.

A rigid body is usually acted on by more than one force. Let the forces $f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_k$ act at the points $p_1, \ldots, p_k$. For the equilibrium and the motion of the rigid body, the effect of this set of forces is determined by just two quantities: the resultant force $f = \sum f_j$ and the resultant moment about the origin $m = \sum p_j \times f_j$ (Chapter 23 will show this from the Newton–Euler equations). We put these two three-dimensional vectors together:

**Definition 6.6.1 (Wrench)** For a system of forces acting on a rigid body, the six-dimensional vector formed by its resultant moment $m_a$ about the origin of the frame {a} and its resultant force $f_a$ (both as components in {a}),

$$
\mathcal F_a = \begin{pmatrix} m_a \\ f_a \end{pmatrix}
$$

is called the **wrench** of the force system in {a}. The moment comes first, in N·m; the force comes second, in N. Expressed in {s} it is called the **spatial wrench** $\mathcal F_s$, and expressed in the body frame {b} the **body wrench** $\mathcal F_b$.

Note that the wrench puts "the moment first", matching the twist's "angular velocity first". The reason for this ordering will appear shortly.

### 6.6.3 Power: pairing wrenches with twists

When a force f acts on a point moving with velocity $v_P$, the power is

$$
P = f \cdot v_P , \tag{6.6.1}
$$

in W (Chapter 3). Now let the rigid body move with the twist $\mathcal V = (\omega, v)$, and let the force $f_j$ act at the point $p_j$ of the body. By Eq. (6.3.2), the velocity of this point is $v + \omega \times p_j$. The total power of all the forces is

$$
P = \sum_j f_j \cdot (v + \omega \times p_j) = \Big(\sum_j f_j\Big)\cdot v + \omega \cdot \sum_j (p_j \times f_j) = f \cdot v + m \cdot \omega , \tag{6.6.2}
$$

where the middle step uses the cyclic property of the scalar triple product, $f_j \cdot (\omega \times p_j) = \omega \cdot (p_j \times f_j)$ (Eq. (3.2.13)). Eq. (6.6.2) says that the total power depends only on the wrench and the twist, and is exactly the dot product of the two six-dimensional vectors:

$$
P = \mathcal F^{\mathsf T}\mathcal V = m\cdot\omega + f\cdot v , \tag{6.6.3}
$$

provided that both are expressed in the same frame, and that the moment and the linear velocity both refer to the origin of that frame. This is why the wrench puts "the moment first": multiplied term by term with the twist, which puts "the angular velocity first", the moment pairs with the angular velocity and the force with the linear velocity.

Power is a physical quantity and does not depend on the frame in which it is computed. So for any two frames {a} and {b}, $\mathcal F_a^{\mathsf T}\mathcal V_a = \mathcal F_b^{\mathsf T}\mathcal V_b$. Substituting $\mathcal V_a = [\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{ab}}]\mathcal V_b$ (Eq. (6.5.6)) gives $\mathcal F_a^{\mathsf T}[\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{ab}}]\mathcal V_b = \mathcal F_b^{\mathsf T}\mathcal V_b$. This holds for every $\mathcal V_b$, and therefore:

**Theorem 6.6.1** The wrenches of the same force system in {a} and {b} satisfy

$$
\mathcal F_b = [\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{ab}}]^{\mathsf T}\,\mathcal F_a . \tag{6.6.4}
$$

With $T_{ab} = (R, p)$, writing this out by Eq. (6.5.4) gives

$$
f_b = R^{\mathsf T}f_a , \qquad m_b = R^{\mathsf T}\big(m_a - p \times f_a\big) . \tag{6.6.5}
$$

The second equation has a clear mechanical meaning: $m_a - p \times f_a$ is the moment of the force system about the origin of {b} (located at p in {a}), which is exactly the rule of statics for "how the moment changes with the reference point"; multiplying by $R^{\mathsf T}$ then turns it into components in {b}. Note that a twist is taken from {b} to {a} by $[\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{ab}}]$, whereas a wrench is taken from {a} to {b} by its transpose, in the opposite direction. Mathematically, wrenches belong to the "dual space" of twists, and the transpose of the adjoint transformation is exactly the transformation rule in the dual space.

### 6.6.4 Example: joint torques and sensor readings when holding a load

**Example 6.6.1** In the zero position the UR5e holds a 5 kg load whose centre of mass lies at (0, 0.05, 0) m in {b}, that is, 0.05 m from the centre of the flange along the tool direction. Counting only the weight of the load and ignoring the arm's own weight, and taking 𝔤 = 9.81 m/s², find the wrench the robot applies to the load and the static torque needed at each joint.

The position of the load's centre of mass in {s} is $c = M(0, 0.05, 0, 1)^{\mathsf T} = {{ex6_6.c}}$ m. To hold the load, the robot must apply to it a force pointing vertically upwards, of magnitude m𝔤 = {{ex6_6.mg:.2f}} N. This force acts at the centre of mass, and its moment about the origin of {s} is c × f, so

$$
\mathcal F_s = \begin{pmatrix} c \times f \\ f \end{pmatrix} = {{ex6_6.F}} .
$$

How do we find the joint torques? Imagine that joint i turns through a very small angle δθ while the other joints stay still (such an "imagined displacement" is called a virtual displacement). The part of the robot beyond joint i moves with the twist $\mathcal S_i\,\delta\theta$, and the work the robot does on the load is $\mathcal F_s^{\mathsf T}\mathcal S_i\,\delta\theta$; this work is supplied by the motor of joint i and equals $\tau_i\,\delta\theta$. When the arm is at rest the two must be equal (this is the **principle of virtual work**), and so

$$
\tau_i = \mathcal S_i^{\mathsf T}\,\mathcal F_s . \tag{6.6.6}
$$

Here $\mathcal S_i$ is the screw axis of joint i at this moment; in the zero position it is the value in Table 12.1.1. When the arm is not in the zero position, $\mathcal S_i$ must be replaced by the screw axis in the current configuration; these screw axes form the Jacobian matrix of Chapter 16, and Eq. (6.6.6) becomes $\tau = J^{\mathsf T}\mathcal F$. Substituting the six screw axes of Table 12.1.1 gives

$$
\tau = {{ex6_6.tau}}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} .
$$

The result is easy to explain geometrically (Figure 6.6.1). Since $\mathcal S_i = (\hat\omega_i, -\hat\omega_i \times q_i)$, Eq. (6.6.6) can be written $\tau_i = \hat\omega_i \cdot ((c - q_i) \times f)$, that is, "the moment of the force about the joint axis". The axes of joints 2 and 3 are horizontal and the force is vertical, so the lever arm is the horizontal distance from the centre of mass to the axis: $\tau_2 = m\mathfrak g \times (-0.817)$ m = ${{ex6_6.tau2:.2f}}$ N·m, $\tau_3 = m\mathfrak g \times (-0.817 + 0.425)$ m = ${{ex6_6.tau3:.2f}}$ N·m. The minus sign means that the motor must push in the direction of decreasing joint angle, that is, lift the arm (by Chapter 12, the arm rises when the angle of joint 2 decreases). The axis of joint 4 lies directly above the centre of mass, so the lever arm is zero and $\tau_4 = 0$. The axes of joints 1 and 5 are vertical, parallel to the vertical force, and need no torque either; the axis of joint 6 runs along the tool direction and passes right through the centre of mass, so its lever arm is zero and its torque is zero as well.

Program 6.6.1 checks these six values independently with another form of the principle of virtual work: the gravitational potential energy of the load is $U = m\mathfrak g\,z_c(\theta)$, and the joint torque needed to hold the load equals $\partial U/\partial\theta_i$. Computing the height of the centre of mass $z_c(\theta)$ with the product-of-exponentials formula and differentiating numerically gives the same six numbers as Eq. (6.6.6).

**Example 6.6.2** Find the readings of the force/torque sensor on the flange, and the power with which the robot does work on the load when joint 2 turns at 30°/s.

The sensor measures the wrench that the load exerts on the flange, which is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the wrench the robot applies to the load: $-\mathcal F_s$. The sensor gives its components in {b}; by Eq. (6.6.4),

$$
\mathcal F_b = [\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{sb}}]^{\mathsf T}(-\mathcal F_s) = {{ex6_6.Fb}} .
$$

Check directly with Eq. (6.6.5): the z axis of {b} points downwards, so the weight in {b} is ${{ex6_6.fb}}$ N; the centre of mass lies at (0, 0.05, 0) m in {b}, and the moment is $m_b = (0, 0.05, 0) \times f_b = {{ex6_6.mb}}$ N·m. The two calculations agree. In engineering practice, exactly this relation is used in reverse to find the mass and the centre of mass of an unknown load from the sensor readings (Exercise 6.6.4).

When joint 2 turns at $\dot\theta_2 = \pi/6$ rad/s, by Example 6.3.1, $\mathcal V_s = {{ex6_6.Vs}}$ and $\mathcal V_b = {{ex6_6.Vb}}$. The power is

$$
P = \mathcal F_s^{\mathsf T}\mathcal V_s = \mathcal F_b'^{\mathsf T}\mathcal V_b = \tau_2\,\dot\theta_2 \approx {{ex6_6.P:.2f}}\ \mathrm{W} ,
$$

where $\mathcal F_b' = [\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{sb}}]^{\mathsf T}\mathcal F_s$ holds the components in {b} of the wrench applied by the robot. The three calculations give the same value, confirming that power does not depend on the frame. The power is negative: when joint 2 turns in the positive direction the arm descends, the weight of the load does work on the robot, and the motor is "braking".

### 6.6.5 A wrench is a screw too: Poinsot's central axis

Section 6.3 said that every twist is "a rotation about some axis plus a translation along that axis". Wrenches have an exactly corresponding result, which was obtained from statics at the beginning of the nineteenth century, about forty years after Mozzi's instantaneous screw axis and more than twenty years before Chasles' theorem. In his *Éléments de statique* (Elements of statics), published in 1803, the French mechanician Louis Poinsot introduced the concept of the **couple** (a pair of equal and opposite forces that do not lie on the same line, which produces only a moment and no resultant force), and proved that every force system can be reduced to a resultant force plus a couple; in his 1806 paper on the composition of moments he went on to discuss how the couple changes when the reference point is changed. From this, the resultant force can be moved onto one particular line such that the remaining couple moment is parallel to the resultant. This line is called the **central axis** of the force system.

**Theorem 6.6.2 (Central axis)** Let $\mathcal F = (m, f)$ be a wrench with f ≠ 0. Then there is a unique line L in the direction of f such that the force system is equivalent to the force f acting along L plus a couple moment hf about L, that is,

$$
\mathcal F = \begin{pmatrix} q \times f + h f \\ f \end{pmatrix} , \tag{6.6.7}
$$

where

$$
h = \frac{m \cdot f}{|f|^2} , \tag{6.6.8}
$$

$$
q = \frac{f \times m}{|f|^2} . \tag{6.6.9}
$$

The proof is exactly the same as that of Theorem 6.3.2, with ω replaced by f and v by m, noting that the sign of the moment $q \times f = -f \times q$ agrees with that of $-\omega \times q$. h is called the pitch of the wrench, in m. When h = 0 the force system is equivalent to a single force along L; when f = 0 only a couple is left, which corresponds to an infinite pitch. Comparing Eq. (6.6.7) with Eq. (6.3.10) shows that a wrench and a twist are the same geometric object, a line with a pitch, multiplied by different "intensities": a twist by a rate of rotation, a wrench by a magnitude of force. This is the original meaning of the word "screw".

**Example 6.6.3** Two forces act on a part: $f_1 = (0, 0, -10)$ N at (0.2, 0, 0) m and $f_2 = (0, 10, 0)$ N at (0, 0, 0.3) m. The two forces are neither parallel nor intersecting, so they cannot be combined into a single force. Find the central axis and the pitch of this force system.

The resultant force is $f = {{ex6_6.fR}}$ N, and the moment about the origin is $m = p_1 \times f_1 + p_2 \times f_2 = {{ex6_6.mR}}$ N·m. By Eqs. (6.6.8) and (6.6.9), the pitch is h = {{ex6_6.hw:.1f}} m, and the central axis passes through the point $q = {{ex6_6.qw}}$ m in the direction of f. In other words, the effect of these two forces equals a force of magnitude $10\sqrt2$ N acting along the central axis together with a couple of {{ex6_6.m_central:.4f}} N·m about the same axis, like pushing and twisting a screwdriver at the same time (Figure 6.6.2).

### 6.6.6 The reciprocal product and reciprocal screws

The power in Eq. (6.6.3) can also be written in purely geometric language. If we write the wrench too in the order "direction first", as $\hat{\mathcal F} = (f, m)$, in the same way as the twist $\mathcal V = (\omega, v)$, then the power $m\cdot\omega + f\cdot v$ is a special case of the following operation.

**Definition 6.6.2 (Reciprocal product)** The **reciprocal product** of two screws $\mathcal S_1 = (s_1, v_1)$ and $\mathcal S_2 = (s_2, v_2)$ (the first three components being the direction and the last three the moment) is defined as

$$
\mathcal S_1 \circ \mathcal S_2 = s_1 \cdot v_2 + s_2 \cdot v_1 . \tag{6.6.10}
$$

If $\mathcal S_1 \circ \mathcal S_2 = 0$, the two are called **reciprocal screws**.

Thus the power is $P = \mathcal V \circ \hat{\mathcal F}$. The reciprocal product does not depend on the frame, because it is simply power. It also has a geometric expression that depends only on the relative position of the two axes.

**Theorem 6.6.3** Let $\mathcal S_1$ and $\mathcal S_2$ be unit screw axes (with unit direction vectors) of pitches $h_1$ and $h_2$, let d be the length of the common perpendicular of the two axes, let $\hat n$ be the unit vector along the common perpendicular pointing from axis 1 to axis 2, and let α be the angle from $\hat s_1$ to $\hat s_2$ measured about $\hat n$ by the right-hand rule. Then

$$
\mathcal S_1 \circ \mathcal S_2 = (h_1 + h_2)\cos\alpha - d\,\sin\alpha . \tag{6.6.11}
$$

*Proof*　Take the two feet of the common perpendicular, $q_1$ and $q_2$, as the points on the two axes, so that $q_2 - q_1 = d\,\hat n$. By Eq. (6.3.13), $v_i = q_i \times \hat s_i + h_i\hat s_i$. Substituting into Eq. (6.6.10) gives

$$
\hat s_1 \cdot (q_2 \times \hat s_2) + \hat s_2 \cdot (q_1 \times \hat s_1) + (h_1 + h_2)\,\hat s_1\cdot\hat s_2 .
$$

By the cyclic property of the scalar triple product, the sum of the first two terms is $q_2 \cdot (\hat s_2 \times \hat s_1) + q_1 \cdot (\hat s_1 \times \hat s_2) = -(q_2 - q_1)\cdot(\hat s_1 \times \hat s_2)$. And $\hat s_1 \times \hat s_2 = \sin\alpha\,\hat n$, $\hat s_1\cdot\hat s_2 = \cos\alpha$, which gives Eq. (6.6.11).　∎

Ball called half the right-hand side of Eq. (6.6.11) the **virtual coefficient** of the two screws. Program 6.6.1 checks Eq. (6.6.11) with randomly generated screws.

This formula at once answers one of the engineering questions raised at the start of this section: **what kind of force will not make a revolute joint turn?** The screw axis of a revolute joint has zero pitch, and so does the wrench of a single force. By Eq. (6.6.11), their reciprocal product is −d sin α, which is zero if and only if d = 0 or sin α = 0, that is, if the line of action of the force intersects the joint axis or is parallel to it. In Example 6.6.1 the axes of joints 1 and 5 are parallel to the vertical force, and the axes of joints 4 and 6 intersect the line of action of the force, so none of these four joints carries a torque, in agreement with the calculation. This result is used again and again in mechanism design: a revolute joint can only "resist" wrenches that are reciprocal to its axis, and how to place the fingers of a gripper so that it holds a workpiece firmly, or how to analyse the constraints of a parallel mechanism, all come down to finding reciprocal screws (Chapters 18 and 33).

### 6.6.7 Background and history: Ball's theory of screws

Uniting motion and force under the single geometric object of the "screw" was the work of the Irish astronomer and mathematician Robert Stawell Ball (1840–1913). While he was Royal Astronomer of Ireland in Dublin, he studied how a constrained rigid body moves under small forces, and in 1876 published *The Theory of Screws: A Study in the Dynamics of a Rigid Body*; he kept extending it for more than twenty years, and in 1900 published in Cambridge the comprehensive *A Treatise on the Theory of Screws*. The book introduced the concepts of the twist, the wrench, reciprocal screws, the virtual coefficient and others, and most of the definitions and theorems of this section can be traced back to it.

Ball's work built on that of his predecessors: the screw motions of Mozzi (1763) and Chasles (1830); the couple and the central axis of Poinsot (1803, 1806); and the line coordinates proposed in 1865 by the German mathematician Julius Plücker, which describe a line in space by six numbers and are essentially the same as the way this book writes screw axes. At about the same time, the English mathematician William Kingdon Clifford gave an algebraic representation of screws by "biquaternions" in 1873, and the German mathematician Eduard Study developed this method systematically with dual numbers in 1903.

Ball's theory attracted little attention for a time in the first half of the twentieth century, and was taken up again in the middle of the century with the development of spatial mechanism theory. From the 1980s on, Brockett connected screws with Lie groups and proposed the product-of-exponentials formula (Chapter 12), and the textbooks of Murray, Li and Sastry and of Lynch and Park made it the standard language of robotics. In taking it as its main thread, this book carries on that line.

### 6.6.8 An everyday example

When you turn a bolt with a spanner, the force is applied at the end of the spanner, and the longer the lever arm, the less effort it takes: this is the moment of the force about the axis of the bolt. If the force is aimed along the axis of the bolt (intersecting the axis) or parallel to it, the bolt will not turn however hard you push: the wrench is reciprocal to the bolt's rotation twist. Pushing a door near its handle is easy and pushing near its hinges is hard, for the same reason. Turning a screwdriver, on the other hand, applies a wrench with a non-zero pitch: the hand pushes along the axis and twists about it at the same time, so a thrust and a torque act on the same axis together.

Twists and wrenches have both been established. The last section studies an operation between twists, the Lie bracket. It captures the difference between "motion A followed by motion B" and "B followed by A", and is an indispensable tool in dynamics and in the control of nonholonomic systems.

**Engineer's notes**

- The readings of a six-axis force/torque sensor are given in the sensor's own frame, with the moment taken about the centre of the sensor. To convert them to the tool centre point or the base frame, use Eq. (6.6.5); do not just rotate and forget the term $-p \times f$, through which the moment changes with the reference point.
- Payload identification: the robot stops in several different configurations and reads the sensor's force and moment; the mass is found from $f_b = R^{\mathsf T}(0, 0, -m\mathfrak g)$, and the centre-of-mass position $r_b$ from $m_b = r_b \times f_b$ by least squares. The "payload setting" and the collision detection of collaborative robots both rely on this step.
- When selecting a robot, the rated payload must always be read together with the position of the centre of mass. With the same 5 kg, moving the centre of mass 0.1 m further from the flange horizontally increases the static torques of joints 2 and 3 by about 5 N·m each, while the torque of a wrist joint may go from zero to about 5 N·m, and the wrist motors are much smaller than the shoulder motors.
