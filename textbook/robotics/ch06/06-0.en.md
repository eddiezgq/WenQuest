title: Chapter Overview
---

Chapter 4 studied the rotation of a rigid body, and Chapter 5 put rotation and translation together, describing the pose of a rigid body by a homogeneous transformation. But robotics is concerned with far more than "where it is": when an arm moves, how fast does its end-effector turn and move, and about and along which axis; when the end-effector applies a force, how are the force and the moment passed on to the joints; between two poses, what is the most direct way to move? Answering these questions calls for a language that unifies rotation and translation, motion and force. The screw theory introduced in this chapter, together with the Lie groups and Lie algebras behind it, is that language, and it is also the common foundation for the kinematics, dynamics and control in the rest of the book.

The chapter follows a line that is both historical and logical:

- Section 6.1 starts from Euler's rotation theorem and proves Chasles' theorem: every rigid-body displacement is a screw motion about some axis, that is, a rotation about the axis together with a translation along it;
- Section 6.2 obtains the angular velocity from the derivative of the rotation matrix, introduces the Lie group SO(3) and its Lie algebra so(3), and explains that "orientations live in the Lie group, angular velocities in the Lie algebra";
- Section 6.3 applies the same ideas to general rigid-body motion, arrives at the six-dimensional twist and se(3), and proves Mozzi's theorem: at every instant a rigid body is performing a screw motion;
- Section 6.4 derives the exponential map and the logarithm map, which give the displacement from a screw and the screw from a displacement, and gives the closed form of the matrix exponential used by the product-of-exponentials formula of Chapter 12;
- Section 6.5 studies the adjoint transformation: when the same motion is described in another frame, how its six components change and what stays the same;
- Section 6.6 introduces the wrench, proves that its dot product with a twist is power, and from this obtains the transformation rule for wrenches, the method of computing static joint torques and the concept of reciprocal screws; it also presents Ball's theory of screws;
- Section 6.7 (advanced) introduces the Lie bracket and explains the net effect of two motions carried out alternately.

The chapter uses the cross product, skew-symmetric matrices, the vector triple product formula and angular velocity from Chapter 3, the rotation matrix, Euler's theorem, Rodrigues' formula and the matrix exponential from Chapter 4, and the homogeneous transformation from Chapter 5. Most of its examples use the UR5e, with the joint screw axes of Table 12.1.1 in Chapter 12; readers who have not yet studied Chapter 12 need only treat them as given data. The results of this chapter are used again and again later: the product-of-exponentials formula of Chapter 12 follows from the exponential map and the adjoint transformation of this chapter, the Jacobian of Chapter 16 is made up of twists, the trajectory planning of Chapter 22 uses screw interpolation, the Newton–Euler recursion of Chapter 25 rests on twists, wrenches and the Lie bracket, and the force control of Chapter 45 takes the wrench as its basic quantity.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. find, from two poses, the axis, angle and pitch of the screw motion that connects them, and explain the pole of a planar displacement;
2. find the spatial angular velocity and the body angular velocity from a rotation matrix and its derivative, and explain why so(3) is the Lie algebra of SO(3);
3. find the spatial twist and the body twist from a pose and its derivative, and locate the instantaneous screw axis;
4. derive and compute the exponential map and the logarithm map of SE(3), and use them for screw interpolation;
5. transform twists between frames with the adjoint matrix, and transform wrenches with its transpose;
6. compute static joint torques with τ = SᵀF, and use the reciprocal product to decide whether a force can drive a joint;
7. compute Lie brackets, and use them to estimate the net effect of alternating motions.
