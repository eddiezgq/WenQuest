The main thread of this chapter can be summed up in one sentence: **the motion of a rigid body is a screw motion, and both motion and force are screws**.

We started from Chasles' theorem. Every rigid-body displacement, however complicated the path, is equivalent to turning through some angle about some axis while translating some distance along that axis. The axis, the angle and the pitch are geometric properties of the displacement itself and do not depend on the frame. In the plane, this axis degenerates into a pole.

Next we studied velocity. The derivative of a rotation matrix can always be written as $\dot R = [\omega_s]R = R[\omega_b]$, so the same angular velocity has two sets of components, in the space frame and in the body frame; the set of all skew-symmetric matrices so(3) is the tangent space of SO(3) at the identity, that is, its Lie algebra. Applying the same derivation to homogeneous transformations, $\dot T T^{-1}$ and $T^{-1}\dot T$ give the spatial and body twists (ω, v), which belong to se(3). Mozzi's theorem says that at every instant a rigid body performs a screw motion about an instantaneous screw axis; every twist can be written as a screw axis times a rate of rotation. Poses are matrices in the Lie group SE(3) and can only be multiplied; twists are vectors in the Lie algebra and can be added.

The exponential map connects the two: moving with a constant twist, the pose changes as $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$, which has a closed form containing G(θ) and is geometrically a turn of θ along the screw axis. The logarithm map goes the other way, finding the screw from a displacement, and proves Chasles' theorem again by algebra. Under a change of frame, a twist transforms by the adjoint matrix; the axis is carried along, while the pitch and the angle stay the same.

The wrench (m, f) pairs with the twist, and the dot product of the two is power. The invariance of power gives at once the transformation rule of wrenches (the transpose of the adjoint matrix) and the static joint torques τ = SᵀF. Like a motion, a force system has a central axis and a pitch; when the reciprocal product of two screws is zero, a wrench does no work on a motion, and so a revolute joint cannot be driven by a force coplanar with its axis. These are the core of Ball's theory of screws. Finally, the Lie bracket captures the extent to which motions fail to commute: the net effect of two alternating motions is, to second order, their Lie bracket.

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| Screw motion | Turning about an axis while translating along it; pitch = distance advanced per radian turned |
| Chasles' theorem | Every rigid-body displacement is a screw motion |
| Pole | The unique fixed point of a planar displacement; the displacement is a single turn about it |
| Spatial and body angular velocity | The components of the same angular velocity vector in {s} and in {b} |
| Lie group and Lie algebra | A Lie group is a smooth group of matrices (SO(3), SE(3)); its Lie algebra is its tangent space at the identity (so(3), se(3)), equipped with the Lie bracket |
| Twist | The six-dimensional description (ω, v) of the velocity of a rigid body, where v is the velocity of the point of the body that is at the origin at this instant |
| Instantaneous screw axis | The line formed by the points whose velocity is parallel to ω (Mozzi's theorem) |
| Screw axis | A line with a pitch, written as a six-dimensional vector; twist = screw axis × rate of rotation |
| Exponential coordinates Sθ | The displacement reached by moving for 1 s with the constant twist Sθ |
| Adjoint matrix | The 6×6 matrix that takes a twist from one frame to another; its transpose transforms wrenches |
| Wrench | The six-dimensional description (m, f) of a force system, moment first |
| Central axis | The line along which a force system is equivalent to a force along it plus a couple about it (Poinsot) |
| Reciprocal screws | Two screws whose reciprocal product is zero: the corresponding wrench does no work on the twist |
| Lie bracket | The direction of the net effect of two alternating motions; in so(3) it is the cross product |

### Historical notes

| Year | Person | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| 1763 | Giulio Mozzi | Proved that the instantaneous motion of a rigid body is a screw motion about an axis (the instantaneous screw axis) |
| 1775 | Leonhard Euler | Proved that a motion of a rigid body with one fixed point is a rotation about an axis (Chapter 4) |
| 1803, 1806 | Louis Poinsot | Introduced the couple and proved that a force system reduces to a force plus a couple; the central axis |
| 1830 | Michel Chasles | Pointed out that every finite rigid-body displacement is a screw motion (Olinde Rodrigues gave an analytic proof in 1840) |
| 1865 | Julius Plücker | Described lines in space by six coordinates |
| 1870s | Sophus Lie | Studied continuous groups of transformations and found that their structure is determined by the "infinitesimal transformations" near the identity |
| 1873 | William Kingdon Clifford | Biquaternions: an algebraic representation of screws |
| 1876, 1900 | Robert Stawell Ball | *The Theory of Screws* and *A Treatise on the Theory of Screws* set out a systematic theory of twists, wrenches and reciprocal screws |
| 1897–1906 | John Edward Campbell, Henry Frederick Baker, Felix Hausdorff | Showed that the exponent of a product of two exponentials is given by a series of Lie brackets |
| 1903 | Eduard Study | Developed the algebra of screws with dual numbers |
| 1930s | Hermann Weyl | Introduced the name "Lie algebra" |
| 1938, 1939 | Petr Konstantinovich Rashevsky, Wei-Liang Chow | Decided the reachability of nonholonomic systems by Lie brackets |
| 1984 | Roger W. Brockett | Applied screws and Lie groups to robot arms and proposed the product-of-exponentials formula (Chapter 12) |
| 1994, 2017 | Richard M. Murray, Zexiang Li, S. Shankar Sastry; Kevin M. Lynch, Frank C. Park | Robotics textbooks built around screws and Lie groups |
