### 6.3.1 The problem

The controller of the UR5e reads the speeds of the six joints every 2 ms. To make the tool on the end-effector move at constant speed along a weld seam, or, when driving a screw, to "turn once and advance one lead", the controller must know how the whole end-effector is moving at this instant. The end-effector is a rigid body, and its motion is more than the velocity of a single point: it is turning, about some axis and at some rate; and it is translating, in some direction and at some speed. How many numbers does it take to describe the velocity of a rigid body? And what is the most natural way to organize them?

The previous section answered "how fast it turns, and about which direction": the angular velocity **ω**, three numbers. In this section we add the translational part and obtain a six-dimensional quantity that describes the velocity of a rigid body, called the twist. We shall also see that at every instant the motion of a rigid body is a screw motion; this is the "instantaneous version" of Chasles' theorem of Section 6.1, and it was first proved by Mozzi in 1763.

### 6.3.2 The velocities of the points of a rigid body

Let the body frame {b} be fixed to the rigid body, with pose $T(t) = \begin{pmatrix} R(t) & p(t) \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ relative to {s}. The coordinates $x_b$ of a point of the body in {b} do not change with time, and its coordinates in {s} are $x = R\,x_b + p$. Differentiating with respect to time, $\dot x = \dot R\,x_b + \dot p$. By Eq. (6.2.3), $\dot R = [\omega_s]R$, and $R\,x_b = x - p$, so

$$
\dot x = \dot p + \omega_s \times (x - p) . \tag{6.3.1}
$$

This generalizes **v** = **ω** × **r** of Chapter 3: the velocity of a point of a rigid body equals the velocity of the reference point p plus the velocity caused by the rotation about the reference point. Eq. (6.3.1) involves one special point of the body, p, the origin of {b}. Can we do without this special point? Collect the terms of Eq. (6.3.1) that do not depend on x:

$$
\dot x = v_s + \omega_s \times x , \tag{6.3.2}
$$

$$
v_s = \dot p - \omega_s \times p . \tag{6.3.3}
$$

$v_s$ has a concrete physical meaning: setting x = 0 in Eq. (6.3.2) gives $\dot x = v_s$. In other words, **$v_s$ is the velocity of the point of the body that happens to be at the origin of {s} at this instant**. This point may not lie on the physical body at all; in that case, imagine the body extended to an infinite space rigidly attached to it.

Eq. (6.3.2) shows that once the six numbers $\omega_s$ and $v_s$ are known, the velocity of every point of the body can be calculated. These six numbers do not depend on where {b} is chosen; they depend only on {s}.

### 6.3.3 Twists and se(3)

These six numbers can also be obtained directly from the pose matrix, just as the previous section obtained $[\omega_s]$ from $\dot R R^{\mathsf T}$. By Chapter 5, $T^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} R^{\mathsf T} & -R^{\mathsf T}p \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $\dot T = \begin{pmatrix} \dot R & \dot p \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Multiplying:

$$
\dot T\,T^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} \dot R R^{\mathsf T} & \dot p - \dot R R^{\mathsf T}p \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} [\omega_s] & v_s \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{6.3.4}
$$

The upper-right block is exactly Eq. (6.3.3). So $\dot T T^{-1}$ holds precisely $\omega_s$ and $v_s$, in just the same way as $\dot R R^{\mathsf T} = [\omega_s]$ in the previous section. Arrange the six numbers in a column:

$$
\mathcal V_s = \begin{pmatrix} \omega_s \\ v_s \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^6 . \tag{6.3.5}
$$

Conversely, from a six-dimensional vector $\mathcal V = (\omega, v)$ construct the 4×4 matrix

$$
[\mathcal V] = \begin{pmatrix} [\omega] & v \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{6.3.6}
$$

so that Eq. (6.3.4) reads $\dot T T^{-1} = [\mathcal V_s]$. The notation $[\cdot]$ means, as in three dimensions, "arrange the vector as a matrix".

The same motion can also be described from the standpoint of the body. Compute the other product:

$$
T^{-1}\dot T = \begin{pmatrix} R^{\mathsf T}\dot R & R^{\mathsf T}\dot p \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} [\omega_b] & v_b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad v_b = R^{\mathsf T}\dot p . \tag{6.3.7}
$$

$\omega_b$ is the body angular velocity of the previous section; $v_b = R^{\mathsf T}\dot p$ holds the components in {b} of the velocity of the origin of {b}. Writing $\mathcal V_b = (\omega_b, v_b)$, we have $T^{-1}\dot T = [\mathcal V_b]$. Putting the two together,

$$
\dot T = [\mathcal V_s]\,T = T\,[\mathcal V_b] . \tag{6.3.8}
$$

This has exactly the same form as $\dot R = [\omega_s]R = R[\omega_b]$ in Eqs. (6.2.3) and (6.2.5), with 3×3 replaced by 4×4.

**Definition 6.3.1 (Twist)** For a rigid body with pose T(t), the six-dimensional vector $\mathcal V_s = (\omega_s, v_s)$ determined by $[\mathcal V_s] = \dot T T^{-1}$ is called the **spatial twist**, and $\mathcal V_b = (\omega_b, v_b)$ determined by $[\mathcal V_b] = T^{-1}\dot T$ is called the **body twist**. Both are called **twists** (Lynch and Park also call a twist, in either form, a **spatial velocity**). The first three components are an angular velocity, in rad/s; the last three are a linear velocity, in m/s.

This book follows Lynch and Park in putting the angular velocity first and the linear velocity second. The book by Murray, Li and Sastry puts the linear velocity first, so take care when reading the literature.

The two twists describe the same motion, and the relation between their components follows at once from Eqs. (6.3.3) and (6.3.7) and $\omega_s = R\omega_b$: $\dot p = R v_b$, so $v_s = R v_b - (R\omega_b) \times p$, that is,

$$
\omega_s = R\,\omega_b , \qquad v_s = R\,v_b + p \times (R\,\omega_b) . \tag{6.3.9}
$$

This is a special case of the adjoint transformation of Section 6.5. Note that $v_s$ and $v_b$ differ by more than a rotation matrix: they are the velocities of two different points (the point at the origin of {s} and the origin of {b}), so they also differ by the term $p \times R\omega_b$.

**Definition 6.3.2 (se(3) and SE(3))** The set of all 4×4 matrices of the form (6.3.6) is denoted se(3). The set of all homogeneous transformation matrices is denoted SE(3) (Chapter 5).

**Theorem 6.3.1** SE(3) is a matrix Lie group, and its Lie algebra is se(3).

*Proof*　SE(3) is closed under multiplication and inversion (Chapter 5), and it is smoothly parametrized by the three degrees of freedom of R and the three of p, so it is a six-dimensional matrix Lie group. Let T(t) be a smooth curve through I with T(0) = I; by Eq. (6.3.4), $\dot T(0) = \dot T(0)T(0)^{-1} \in \mathrm{se}(3)$. Conversely, Section 6.4 will prove that for any $[\mathcal V] \in \mathrm{se}(3)$, $e^{[\mathcal V]t} \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$, and its derivative at t = 0 is $[\mathcal V]$.　∎

**Example 6.3.1** The UR5e is at its zero position, and only joint 2 is turning, at 30°/s. Find the spatial twist and the body twist of the flange, and the velocity of the flange centre.

At the zero position the pose of the flange is the M of Example 12.1.2 in Chapter 12:

$$
M = {{ex6_3.M}} , \qquad p = {{ex6_3.p}}\ \mathrm{m} .
$$

The axis of joint 2 points along −y and passes through the point (0, −0.138, 0.163) m; Chapter 12 has already worked out its screw axis $\mathcal S_2 = {{ex6_3.S2}}$. The body turns about this axis at $\dot\theta_2 = \pi/6$ rad/s, and by the interpretation of the screw axis in Section 12.1.5 (the first three components are the angular velocity for a unit rate of rotation, the last three the velocity of the point at the origin), the spatial twist is simply

$$
\mathcal V_s = \mathcal S_2\,\dot\theta_2 = {{ex6_3.Vs}} .
$$

The last three components say that the point at the origin of {s} (imagine it rigidly attached to the upper arm) moves along x at {{ex6_3.vsx:.4f}} m/s. This point lies 0.163 m directly below the axis of joint 2; turning about the axis, it moves horizontally, with speed 0.163 m × π/6 rad/s. By Eq. (6.3.2), the velocity of the flange centre is

with magnitude {{ex6_3.pdot_len:.4f}} m/s. This is the same as the result obtained with ω × r (r measured from a point on the axis) in Example 3.6.2 of Chapter 3. ### 6.3.4 The instantaneous screw axis: Mozzi's theorem

Eq. (6.3.2) gives the velocity of every point of the rigid body. In general the points have different velocities, pointing in all directions. But Mozzi discovered that a very simple structure lies hidden among them.

**Theorem 6.3.2 (Mozzi's theorem)** Let $\mathcal V = (\omega, v)$ be a twist with ω ≠ 0. Then there is a unique line L in space, with direction ω, such that the velocities of all points on it are parallel to ω and of equal magnitude. The motion of the body at this instant is a rotation about L with angular velocity |ω| together with a translation along L at speed h|ω|, that is,

$$
\mathcal V = \begin{pmatrix} \omega \\ -\omega \times q + h\,\omega \end{pmatrix} , \tag{6.3.10}
$$

where the pitch h and the point q of L nearest the origin are

$$
h = \frac{\omega \cdot v}{|\omega|^2} , \tag{6.3.11}
$$

$$
q = \frac{\omega \times v}{|\omega|^2} . \tag{6.3.12}
$$

*Proof*　Split v into a part parallel to ω and a part perpendicular to it: $v = h\omega + v_\perp$, with h given by Eq. (6.3.11). We want to write $v_\perp$ as $-\omega \times q$. By the vector triple product formula (3.2.14),

$$
-\omega \times \frac{\omega \times v}{|\omega|^2} = -\frac{\omega\,(\omega\cdot v) - v\,|\omega|^2}{|\omega|^2} = v - h\omega = v_\perp ,
$$

so it suffices to take q as in Eq. (6.3.12), which proves Eq. (6.3.10). For a point $x = q + \lambda\omega$ on L, by Eq. (6.3.2) the velocity is $v + \omega \times q = v - v_\perp = h\omega$, parallel to ω with magnitude h|ω|. Conversely, if the velocity $v + \omega\times x$ of some point x is parallel to ω, then its perpendicular part $v_\perp + \omega \times x = \omega \times (x - q) = 0$, so x − q is parallel to ω and x lies on L.　∎

L is called the **instantaneous screw axis**. For a point off L, the velocity has two parts: h|ω| along the axis, and a rotational velocity perpendicular to the axis whose magnitude is |ω| times the distance of the point from the axis (Figure 6.3.1). So the farther a point is from the axis, the faster it moves, and the points on the axis move most slowly. When ω = 0, Eq. (6.3.2) shows that all points have the same velocity: the motion is a pure translation, which amounts to "infinite pitch".

The form of Eq. (6.3.10) suggests splitting a twist into two parts: "which axis" and "how fast".

**Definition 6.3.3 (Screw axis)** Let the line L pass through the point q with direction the unit vector $\hat s$, and let the pitch be h. The six-dimensional vector

$$
\mathcal S = \begin{pmatrix} \hat s \\ -\hat s \times q + h\,\hat s \end{pmatrix} \tag{6.3.13}
$$

is called the **screw axis** on L with pitch h. When the pitch is infinite (a pure translation in the direction $\hat v$), we set $\mathcal S = (0, \hat v)$, $|\hat v| = 1$.

Then every twist can be written as

$$
\mathcal V = \mathcal S\,\dot\theta , \tag{6.3.14}
$$

where, when ω ≠ 0, $\dot\theta = |\omega|$ is the angular velocity of the rotation about the axis, and when ω = 0, $\dot\theta = |v|$ is the speed of the translation. The screw axis $\mathcal S$ is "the direction of the motion" and $\dot\theta$ is "the rate of the motion", just as a unit vector and a speed are for an ordinary velocity vector.

The joint screw axes of Eqs. (12.1.3) and (12.1.4) in Chapter 12 are exactly special cases of Definition 6.3.3: a revolute joint has zero pitch, $\mathcal S = (\hat\omega, -\hat\omega \times q)$; a prismatic joint has infinite pitch, $\mathcal S = (0, v)$. Now we know the precise meaning of the statement in Chapter 12: when a joint moves at rate $\dot\theta_i$, the twist of the link beyond it is $\mathcal S_i\dot\theta_i$.

**Example 6.3.2** The UR5e is at its zero position, and joints 1 and 3 turn together at 30°/s, the other joints being still. Find the spatial twist of the end-effector and its instantaneous screw axis.

At the zero position the joint axes are still in their reference positions, so the screw axes can be taken directly from Table 12.1.1 of Chapter 12:

$$
\mathcal S_1 = {{ex6_3.S1}} , \qquad \mathcal S_3 = {{ex6_3.S3}} .
$$

Joint 1 carries all the links beyond joint 1, and on top of that joint 3 carries the links beyond it, so the twist of the end-effector is the sum of the two (Section 6.3.5 will explain why twists can be added):

$$
\mathcal V_s = \mathcal S_1\dot\theta_1 + \mathcal S_3\dot\theta_3 = {{ex6_3.V}} .
$$

By Eqs. (6.3.11) and (6.3.12): $|\omega| = {{ex6_3.wn:.4f}}$ rad/s, $\omega\cdot v = {{ex6_3.wv:.4f}}$,

$$
\hat s = {{ex6_3.s_hat}} , \qquad q = {{ex6_3.q}}\ \mathrm{m} , \qquad h = {{ex6_3.h:.4f}}\ \mathrm{m/rad} .
$$

Two revolute joints, each a pure rotation of zero pitch, combine into a screw motion of non-zero pitch, and h equals exactly $L_1/2$ ($L_1$ = 0.425 m is the distance between the two axes; Exercise 6.3.4). The reason is that the two axes are neither parallel nor intersecting. Figure 6.3.2 shows this instantaneous screw axis: it passes obliquely between the two joint axes, through exactly the midpoint ${{ex6_3.mid}}$ m of their common perpendicular (Exercise 6.3.4). The points on the axis move along it at {{ex6_3.vmin:.4f}} m/s, the smallest speed of all points of the end-effector at this instant.

Program 6.3.1 checks this result in three ways: the velocities of the points on the axis are parallel to ω; for a point at distance r from the axis, the velocity component perpendicular to the axis equals |ω|r; and finding "the point of smallest velocity" by least squares gives exactly q.

### 6.3.5 Screws describe motion; poses are matrices in SE(3)

We have now met two kinds of objects that both have to do with "rigid-body motion" but are completely different in nature. Beginners easily confuse them, so they are worth comparing explicitly.

A **pose** T is an element of SE(3), a 4×4 matrix. It answers "where the rigid body is and which way it faces". Two poses can only be multiplied (composed), not added: the sum of two homogeneous transformation matrices has 2 rather than 1 in its lower-right corner and is no longer a pose. Nor can a pose be "multiplied by 2". It has 6 degrees of freedom, but written as a matrix it takes 12 numbers, which constrain one another.

A **twist** $\mathcal V$ is an element of se(3), written as a six-dimensional vector. It answers "how the rigid body is moving at this instant". Twists can be added and multiplied by numbers; they form an ordinary six-dimensional linear space: when two motions take place together, their twists add (Example 6.3.2); when a motion is twice as fast, its twist is multiplied by 2. Its 6 components are independent of one another, with no constraints.

Why twists add when two joints turn together can be verified directly. Take Example 6.3.2: by the product-of-exponentials formula of Chapter 12, the pose of the end-effector is $T = e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3}M$. Using $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}e^{[\mathcal S]\theta} = [\mathcal S]\dot\theta\,e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$ (as in Section 6.2.4) and the product rule,

$$
\dot T\,T^{-1} = [\mathcal S_1]\dot\theta_1 + e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}[\mathcal S_3]e^{-[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}\,\dot\theta_3 .
$$

At the zero position $\theta_1 = 0$, the second term is just $[\mathcal S_3]\dot\theta_3$, so $\mathcal V_s = \mathcal S_1\dot\theta_1 + \mathcal S_3\dot\theta_3$. Away from the zero position, the factor $e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}[\mathcal S_3]e^{-[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}$ in the second term is the screw axis of joint 3 after joint 1 has carried it to its new place (the adjoint transformation of Section 6.5); the rule of addition still holds, only what is added are the joints' screw axes at this instant. This is exactly how the Jacobian matrix of Chapter 16 is obtained.

The relation between the two is like that between a point on a surface and a tangent vector of the surface at that point: the pose T is the "point", $\dot T = [\mathcal V_s]T$ is the "tangent vector", and the twist is the "representative" of the tangent vector after it has been carried back to the identity. R and ω in the previous section are the special case of the same relation for rotation. This is what the title of this section says: **screws describe motion; poses are matrices in SE(3)**.

The distinction has very practical consequences. To interpolate between two poses, we cannot add the two matrices in proportion; the correct way is first to find the twist joining them (the logarithm map), scale the twist in proportion, and then turn it back into a pose (the exponential map). This is the subject of the next section. Likewise, a robot's Jacobian matrix turns joint velocities into the twist of the end-effector (Chapter 16), not into a pose; when the contributions of several joints are added, what is added are twists.

A word should also be said about the term "screw". In screw theory, a screw means the geometric object "a line with a pitch", that is, the screw axis of Definition 6.3.3; a twist is "a screw times a rate of rotation", and the wrench of Section 6.6 is "a screw times the magnitude of a force".

### 6.3.6 Everyday examples

When a bicycle rolls without slipping along the road, the point where the wheel touches the ground has zero velocity at that instant; it is the instantaneous centre of rotation of the wheel. The top of the wheel moves at twice the speed of the bicycle, which is why, in a photograph of a moving bicycle, the spokes are always more blurred in the upper half than in the lower half. When a door swings on its hinges, the instantaneous screw axis is the hinge axis, with zero pitch. When you turn a screwdriver, the twist of the blade is "turning about the axis of the screw while advancing along it", with the pitch fixed by the thread. In three dimensions, as in Example 6.3.2, rotations about two axes that are neither parallel nor intersecting combine into a screw motion of non-zero pitch: turn a pen about two directions with your fingers at once, and the pen "slides" off in some direction while it turns.

The twists of this section are "instantaneous". If a twist stays constant and the body keeps moving for a while, what pose does it reach? Conversely, given two poses, what twist takes one to the other? These are the subjects of the next section, the exponential and logarithm maps.

**Engineer's notes**

- The "TCP velocity" given by a robot controller is usually the linear velocity of the tool centre point together with the angular velocity of the tool. It is neither $\mathcal V_s$ nor $\mathcal V_b$: its linear part is the velocity of the TCP, whereas $v_s$ is the velocity of the point at the origin of {s}. Convert with Eq. (6.3.2): $v_s = \dot p_{\mathrm{tcp}} - \omega_s \times p_{\mathrm{tcp}}$.
- The linear-velocity part of a twist depends on the choice of origin. Far from the origin even a very small angular velocity makes $v_s$ large. When comparing the "size" of two twists, you cannot simply add the squares of the angular and linear velocities, because they have different units. This problem will come up again in Chapter 16 when we discuss manipulability.
- The pitch h is a geometric quantity, independent of the frame (Section 6.5). It gives a quick way to judge the nature of a motion: h ≈ 0 is a pure rotation, and a very large |h| is close to a pure translation. Whether the feed in driving a screw or tapping a thread matches the speed of rotation can be judged by whether the pitch of the measured twist equals lead/2π of the thread.
