### 6.2.1 The problem

Mount an inertial measurement unit on the flange of a UR5e; the gyroscope inside it measures how fast the end-effector is turning. The gyroscope is fixed to the flange, and its three readings are the components of the angular velocity in its own frame. When the controller plans a motion, however, it often needs the components of the angular velocity in the base frame. How are the two related? And conversely, given the angular velocity at every instant, how do we compute the orientation step by step?

Chapter 3 explained that angular velocity is a vector **ω**, and that when a rigid body turns about a fixed axis, the velocity of a point of the body is **v** = **ω** × **r**. Chapter 4 explained further that the rotation matrix for a turn through θ about a fixed axis is the matrix exponential $e^{[\hat\omega]\theta}$. But both chapters dealt with the case of "a fixed axis". The wrist of an arm has several joints turning at the same time, and the axes themselves are moving; how, then, is the angular velocity read off from the rotation matrix? This section answers these questions, and on the way it introduces two concepts that run through the whole book: the Lie group SO(3) and its Lie algebra so(3).

### 6.2.2 Angular velocity from the derivative of the rotation matrix

Attach a body frame {b} to the rigid body, and let its orientation relative to the space frame {s} be the time-varying rotation matrix $R(t) = R_{sb}(t)$. Eq. (3.6.9) of Chapter 3 already gave $\dot R = [\omega_s]R$ for the case of rotation about a fixed axis. We now prove that this relation still holds when the axis is not fixed, and that the angular velocity can be computed directly from R and its derivative $\dot R$. Here $\dot R$ is the matrix obtained by differentiating every entry of R with respect to time; its unit is 1/s.

A rotation matrix satisfies $R R^{\mathsf T} = I$ at every instant. Differentiate both sides with respect to time: the right-hand side is constant and its derivative is zero; the left-hand side follows the product rule:

$$
\dot R R^{\mathsf T} + R \dot R^{\mathsf T} = 0 . \tag{6.2.1}
$$

Note that $R\dot R^{\mathsf T} = (\dot R R^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T}$, so the equation says

$$
(\dot R R^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T} = -\,\dot R R^{\mathsf T} , \tag{6.2.2}
$$

that is, $\dot R R^{\mathsf T}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix. By Chapter 3, every 3×3 skew-symmetric matrix is the cross-product matrix of exactly one vector. Call this vector $\omega_s$:

$$
\dot R R^{\mathsf T} = [\omega_s] , \qquad \text{that is,}\quad \dot R = [\omega_s]\,R . \tag{6.2.3}
$$

$\omega_s$ holds precisely the components of the angular velocity in {s}. The reason is the same as in Chapter 3: column i of R holds the components in {s} of the i-th axis $\hat x_i$ of {b}, and Eq. (6.2.3) written column by column is $\dot{\hat x}_i = \omega_s \times \hat x_i$; that is, every axis of the body changes at the rate "angular velocity crossed with itself". No assumption of a fixed axis has been used here, so Eq. (6.2.3) holds for general rotations.

The same angular velocity vector can also be expressed in {b}. By the transformation rule for vector components in Chapter 3, $\omega_b = R^{\mathsf T}\omega_s$. Using Eq. (3.4.6), $R^{\mathsf T}[\omega_s]R = [R^{\mathsf T}\omega_s]$, we get

$$
\omega_b = R^{\mathsf T}\omega_s , \qquad [\omega_b] = R^{\mathsf T}[\omega_s]R = R^{\mathsf T}\dot R . \tag{6.2.4}
$$

This gives a second way of writing $\dot R$:

$$
\dot R = R\,[\omega_b] . \tag{6.2.5}
$$

**Definition 6.2.1 (Spatial and body angular velocity)** Let $R = R_{sb}(t)$. The $\omega_s$ determined by $[\omega_s] = \dot R R^{\mathsf T}$ is called the **spatial angular velocity**, and the $\omega_b$ determined by $[\omega_b] = R^{\mathsf T}\dot R$ is called the **body angular velocity**; both are in rad/s.

It must be stressed that $\omega_s$ and $\omega_b$ are the components, in two frames, of one and the same physical quantity (the angular velocity vector **ω**), not two different angular velocities (Figure 6.2.1). "Spatial" and "body" say in which frame the arrow is read. Nor is $\omega_b$ "the angular velocity of {b} relative to {b}", since {b} of course does not turn relative to itself; it is "the angular velocity of {b} relative to {s}, read in components of {b}". A gyroscope turns together with the body, and what it measures is $\omega_b$.

Eqs. (6.2.3) and (6.2.5) are also the "differential versions" of the two multiplication rules of Chapter 4: a small rotation about a fixed axis premultiplies, and a small rotation about one of the body's own axes postmultiplies (Theorem 4.2.2). Over a very short time Δt the change of orientation is a small rotation, $R(t + \Delta t) \approx (I + [\omega_s]\Delta t)\,R(t) = R(t)\,(I + [\omega_b]\Delta t)$: the same small rotation premultiplies when written in spatial components and postmultiplies when written in body components.

**Example 6.2.1** Starting from the zero position, joint 1 of a UR5e turns uniformly at 30°/s and joint 6 at 90°/s, at the same time, while the other joints stay still. Find the spatial angular velocity and the body angular velocity of the flange at t = 1 s.

By the product-of-exponentials formula of Chapter 12, looking only at the rotational part, the orientation of the flange is

$$
R(t) = e^{[\hat\omega_1]\theta_1(t)}\, e^{[\hat\omega_6]\theta_6(t)}\, R_0 ,
$$

where $\hat\omega_1 = (0, 0, 1)$ and $\hat\omega_6 = (0, -1, 0)$ are the directions of the two joint axes at the zero position (Table 12.1.1), $R_0$ is the orientation of the flange at the zero position, $\theta_1 = \pi t/6$ and $\theta_6 = \pi t/2$. At t = 1 s,

$$
R = {{ex6_2.Rt}} .
$$

**Method 1: the vector sum of angular velocities.** When two joints turn at the same time, their angular velocities add as vectors (cf. Theorem 3.6.2). The axis of joint 1 does not move and contributes $\dot\theta_1\hat\omega_1$; the axis of joint 6 has been carried round through $\theta_1$ by joint 1, its direction at this moment is $e^{[\hat\omega_1]\theta_1}\hat\omega_6$, and it contributes $\dot\theta_6\, e^{[\hat\omega_1]\theta_1}\hat\omega_6$. Adding them gives

$$
\omega_s = {{ex6_2.ws}}\ \mathrm{rad/s} , \qquad \omega_b = R^{\mathsf T}\omega_s = {{ex6_2.wb}}\ \mathrm{rad/s} .
$$

**Method 2: differentiating the orientation.** Compute $\dot R$ by central differences, and then $\dot R R^{\mathsf T}$ and $R^{\mathsf T}\dot R$ as in Definition 6.2.1:

$$
\dot R R^{\mathsf T} = {{ex6_2.Ws}} , \qquad R^{\mathsf T}\dot R = {{ex6_2.Wb}} .
$$

Both matrices are skew-symmetric, and the vectors read off from them are exactly the same as those of Method 1. Both sets of components have length {{ex6_2.wnorm:.4f}} rad/s, that is, {{ex6_2.wnorm_deg:.2f}}°/s, which is exactly $\sqrt{30^2 + 90^2}$°/s, because the two joint axes are always perpendicular.

It is instructive to compare the values at t = 0: then $\omega_s = {{ex6_2.ws0}}$ and $\omega_b = {{ex6_2.wb0}}$. One second later, the direction of $\omega_s$ has changed, because the axis of joint 6 has been carried away by joint 1; $\omega_b$ has changed too, the contribution of joint 1 having turned from the $-z_b$ direction to the $x_b$ direction, because the flange has turned through 90° about joint 6. So in a general motion both the spatial angular velocity and the body angular velocity change with time.

### 6.2.3 so(3): the "home" of angular velocity

Eq. (6.2.3) shows that the derivative of a rotation matrix can always be written as "a skew-symmetric matrix times R". These skew-symmetric matrices form a set of their own, which deserves a name.

**Definition 6.2.2 (so(3))** The set of all 3×3 real skew-symmetric matrices is written so(3):

$$
\mathrm{so}(3) = \{\, A \in \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3} : A^{\mathsf T} = -A \,\} = \{\, [\omega] : \omega \in \mathbb{R}^3 \,\} .
$$

so(3) and SO(3) behave very differently. SO(3) is closed under multiplication but not under addition: the sum of two rotation matrices is in general not a rotation matrix. so(3) is just the opposite: it is closed under addition and multiplication by scalars, and is a three-dimensional linear space: $[\omega_1] + [\omega_2] = [\omega_1 + \omega_2]$, $c[\omega] = [c\,\omega]$. This agrees with the physical facts: angular velocities can be added (Theorem 3.6.2), whereas finite rotations cannot be added, only composed in order.

Each element of SO(3) has 9 numbers, but they are subject to the 6 constraints of $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ (each of the three columns has unit length, and they are mutually orthogonal), which leave only 3 degrees of freedom. One can picture SO(3) as a smooth "three-dimensional surface" in nine-dimensional space. A set of this kind, which near each of its points looks like an ordinary linear space and is smooth everywhere, is called a **manifold** in mathematics. SO(3) is both a group and a manifold, and its multiplication and inversion are smooth operations; such an object is called a Lie group.

**Definition 6.2.3 (Matrix Lie group and Lie algebra)** A set G of invertible matrices that is closed under multiplication and inversion and is at the same time a smooth manifold is called a **matrix Lie group**, or a **Lie group** for short. The set of the derivatives $\dot A(0)$ at t = 0 of all smooth curves A(t) in G through the identity matrix I (A(0) = I) is called the **tangent space** of G at I, also called the **Lie algebra** of G.

The intuitive meaning of "tangent space" is shown in Figure 6.2.2: at a point of a surface, draw the plane tangent to the surface; the velocity vectors of all the curves on the surface through this point lie in this plane. The Lie algebra is just "the tangent plane at the identity".

**Theorem 6.2.1** The Lie algebra of SO(3) is so(3).

*Proof*　Let R(t) be a smooth curve in SO(3) with R(0) = I. By Eq. (6.2.2), $\dot R(0)R(0)^{\mathsf T} = \dot R(0)$ is a skew-symmetric matrix, so the Lie algebra is contained in so(3). Conversely, for any $[\omega] \in \mathrm{so}(3)$, the curve $R(t) = e^{[\omega]t}$ lies in SO(3) (Rodrigues' formula, Section 4.4), and $\dot R(0) = [\omega]$. So every element of so(3) is the tangent vector of some curve.　∎

At an arbitrary point R, the form of a tangent vector $\dot R$ is given by Eqs. (6.2.3) and (6.2.5): $\dot R = [\omega_s]R = R[\omega_b]$. In other words, the tangent space at R is so(3) "carried over" by premultiplying or postmultiplying by R. This is why we need only study so(3) at the identity: the tangent spaces at all other points can be obtained from it.

The names "Lie group" and "Lie algebra" come from the Norwegian mathematician Sophus Lie. In the 1870s, in order to solve differential equations by means of symmetry, he studied continuous groups of transformations and found that the structure of such a group is determined almost entirely by its "infinitesimal transformations" near the identity. The German mathematician Wilhelm Killing arrived independently at a similar algebraic structure in the 1880s. At the time this structure was called an "infinitesimal group"; the name "Lie algebra" was introduced by Hermann Weyl in the 1930s. For robotics, Lie's idea can be summed up in one sentence: **orientations live in the Lie group, angular velocities in the Lie algebra**.

### 6.2.4 The exponential map and the logarithm map

We now answer the second question raised at the start of this section: given the angular velocity, how do we compute the orientation?

The simplest case is when the components $\omega_s$ of the angular velocity in {s} do not change with time. Eq. (6.2.3) then becomes the linear differential equation with constant coefficients $\dot R = [\omega_s]R$, exactly the same as the equation for a single vector in Section 4.4.4, except that the unknown is now a matrix instead of a vector (each column satisfies the same equation). By Section 4.4.4, its solution is

$$
R(t) = e^{[\omega_s]t}\, R(0) . \tag{6.2.6}
$$

Likewise, if $\omega_b$ is constant, Eq. (6.2.5) gives

$$
R(t) = R(0)\, e^{[\omega_b]t} . \tag{6.2.7}
$$

These two formulas once again show the rule "space frame: premultiply; body frame: postmultiply". In fact, when $\omega_s$ is constant, $\omega_b = R(0)^{\mathsf T}e^{-[\omega_s]t}\omega_s = R(0)^{\mathsf T}\omega_s$ is constant too (because $\omega_s$ is the axis of $e^{[\omega_s]t}$), so the two formulas describe the same kind of motion: uniform rotation about a fixed axis.

Eq. (6.2.6) provides a bridge from the Lie algebra to the Lie group: take an element $[\omega]$ of so(3) and let the body turn with this angular velocity for unit time, and it arrives at the element $e^{[\omega]}$ of SO(3).

**Definition 6.2.4 (Exponential map and logarithm map of SO(3))** The mapping

$$
\exp : \mathrm{so}(3) \to \mathrm{SO}(3) , \qquad [\hat\omega]\theta \mapsto e^{[\hat\omega]\theta} = I + \sin\theta\,[\hat\omega] + (1 - \cos\theta)\,[\hat\omega]^2 \tag{6.2.8}
$$

is called the **exponential map** of SO(3). For a given $R \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$, finding $[\hat\omega]\theta$ with $e^{[\hat\omega]\theta} = R$ and $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ is called the **logarithm map** of SO(3), written $[\hat\omega]\theta = \log R$.

The way to compute the logarithm map was given in Section 4.4.6: θ from the trace, $\hat\omega$ from $R - R^{\mathsf T}$, with the two cases θ = 0 and θ = π handled separately.

**Theorem 6.2.2** The exponential map of SO(3) is surjective: every rotation matrix is the exponential of some element of so(3). If θ is restricted to $[0, \pi)$, the logarithm map is unique; for θ = π there are two logarithms, $\pm\hat\omega$.

*Proof*　By Euler's rotation theorem (Theorem 4.3.1), every R is a turn through some $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ about some $\hat\omega$, and then by Rodrigues' formula $R = e^{[\hat\omega]\theta}$. For uniqueness, see the discussion in Section 4.3.5.　∎

The **exponential coordinates** $\hat\omega\theta$ of Chapter 4 thus acquire a new meaning: they are the "representative" of R in the Lie algebra, and physically they give "the orientation reached by turning for 1 s at the constant angular velocity $\hat\omega\theta$".

### 6.2.5 Exponential coordinates are not the integral of angular velocity

A common misconception is that integrating the angular velocity over time gives the exponential coordinates of the change of orientation. This is true only when the axis is fixed.

**Example 6.2.2** For the motion of Example 6.2.1, compare the exponential coordinates of the total change of orientation over 1 s, $R(1)R(0)^{\mathsf T}$, with $\int_0^1 \omega_s\,\mathrm dt$.

Applying the logarithm map to $R(1)R(0)^{\mathsf T}$ gives an angle of {{ex6_2.th_log_deg:.2f}}°, an axis $\hat\omega = {{ex6_2.w_log}}$, and exponential coordinates

$$
\hat\omega\theta = {{ex6_2.expc}}\ \mathrm{rad} .
$$

On the other hand, integrating the expression of Method 1 term by term (the axis of joint 6 turns uniformly through 30° about the z axis, so its contribution can be integrated in closed form) gives

$$
\int_0^1 \omega_s\,\mathrm dt = {{ex6_2.integral}}\ \mathrm{rad} .
$$

The two differ by {{ex6_2.gap:.4f}} rad, about {{ex6_2.gap_deg:.1f}}°. Program 6.2.1 also verifies that if only joint 6 turns, so that the axis is fixed, the two are exactly the same.

The reason is that when the axis changes, turning a little about this axis and then a little about that one is not the same as "turning together about the average axis"; this is precisely the non-commutativity of finite rotations (Section 4.2). Section 6.7 will describe this difference quantitatively with the Lie bracket. The engineering consequence is that the readings of an IMU gyroscope cannot simply be summed; they must be applied step by step as matrix multiplications. By Eq. (6.2.5), within one sampling period Δt, $\omega_b$ is approximately constant, and the orientation is updated by

$$
R_{k+1} = R_k\, e^{[\omega_b]\Delta t} ,
$$

with the gyroscope reading postmultiplying. For $\omega_b$ take the mean reading over the period (the program takes the value at the middle of the period); then the error of each step is of third order in $\Delta t$. For the motion of Example 6.2.1, updating 10 times per second gives an orientation error of about {{ex6_2.e_r10:.3f}}° after 1 s, and updating 100 times per second about $ {{ex6_2.e_r100:sci1}} $°; the error decreases as the square of the step size. If the gyroscope reading is wrongly premultiplied, the error is about {{ex6_2.e_l100:.0f}}° even with 100 updates per second, and it does not go away however small the step, because it is an error in the formula itself, not a truncation error.

### 6.2.6 An everyday example

A phone switches its screen between portrait and landscape automatically with the help of its accelerometer; but when you turn the phone like a steering wheel in a racing game, or turn it to look around a panoramic photo and the picture turns with it, that is the work of the gyroscope. The phone reads its gyroscope hundreds of times a second and updates its orientation with the postmultiplication formula above. Try it yourself: lay the phone flat, turn it 90° about its long edge and then 90° about its short edge; then, starting from the same position, turn it first about the short edge and then about the long edge. The two final orientations are not the same, and this is exactly why angular velocities cannot simply be "added up".

This section has placed the velocity of rotation in the Lie algebra so(3). The next section applies the same ideas to general rigid-body motion: poses lie in the Lie group SE(3), velocities lie in its Lie algebra se(3), and the elements of se(3) are twists.

**Engineer's notes**

- Whenever you read angular velocity data, first make sure which frame the components are in. A gyroscope, and a robot controller's "velocity in the tool frame", give body components; "velocity in the base frame" gives spatial components. The two differ by $R_{sb}$.
- When integrating orientation with $R_{k+1} = R_k e^{[\omega_b]\Delta t}$, Rodrigues' formula is exact for small angles too; do not use a first-order approximation such as $R_k(I + [\omega_b]\Delta t)$, or R will soon cease to be orthogonal and will need frequent orthonormalization (Section 4.8).
- To estimate the angular velocity from the orientations of two consecutive frames, use $\log(R_k^{\mathsf T}R_{k+1})/\Delta t$ for the body angular velocity and $\log(R_{k+1}R_k^{\mathsf T})/\Delta t$ for the spatial angular velocity. Taking differences of the matrix entries directly and then the skew-symmetric part gives large errors when the rotation is fast and the sampling slow.
