### 6.1.1 The problem

A UR5e on an assembly line is loading and unloading parts: it picks a rectangular block off a conveyor and puts it into a fixture next to it. On the conveyor the centre of the part is at (0.5, −0.2, 0.1) m, and its edges are parallel to the axes of the base frame {s}; in the fixture its centre is at (0.3, 0.4, 0.25) m, and it has been turned through 90° about the vertical. The arm can take all sorts of paths between the two poses, but there is only one "displacement" that the part finally undergoes: the rigid-body transformation from the first pose to the second.

Euler's rotation theorem of Chapter 4 tells us that if the part only rotates, with one point held fixed, then the rotation is necessarily a turn through some angle about some axis. Here, however, the part both rotates and moves, and no point stays fixed. The natural question is whether a general rigid-body displacement also has such a simple geometric description. In other words, can we find an axis such that the part gets from the first pose to the second simply by "turning about it and sliding along it"?

The answer is yes, and this is Chasles' theorem, the subject of this section. It is the starting point of the screw methods used throughout the book: when Chapter 12 says "the motion of every joint is a screw motion about a screw axis", and when Chapter 16 writes the velocity of the end-effector as a screw, this theorem lies behind both. This section first makes the idea clear in the plane and then extends it to space.

### 6.1.2 Describing a rigid-body displacement

First let us say precisely what "displacement" means. When a rigid body moves from one pose to another, every point of the body moves from its original position x to a new position x′. The distance between any two points of the body stays the same, and the body does not turn into its own mirror image. It can be proved (Chapter 5) that such a mapping can always be written as

$$
x' = R\,x + p , \tag{6.1.1}
$$

where x and x′ are both components in the fixed frame {s} (in m), $R \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ is a rotation matrix and $p \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is a constant vector. Eq. (6.1.1) first rotates the body by R about the origin of {s} and then translates it by p. Writing R and p together as the 4×4 matrix $T = \begin{pmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ gives the homogeneous transformation of Chapter 5; the poses $T_1$ and $T_2$ of the part before and after the displacement satisfy $T_2 = T\,T_1$.

There is something unnatural about Eq. (6.1.1): it splits the displacement into two steps, "a rotation about the origin" and "a translation", while the origin is a point we chose arbitrarily. With a different origin, R stays the same but p changes. The same physical displacement can be split in this way in infinitely many ways. What this section looks for is a description determined by the displacement alone, independent of the choice of origin.

### 6.1.3 The planar case: the pole

Take the plane first. Let the part move on a tabletop, let points on the tabletop be given by two-dimensional components, and denote the angle of turn by φ, positive anticlockwise. Then R in Eq. (6.1.1) is the plane rotation matrix R(φ) of Section 4.1, and p is a two-dimensional vector.

If φ ≠ 0, let us look for a fixed point c, that is, a point satisfying $R\,c + p = c$:

$$
(I - R)\,c = p . \tag{6.1.2}
$$

The determinant of the 2×2 matrix $I - R$ is $(1 - \cos\varphi)^2 + \sin^2\varphi = 2 - 2\cos\varphi$, which is positive when φ ≠ 0, so Eq. (6.1.2) has a unique solution. With c in hand, Eq. (6.1.1) can be rewritten as

$$
x' = c + R\,(x - c) , \tag{6.1.3}
$$

which is exactly "a turn through φ about the point c". This gives the following result.

**Theorem 6.1.1 (Planar displacement)** A rigid-body displacement in the plane with angle of turn φ ≠ 0 is equivalent to a turn through φ about a unique point c; if φ = 0, it is a translation.

The point c is called the **pole** of the planar displacement. The pole need not lie on the part; quite often it lies outside it.

The pole also has a neat geometric construction. Take any point P on the part; it moves to P′. By Eq. (6.1.3), P and P′ are at the same distance from c, so c lies on the **perpendicular bisector** of the segment PP′. Take another point $P_2$; c also lies on the perpendicular bisector of $P_2P_2'$. The intersection of the two perpendicular bisectors is the pole (Figure 6.1.1).

**Example 6.1.1** A SCARA robot moves a part on a tabletop from pose A to pose B: the reference point of the part moves from (0.30, 0.05) m to (0.54, 0.13) m, and at the same time the part turns 60° anticlockwise. Find the pole.

By Eq. (6.1.1), $p = p_B - R\,p_A$, where

$$
R = R(60^\circ) = {{ex6_1.R2}} , \qquad p = {{ex6_1.p2}}\ \mathrm{m} .
$$

Solving Eq. (6.1.2) gives

$$
c = {{ex6_1.c}}\ \mathrm{m} .
$$

Check this with the perpendicular-bisector construction: take the reference point $P_1$ and another point $P_2$ 0.15 m along the long side of the part, compute their new positions, and draw the two perpendicular bisectors; they meet at the same c as above. The distance from the reference point to the pole is {{ex6_1.rA:.4f}} m both before and after the displacement. In other words, however complicated the path the robot takes, the final displacement of the part is equivalent to a turn of 60° about the point ({{ex6_1.cx:.3f}}, {{ex6_1.cy:.3f}}) m on the tabletop.

### 6.1.4 The spatial case: screw motion

Back to space. If R = I, Eq. (6.1.1) is a translation and there is nothing more to say. From now on let R ≠ I. By Euler's rotation theorem (Theorem 4.3.1), R is a turn through an angle θ about some unit vector $\hat s$, with 0 < θ ≤ π. The direction of the axis is written $\hat s$ here rather than $\hat\omega$ to stress that it is the axis of the whole displacement, not just of its rotational part; numerically the two are the same.

In the plane we found a fixed point. In space there is in general no fixed point, for a simple reason: if p has a component along $\hat s$, the rotation does not change the coordinate in this direction, and this part of the translation cannot be "absorbed" by the rotation. So we split p into a part along the axis and a part perpendicular to it:

$$
p = d\,\hat s + p_\perp , \qquad d = \hat s \cdot p , \qquad p_\perp = p - (\hat s \cdot p)\,\hat s . \tag{6.1.4}
$$

The perpendicular part $p_\perp$ can be absorbed as in the plane: in the plane perpendicular to $\hat s$, R is just a plane rotation through θ, so the equation

$$
(I - R)\,q = p_\perp , \qquad q \perp \hat s \tag{6.1.5}
$$

has a unique solution. Substituting into Eq. (6.1.1) and using $R\hat s = \hat s$, one can verify that

$$
x' = q + R\,(x - q) + d\,\hat s . \tag{6.1.6}
$$

Indeed, the right-hand side equals $R\,x + (I - R)\,q + d\hat s = R\,x + p_\perp + d\hat s = R\,x + p$. Eq. (6.1.6) means that the body first turns through θ about the line through the point q with direction $\hat s$, and then translates by d along this line. The rotation and the translation have the same line as their axis, so the two steps can be done in either order, or at the same time. This kind of motion is just the motion of a screw being driven into a nut.

**Definition 6.1.1 (Screw motion)** Let the line L pass through the point q with the direction of the unit vector $\hat s$. A rigid body turning through θ about L while translating by hθ along L, that is,

$$
x' = q + e^{[\hat s]\theta}(x - q) + h\theta\,\hat s ,
$$

is said to perform a **screw motion** about L. L is called its axis, and h is called its **pitch**, in m/rad, which is the distance advanced along the axis per radian turned. h = 0 gives a pure rotation; a pure translation is regarded as the limiting case h = ∞, in which only the distance d moved along $\hat s$ has a meaning.

Here $e^{[\hat s]\theta}$ is the rotation matrix given by Rodrigues' formula (4.4.9). What mechanical engineers call the thread pitch (strictly the **lead**, the same thing for a single-start thread; this book says lead to avoid confusion with h) is the distance the thread advances in one full turn, which equals 2πh. For example, a screw with a lead of 1 mm has pitch h = 1/(2π) mm/rad.

**Theorem 6.1.2 (Chasles' theorem)** Every rigid-body displacement is a screw motion. If the displacement is not a pure translation, its axis (as a line in space), its angle θ ∈ (0, π] and its translation vector along the axis $d\,\hat s$ are uniquely determined by the displacement: $\hat s$ and θ are determined by R through Euler's theorem, $d = \hat s \cdot p$, the pitch is h = d/θ, and the point q on the axis is determined by Eq. (6.1.5). For 0 < θ < π, $\hat s$ and h are unique as well; for θ = π, either $\hat s$ or $-\hat s$ will do, and d and h change sign together.

*Proof*　Existence has already been shown by Eqs. (6.1.4) to (6.1.6); it remains only to prove that Eq. (6.1.5) has a unique solution. Choose unit vectors u and w such that $(u, w, \hat s)$ is a right-handed system. In this basis R acts as $\begin{pmatrix} R(\theta) & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, where R(θ) is the plane rotation matrix. Restricted to the plane spanned by u and w, $I - R$ is just $I - R(\theta)$, whose determinant is $2 - 2\cos\theta > 0$, so for $p_\perp$ in the plane there is a unique q in the plane. Uniqueness: if another axis L′, angle θ′ and translation d′ give the same displacement, its rotational part is also R, so by Euler's theorem its direction must be $\pm\hat s$ and its angle ±θ; having fixed the signs of $\hat s$ and θ, comparing the components along $\hat s$ in Eq. (6.1.6) gives d′ = d, and comparing the components perpendicular to $\hat s$ gives the same equation (6.1.5), so L′ and L are the same line. For θ = π, a turn of π about $-\hat s$ is the same rotation as a turn of π about $\hat s$; then $d' = (-\hat s)\cdot p = -d$, the translation vector $d'(-\hat s) = d\,\hat s$ is unchanged, and the pitch is h′ = d′/π = −h.　∎

The point q on the axis can also be written in closed form. In the plane perpendicular to $\hat s$, "multiplying by R" amounts to turning a vector through θ, and "crossing with $\hat s$" amounts to turning a vector through 90°. A direct check with these two operations shows (Exercise 6.1.3) that the solution of Eq. (6.1.5) is

$$
q = \frac{1}{2}\Big(p_\perp + \cot\frac{\theta}{2}\;\hat s \times p_\perp\Big) . \tag{6.1.7}
$$

Its geometric meaning is that q lies on the perpendicular bisector of the segment from the origin to $p_\perp$, at a distance $\frac12|p_\perp|\cot\frac{\theta}{2}$ from the midpoint of the segment. This is the same thing as finding the pole in the plane with perpendicular bisectors.

### 6.1.5 Example: the screw axis of a loading-and-unloading displacement

**Example 6.1.2** Find the screw motion corresponding to the displacement of the part from the conveyor to the fixture described at the start of this section.

The pose $T_1$ of the part on the conveyor: R = I, centre $p_1 = (0.5, -0.2, 0.1)$ m; the pose $T_2$ in the fixture: $R_2 = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, 90^\circ)$, centre (0.3, 0.4, 0.25) m. From $T_2 = T\,T_1$ we get $T = T_2 T_1^{-1}$:

$$
T = {{ex6_1.D}} , \qquad p = {{ex6_1.p}}\ \mathrm{m} .
$$

Step 1: find the axis and the angle from R: $\hat s = {{ex6_1.s}}$, θ = {{ex6_1.th_deg:.0f}}°, that is, π/2 rad.

Step 2: split p by Eq. (6.1.4): the translation along the axis is $d = \hat s \cdot p = {{ex6_1.d:.2f}}$ m, and the perpendicular part is $p_\perp = {{ex6_1.p_perp}}$ m.

Step 3: find a point on the axis. By Eq. (6.1.7), $\cot 45^\circ = 1$ and $\hat s \times p_\perp = {{ex6_1.s_cross}}$, so

$$
q = \frac12\big[(0.1, -0.1, 0) + (0.1, 0.1, 0)\big] = {{ex6_1.q}}\ \mathrm{m} .
$$

Step 4: the pitch is $h = d/\theta = 0.15/(\pi/2) \approx {{ex6_1.h:.4f}}$ m/rad, that is, the part rises about {{ex6_1.h_mm_per_deg:.2f}} mm for every degree it turns.

The conclusion: this loading-and-unloading displacement is equivalent to the part turning 90° about the vertical axis through the point (0.1, 0, 0) m while rising 0.15 m (Figure 6.1.2). The distance from the centre of the part to this axis is {{ex6_1.r0:.4f}} m and stays the same throughout the screw motion, so the centre traces out an arc of a helix.

Program 6.1.1 checks this conclusion in three ways: the new positions of the eight corners of the part computed by Eq. (6.1.6) are exactly the same as those computed by $R\,x + p$; sliding first and then turning gives the same result as turning first and then sliding; and the q obtained by solving Eq. (6.1.5) directly by least squares is the same as the closed form (6.1.7).

Note that the screw motion is only an equivalent description of the displacement; it does not mean that the arm actually carries the part along this helix. The path the arm really takes is decided by trajectory planning. But in many situations, moving the end-effector "uniformly along the screw axis" is in fact a good plan: it is the most direct transition between two poses, and Section 6.4 will write it as a formula using the exponential map.

### 6.1.6 Background and history

In 1775 Euler proved the theorem on rotations with one point fixed (Section 4.3). What happens when the condition of a fixed point is dropped? The first to answer this question systematically was the Italian mathematician Giulio Mozzi. In a short book published in Naples in 1763, *Discorso matematico sopra il rotamento momentaneo dei corpi* (A Mathematical Discourse on the Instantaneous Rotation of Bodies), he proved that the motion of a rigid body at any instant can be regarded as a rotation about some axis plus a translation along this axis; today this axis is called the **instantaneous screw axis**. Mozzi dealt with velocities, that is, with "instantaneous" motion, and Section 6.3 will reach this result again from the point of view of velocity. His work did not circulate widely and remained little known for a long time, until studies in the history of mechanisms drew attention to it again at the end of the twentieth century (see the article by Ceccarelli in the chapter references).

In 1830 the French geometer Michel Chasles, in a short note on the relation between two similar figures in space, stated Theorem 6.1.2 for finite displacements, and the result has borne his name ever since. Around the same time, Augustin-Louis Cauchy discussed the instantaneous motion of a rigid body in 1827, and Olinde Rodrigues, in his paper of 1840, gave a complete analytical proof for finite displacements together with the formula for composing displacements (Section 4.4). The theorem extends Euler's theorem from "rotation about a point" to "general rigid-body motion", and it later became the starting point of the theory of screws built by the Irish astronomer and mathematician Robert Stawell Ball (Section 6.6).

From Mozzi to Chasles, the discovery of screw motion went "instantaneous first, finite later". This chapter tells the story in the opposite order: finite displacements first (this section), then velocities (Section 6.3), and finally Section 6.4 unifies the two with the exponential map.

### 6.1.7 Everyday screw motions

Driving a screw, twisting off a bottle cap and opening a wine bottle with a corkscrew are all screw motions, with the pitch set by the thread. Pushing a door open is a screw motion of zero pitch whose axis is the hinge; pulling out a drawer is a screw motion of infinite pitch, a pure slide. Chasles' theorem says something less obvious: even for a whole sequence of complicated actions, such as picking up a book from a desk, turning it over a few times in the air and putting it on a shelf, the final displacement can always be achieved by "a single twist about some invisible axis".

**Engineer's notes**

- If a teach-pendant instruction "turn the tool about some axis" uses an axis that does not pass through the tool centre point, the tool centre point moves along a circular arc; if there is also a feed along the axis, the motion is a screw motion. In operations such as driving screws, tapping and tightening valves, the feed rate must match the rotational speed through the lead, feeding one lead per revolution, or the thread will be damaged.
- When finding the screw axis from two poses, cot(θ/2) is very large for angles close to 0, and the q given by Eq. (6.1.7) is far from the origin and numerically unreliable. The displacement is then close to a pure translation and should be treated directly as a translation.
- To decide whether a motion "contains a translation along the axis", look at whether $\hat s \cdot p$ is zero, not whether p is zero: p depends on the choice of origin, while $\hat s \cdot p$ does not (Exercise 6.1.4).
