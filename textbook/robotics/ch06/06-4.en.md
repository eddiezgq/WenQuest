### 6.4.1 The problem

A pipe in a factory has a manual valve. So that the robot can open and close valves as it goes round on inspection, we have the UR5e grip the rim of the valve handwheel and turn the handwheel 90° anticlockwise. The valve stem is driven by a thread and rises 4 mm per turn, so the handwheel also rises slightly as it turns. After the turn, what is the pose of the gripper?

This is a problem of "given the twist, find the displacement": the gripper makes a screw motion together with the handwheel, the screw axis is the axis of the valve stem, and the pitch is fixed by the thread. The previous section told us how to describe such a motion by a six-dimensional screw axis, but not yet where the motion ends up after it has gone on for a while.

The reverse problem is just as common. In the loading and unloading task of Section 6.1, we know two poses of a part and want to find the screw motion joining them. The product-of-exponentials formula of Chapter 12 writes the motion of each joint as $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$, but it did not derive how this matrix exponential is computed; it only gave the result (12.1.5). This section does both things: finding the displacement from the screw is the exponential map; finding the screw from the displacement is the logarithm map.

### 6.4.2 Motion with a constant twist

Let a rigid body move with a constant spatial twist $\mathcal V = \mathcal S\dot\theta$, where $\mathcal S$ is the screw axis and $\dot\theta$ is the constant rate. By Eq. (6.3.8), the pose satisfies

$$
\dot T = [\mathcal S]\dot\theta\; T .
$$

Taking the angle turned, θ, instead of time as the independent variable ($\mathrm dT/\mathrm d\theta = \dot T/\dot\theta$), the equation becomes $\mathrm dT/\mathrm d\theta = [\mathcal S]\,T$. This has exactly the same form as the equations of Sections 4.4.4 and 6.2.4, except that the matrix has gone from 3×3 to 4×4. By the same argument, its solution is

$$
T(\theta) = e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}\; T(0) , \tag{6.4.1}
$$

where $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$ is defined by the matrix exponential series (4.4.7). If the twist is constant in the body frame, then in the same way $\dot T = T[\mathcal V_b]$ gives $T(\theta) = T(0)\,e^{[\mathcal S_b]\theta}$. Once again the rule is "space frame: premultiply; body frame: postmultiply".

The remaining question is: what kind of matrix is $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$?

### 6.4.3 The closed form of the exponential map

**Theorem 6.4.1** Let $\mathcal S = (\omega, v)$ be a screw axis.

(1) If $|\omega| = 1$, then

$$
e^{[\mathcal S]\theta} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{[\omega]\theta} & G(\theta)\,v \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{6.4.2}
$$

$$
G(\theta) = I\theta + (1 - \cos\theta)\,[\omega] + (\theta - \sin\theta)\,[\omega]^2 . \tag{6.4.3}
$$

(2) If ω = 0 and |v| = 1, then

$$
e^{[\mathcal S]\theta} = \begin{pmatrix} I & v\,\theta \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{6.4.4}
$$

*Proof*　First compute the powers of $[\mathcal S]$. By Eq. (6.3.6) and block multiplication,

$$
[\mathcal S]^2 = \begin{pmatrix} [\omega] & v \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} [\omega] & v \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} [\omega]^2 & [\omega]v \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} ,
$$

and so on: for k ≥ 1,

$$
[\mathcal S]^k = \begin{pmatrix} [\omega]^k & [\omega]^{k-1}v \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{6.4.5}
$$

Substituting into the series $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta} = I + \sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\theta^k}{k!}[\mathcal S]^k$, the upper-left block is $\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{\theta^k}{k!}[\omega]^k = e^{[\omega]\theta}$, the lower-right entry is 1 (only the identity matrix for k = 0 contributes), and the upper-right block is

$$
\Big(I\theta + \frac{\theta^2}{2!}[\omega] + \frac{\theta^3}{3!}[\omega]^2 + \frac{\theta^4}{4!}[\omega]^3 + \frac{\theta^5}{5!}[\omega]^4 + \cdots\Big)\,v .
$$

In case (2), [ω] = 0 and only Iθ is left in the brackets, which gives Eq. (6.4.4). In case (1), as in Section 4.4.4, we use $[\omega]^3 = -[\omega]$ (Eq. (4.4.8)), so that $[\omega]^4 = -[\omega]^2$, $[\omega]^5 = [\omega]$, … Collecting the terms in the brackets by $[\omega]$ and $[\omega]^2$:

$$
I\theta + \Big(\frac{\theta^2}{2!} - \frac{\theta^4}{4!} + \frac{\theta^6}{6!} - \cdots\Big)[\omega] + \Big(\frac{\theta^3}{3!} - \frac{\theta^5}{5!} + \frac{\theta^7}{7!} - \cdots\Big)[\omega]^2 .
$$

Comparing with the Taylor series of cosine and sine, the first bracket is $1 - \cos\theta$ and the second is $\theta - \sin\theta$, which is Eq. (6.4.3).　∎

Together, Eqs. (6.4.2) and (6.4.3) are Eq. (12.1.5), quoted in Chapter 12. As for the units of G(θ): θ is in radians (dimensionless), so G(θ)v has the same units as v, namely length.

What is the geometric meaning of Theorem 6.4.1? Substitute $v = -\omega \times q + h\omega$ from Section 6.3 into G(θ)v. Since $[\omega]\omega = 0$, $G(\theta)\,h\omega = h\theta\,\omega$; and

$$
G(\theta)(-[\omega]q) = -\theta[\omega]q - (1 - \cos\theta)[\omega]^2 q - (\theta - \sin\theta)[\omega]^3 q = -\sin\theta\,[\omega]q - (1 - \cos\theta)[\omega]^2 q = (I - e^{[\omega]\theta})\,q ,
$$

where the last step uses Rodrigues' formula (4.4.5). Hence

$$
G(\theta)\,v = (I - e^{[\omega]\theta})\,q + h\theta\,\omega . \tag{6.4.6}
$$

Substituting this into Eq. (6.4.2), $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$ takes the point x to $e^{[\omega]\theta}x + (I - e^{[\omega]\theta})q + h\theta\omega = q + e^{[\omega]\theta}(x - q) + h\theta\,\omega$, which is exactly the screw motion of Definition 6.1.1.

**Theorem 6.4.2** Let $\mathcal S$ be the screw axis through the point q, with direction $\hat s$ and pitch h. Then $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$ is the screw motion that turns through θ about this axis while moving hθ along it.

So the matrix exponential $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$ has a clear geometric interpretation: **screwing by θ along the screw axis $\mathcal S$**.

**Definition 6.4.1 (The exponential map of SE(3))** The map

$$
\exp : \mathrm{se}(3) \to \mathrm{SE}(3) , \qquad [\mathcal S]\theta \mapsto e^{[\mathcal S]\theta} \tag{6.4.7}
$$

is called the **exponential map** of SE(3). The six-dimensional vector $\mathcal S\theta$ is called the **exponential coordinates** of the displacement $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$.

The exponential coordinates $\mathcal S\theta$ generalize the exponential coordinates $\hat\omega\theta$ of rotation in Chapter 4. Their physical meaning is "the displacement reached by moving for 1 s with the constant twist $\mathcal S\theta$". As θ increases continuously from 0, $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$ traces out a curve in SE(3) through I, and it satisfies $e^{[\mathcal S]\alpha}e^{[\mathcal S]\beta} = e^{[\mathcal S](\alpha+\beta)}$. Such a curve is called a **one-parameter subgroup**: a screw motion about one and the same axis can be divided into pieces, or pieces combined, at will.

**Example 6.4.1** For the valve problem at the start of this section, find the pose of the gripper after the handwheel has turned 90°.

Take {s} to be the base frame of the UR5e. The axis of the valve stem is vertical, through the point q = (0.45, 0.20, 0) m, with direction $\hat s = (0, 0, 1)$; turning anticlockwise as seen from above raises the stem, so the pitch is positive. The thread rises 4 mm per turn, so the pitch is $h = 4/(2\pi)$ mm/rad ≈ {{ex6_4.h_mm:.4f}} mm/rad. By Eq. (6.3.13),

$$
\mathcal S = \big(0,\ 0,\ 1,\ 0.20,\ -0.45,\ h\big)^{\mathsf T} ,
$$

where the first two entries of the linear-velocity part are $-\hat s \times q = (q_y, -q_x, 0)$. Before the turn, the gripper is at the rim of the handwheel, 0.12 m from the axis of the stem, with its z axis pointing down:

$$
T_0 = {{ex6_4.T0}} .
$$

For θ = π/2, $e^{[\hat s]\theta} = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, 90^\circ)$, and by Eq. (6.4.3),

$$
G(\pi/2) = {{ex6_4.Gth}} , \qquad G(\pi/2)\,v = {{ex6_4.Gv}}\ \mathrm{m} ,
$$

so that

$$
e^{[\mathcal S]\pi/2} = {{ex6_4.E}} , \qquad T_1 = e^{[\mathcal S]\pi/2}\,T_0 = {{ex6_4.T1}} .
$$

The gripper has gone round the stem to the +y side of the axis, its orientation has turned through 90° with it, and its height has risen by {{ex6_4.rise_mm:.3f}} mm, exactly a quarter of the lead. Note that premultiplication is used here: the screw axis of the handwheel is given in {s}.

Program 6.4.1 computes $e^{[\mathcal S]\pi/2}$ in three ways: by the closed form of Theorem 6.4.1; by taking enough terms of the matrix exponential series (4.4.7); and by writing down directly, following Definition 6.1.1, the transformation "turn 90° about the axis through q, then move hθ along the axis". All three agree. The program also compares the closed form with the series and with SciPy's matrix exponential, one by one, for randomly generated screw axes and angles.

### 6.4.4 The logarithm map

Now the other way round: given a displacement $T = (R, p) \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$, find a screw axis $\mathcal S$ and θ such that $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta} = T$.

By Eq. (6.4.2), the upper-left block must satisfy $e^{[\omega]\theta} = R$, which is exactly the logarithm map of SO(3) (Section 6.2.4). The upper-right block must satisfy $G(\theta)v = p$. So as long as G(θ) is invertible, $v = G(\theta)^{-1}p$. The inverse of G(θ) also has a closed form.

**Lemma 6.4.1** If $|\omega| = 1$ and $0 < \theta < 2\pi$, then G(θ) is invertible, and

$$
G(\theta)^{-1} = \frac{1}{\theta}I - \frac12[\omega] + \Big(\frac1\theta - \frac12\cot\frac\theta2\Big)[\omega]^2 . \tag{6.4.8}
$$

*Proof*　Multiply G(θ) by the right-hand side, expand, and use $[\omega]^3 = -[\omega]$ and $[\omega]^4 = -[\omega]^2$ to reduce everything to a combination of the three terms I, $[\omega]$ and $[\omega]^2$. The coefficient of I is θ · (1/θ) = 1; the coefficients of $[\omega]$ and $[\omega]^2$, simplified with the trigonometric identities $1 - \cos\theta = 2\sin^2\frac\theta2$ and $\sin\theta = 2\sin\frac\theta2\cos\frac\theta2$, are both zero (Exercise 6.4.2).　∎

This yields the following theorem, which also gives the algorithm for the logarithm map.

**Theorem 6.4.3 (The logarithm map of SE(3))** Let $T = (R, p) \in \mathrm{SE}(3)$ be given.

(1) If R = I, then ω = 0, $v = p/|p|$, θ = |p| (if p = 0, then T = I, θ = 0, and the screw axis is undefined).

(2) Otherwise, use the logarithm map of SO(3) to find ω (|ω| = 1) and θ ∈ (0, π] from R, and then take

$$
v = G(\theta)^{-1}\,p . \tag{6.4.9}
$$

Then $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta} = T$ with $\mathcal S = (\omega, v)$. In particular, the exponential map of SE(3) is surjective: every rigid-body displacement is the exponential of some twist.

*Proof*　Verify directly with Theorem 6.4.1. Surjectivity: for any T, the construction above gives a logarithm of it.　∎

"Every rigid-body displacement is the exponential of a twist along some screw axis", translated into geometric language with Theorem 6.4.2, says "every rigid-body displacement is a screw motion", which is exactly Chasles' theorem of Section 6.1. Section 6.1 proved it by a geometric construction; here algebra accomplishes the same thing, and yields an algorithm that can be programmed directly.

**Definition 6.4.2 (The logarithm map of SE(3))** The operation of finding $[\mathcal S]\theta$ from T according to Theorem 6.4.3 is called the **logarithm map** of SE(3), written $[\mathcal S]\theta = \log T$.

As with SO(3), the logarithm is not unique when θ = π, and the screw axis is undefined when θ = 0. A pure translation has infinite pitch, ω = 0, and falls under case (1).

**Example 6.4.2** The UR5e moves from its zero position to the joint angles θ = (30°, −45°, 60°, −15°, 90°, 30°). Find the screw axis, the angle and the pitch of this displacement of the flange.

By the product-of-exponentials formula of Chapter 12, the target pose is

$$
T(\theta) = {{ex6_4.Tt}} .
$$

The pose at the zero position is M. The displacement the flange undergoes in space is $D = T(\theta)M^{-1}$ (so that T(θ) = D M: premultiplication, described in {s}):

$$
D = {{ex6_4.D}} .
$$

Take the logarithm according to Theorem 6.4.3. From tr R we get the angle θ = {{ex6_4.th_log:.4f}} rad, that is, {{ex6_4.th_log_deg:.2f}}°; from $R - R^{\mathsf T}$ we get the axis of rotation; and then v follows from Eqs. (6.4.8) and (6.4.9). The result is

$$
\mathcal S = {{ex6_4.S_log}} .
$$

Read off the geometric quantities with Eqs. (6.3.11) and (6.3.12) of Section 6.3: the direction of the axis is $\hat s = {{ex6_4.s2}}$, the point of the axis nearest the origin is $q = {{ex6_4.q2}}$ m, and the pitch is h = ${{ex6_4.h2:.4f}}$ m/rad. That is, taking the flange from the zero position to the target pose is equivalent to turning through {{ex6_4.th_log_deg:.2f}}° about this downward-slanting axis while moving hθ = ${{ex6_4.d2:.4f}}$ m along it (the minus sign means along $-\hat s$, that is, slanting upwards).

Program 6.4.1 checks this by two independent methods: SciPy's general matrix logarithm logm (a numerical algorithm that does not use the formulas of this section) gives the same $[\mathcal S]\theta$ as above; and substituting the $\mathcal S$ and θ found back into Theorem 6.4.1 gives a matrix that differs from D by less than $10^{-12}$.

Figure 6.4.1 shows this screw axis. If the flange is "screwed" along this axis at a uniform rate, that is, if we take $T(s) = e^{[\mathcal S]\theta s}M$ with s going from 0 to 1, the flange centre travels along a piece of helix, always at a distance of {{ex6_4.r0:.4f}} m from the screw axis. This method of interpolation is called **screw interpolation**. It differs from the common method of "a straight line for the position and spherical linear interpolation for the orientation": at the midpoint, the flange centres given by the two methods are about {{ex6_4.dev_mid_mm:.0f}} mm apart. Which is better depends on the task: screw interpolation is the "most direct" path in SE(3), and it does not depend on the choice of frame (Section 6.5); straight-line interpolation makes the tool centre point move on a straight line and suits processes such as welding and gluing, which require a straight path. Chapter 22 on trajectory planning compares them in detail.

**Example 6.4.3** Two special cases.

(1) The end-effector of a gantry robot only translates by (0.3, 0.4, 0) m, without rotating. R = I, so by Theorem 6.4.3 (1), θ = 0.5 m and $\mathcal S = (0, 0, 0, 0.6, 0.8, 0)$: a translation of 0.5 m in the direction (0.6, 0.8, 0). Here θ is in metres rather than radians.

(2) A part turns 180° about the vertical axis through the point (0.1, 0, 0) m. The trace of R is −1 and the angle is θ = π; by the method of Section 4.4.6 for θ = π we get $\omega = \pm(0, 0, 1)$, and both signs are correct. Then v follows from Eq. (6.4.9); the two choices give the same axis, with opposite senses of rotation. Program 6.4.1 verifies that substituting either back into the exponential map gives the original displacement.

### 6.4.5 An everyday example

When you screw a bottle cap on or off, the motion of the cap is just $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$: the axis is the centre line of the bottle neck and the pitch is fixed by the thread. Turn it twice, θ = 4π, and the cap is back at its original orientation but has risen by two leads; the rotation matrix is then I, but the translation is not zero. So the information "how many turns" is lost in the rotation matrix, and the logarithm map can give only the shortest answer, with θ ∈ [0, π], namely "no turn, translation only". When a robot drives screws or opens valves, the controller must keep its own record of the total angle turned; it cannot expect to recover it from the pose.

Both the exponential map and the logarithm map are carried out in a fixed frame {s}. In another frame, the six components of the screw axis of the same screw motion change, but the axis itself, the angle and the pitch do not. The next section studies this transformation.

**Engineer's notes**

- Software libraries do not agree on the inputs of the "SE(3) exponential": some take (ω, v), others (v, ω); some take $\mathcal S\theta$, others $\mathcal S$ and θ separately. Before calling one, test it once with a pure translation and once with a pure rotation about the z axis.
- When θ is close to 0 in the logarithm map, the 1/θ terms of Eq. (6.4.8) are very large, but they cancel against the large term of cot(θ/2)/2; computing straight from the formula loses significant digits. Mature libraries switch to the Taylor expansion $G^{-1} \approx I/\theta - [\omega]/2 + (\theta/12)[\omega]^2$ when θ is very small.
- Screw interpolation, quaternion spherical linear interpolation and straight-line interpolation of the position all start and end at the same points but take different paths in between. The "linear move" (MoveL) on a teach pendant is a straight line for the position plus interpolation of the orientation, not screw interpolation.
