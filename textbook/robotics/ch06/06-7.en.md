### 6.7.1 The problem

A differential-drive AGV has stopped in front of a workstation, 4 cm to the left of the required position. The two drive wheels of a differential AGV can only roll in the direction of the body and cannot slip sideways, so it cannot simply translate to the right. Yet an experienced operator knows a trick: drive forwards a short way, turn left on the spot through a small angle, back up by the same distance, then turn right again. After these four moves the heading is restored, but the body has shifted a little to the right. Repeating this a few times, the AGV can "shuffle" sideways into place.

Behind this phenomenon is a general fact: if two motions are carried out alternately and then each is undone, in general we do not return to where we started; the net effect is a new motion, and this new motion is often one that neither of the original motions can produce directly. This section uses the Lie bracket to describe this net effect quantitatively. We shall see that the Lie bracket is exactly the precise statement, at the infinitesimal level, of the fact from Section 4.2 that "the order of rotations cannot be exchanged".

### 6.7.2 The commutator of matrices and the Lie bracket in so(3)

The order in which two square matrices are multiplied cannot be exchanged at will. The most direct measure of "how much exchanging the order matters" is the difference between the two products.

**Definition 6.7.1 (Lie bracket)** For square matrices A and B of the same order,

$$
[A, B] = AB - BA \tag{6.7.1}
$$

is called the **commutator** of A and B, also called their **Lie bracket**.

Consider so(3) first. Let $[\omega_1]$ and $[\omega_2]$ be two skew-symmetric matrices. Exercise 3.2.4 of Chapter 3 already asked the reader to prove from the Jacobi identity that

$$
[\omega_1][\omega_2] - [\omega_2][\omega_1] = [\omega_1 \times \omega_2] . \tag{6.7.2}
$$

Here is a direct proof: for any vector x, the left-hand side acting on x gives $\omega_1\times(\omega_2\times x) - \omega_2 \times (\omega_1 \times x)$, which the vector triple product formula (3.2.14) expands into $\omega_2(\omega_1\cdot x) - x(\omega_1\cdot\omega_2) - \omega_1(\omega_2\cdot x) + x(\omega_2\cdot\omega_1) = \omega_2(\omega_1\cdot x) - \omega_1(\omega_2\cdot x)$; by Eq. (3.2.15), this is exactly $(\omega_1\times\omega_2)\times x$.

Eq. (6.7.2) has two meanings. First, the Lie bracket of two elements of so(3) is again in so(3), so the Lie bracket is a kind of "multiplication" on so(3). Second, this multiplication is the familiar cross product: **the Lie bracket of so(3) is the cross product of angular velocities**. Three-dimensional vectors together with the cross product themselves form a Lie algebra (defined at the end of Section 6.7.3).

### 6.7.3 The Lie bracket in se(3)

For two elements $[\mathcal V_1]$ and $[\mathcal V_2]$ of se(3), we compute with block matrices:

$$
[\mathcal V_1][\mathcal V_2] - [\mathcal V_2][\mathcal V_1] = \begin{pmatrix} [\omega_1][\omega_2] - [\omega_2][\omega_1] & [\omega_1]v_2 - [\omega_2]v_1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} [\omega_1 \times \omega_2] & \omega_1 \times v_2 - \omega_2 \times v_1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{6.7.3}
$$

The right-hand side is again in se(3). We denote the corresponding six-dimensional vector by $[\mathcal V_1, \mathcal V_2]$:

$$
[\mathcal V_1, \mathcal V_2] = \begin{pmatrix} \omega_1 \times \omega_2 \\ \omega_1 \times v_2 - \omega_2 \times v_1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{6.7.4}
$$

It is a linear function of $\mathcal V_2$, and can be written as a 6×6 matrix times $\mathcal V_2$. Noting that $-\omega_2 \times v_1 = v_1 \times \omega_2 = [v_1]\omega_2$, we define

$$
[\mathrm{ad}_{\mathcal V}] = \begin{pmatrix} [\omega] & 0 \\ [v] & [\omega] \end{pmatrix} , \tag{6.7.5}
$$

and then

$$
[\mathcal V_1, \mathcal V_2] = [\mathrm{ad}_{\mathcal V_1}]\,\mathcal V_2 . \tag{6.7.6}
$$

$[\mathrm{ad}_{\mathcal V}]$ is called the Lie bracket matrix of $\mathcal V$. Compare it with $[\mathrm{Ad}_T]$ in Eq. (6.5.4): the two have exactly the same structure, with R replaced by [ω] and p by v. This is no coincidence: Theorem 6.7.3 below will show that $[\mathrm{ad}_{\mathcal V}]$ is precisely the "derivative" of $[\mathrm{Ad}_T]$.

**Theorem 6.7.1** The Lie bracket has the following properties:

1. Bilinearity: it is linear in each argument;
2. Antisymmetry: $[\mathcal V_1, \mathcal V_2] = -[\mathcal V_2, \mathcal V_1]$, and in particular $[\mathcal V, \mathcal V] = 0$;
3. The Jacobi identity:
$$
[\mathcal V_1, [\mathcal V_2, \mathcal V_3]] + [\mathcal V_2, [\mathcal V_3, \mathcal V_1]] + [\mathcal V_3, [\mathcal V_1, \mathcal V_2]] = 0 . \tag{6.7.7}
$$

*Proof*　(1) and (2) follow directly from the definition (6.7.1). (3) For matrices, expand $[A, [B, C]] = ABC - ACB - BCA + CBA$; adding the three cyclic permutations, the twelve terms cancel in pairs.　∎

A linear space equipped with a "multiplication" satisfying these three properties is called, in mathematics, a **Lie algebra**. When Sections 6.2 and 6.3 called so(3) and se(3) Lie algebras, they used only the meaning "tangent space"; now the description can be completed: they are linear spaces equipped with a Lie bracket. This is also the structure that Lie discovered in the 1870s while studying continuous groups of transformations: the extent to which the multiplication of a group fails to commute is recorded completely, near the identity, by the Lie bracket.

### 6.7.4 The geometric meaning of the Lie bracket: the net effect of alternating motions

**Theorem 6.7.2** Let A and B be square matrices and ε a small quantity. Then

$$
e^{\varepsilon A}\,e^{\varepsilon B}\,e^{-\varepsilon A}\,e^{-\varepsilon B} = I + \varepsilon^2[A, B] + O(\varepsilon^3) . \tag{6.7.8}
$$

*Proof*　Expand each exponential to second order, for example $e^{\varepsilon A} = I + \varepsilon A + \frac{\varepsilon^2}{2}A^2 + O(\varepsilon^3)$, and multiply the four factors. The first-order term is $\varepsilon(A + B - A - B) = 0$. The second-order terms are of two kinds: the square terms of each factor, totalling $\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2}(A^2 + B^2 + A^2 + B^2) = \varepsilon^2(A^2 + B^2)$; and the products of the first-order terms of two different factors, six in all, taken in the order of the factors: $AB - A^2 - AB - BA - B^2 + AB = AB - BA - A^2 - B^2$ (times $\varepsilon^2$). Adding the two kinds, $A^2$ and $B^2$ cancel, leaving only $\varepsilon^2(AB - BA)$.　∎

Taking A and B to be (the 4×4 matrices of) two twists, the left-hand side of Eq. (6.7.8) is the total displacement of "move ε along A, move ε along B, move back ε along A, move back ε along B". If the two motions commute, this displacement is zero; otherwise, to second order, it is a small motion along $[A, B]$. So **the Lie bracket is the "direction" of the net effect of two alternating motions**, and its size is ε².

**Example 6.7.1** The two basic motions of a differential AGV, written as body twists in the body frame of the vehicle, are: forward $\mathcal V_f = (0, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0)$ (unit speed in the heading direction) and turning left on the spot $\mathcal V_r = (0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0)$ (about the vertical axis through the centre of the body). Find their Lie bracket, and the net displacement after "forward 0.2 m, left 0.2 rad, back 0.2 m, right 0.2 rad".

By Eq. (6.7.4), with $\omega_f = 0$, $v_f = \hat x$, $\omega_r = \hat z$, $v_r = 0$:

$$
[\mathcal V_f, \mathcal V_r] = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \times \hat z \\ 0 \times 0 - \hat z \times \hat x \end{pmatrix} = (0, 0, 0, 0, -1, 0)^{\mathsf T} ,
$$

which is a translation in the −y direction (to the right of the body), exactly the motion the differential AGV cannot make directly. The four moves are all described in the body frame of the vehicle and postmultiply one after another (the rule "space frame: premultiply; body frame: postmultiply" of Sections 6.2 and 6.4), so the total displacement is $e^{[\mathcal V_f]\varepsilon}e^{[\mathcal V_r]\varepsilon}e^{-[\mathcal V_f]\varepsilon}e^{-[\mathcal V_r]\varepsilon}$ with ε = 0.2. Program 6.7.1 computes: the heading is restored, and the net displacement is x = {{ex6_7.x02:.4f}} m, y = ${{ex6_7.y02:.4f}}$ m. Eq. (6.7.8) predicts $\varepsilon^2(0, -1) = (0, -0.04)$ m, less than 1 cm from the exact value. This is also easy to check geometrically: the exact displacement is $(\varepsilon(1 - \cos\varepsilon),\ -\varepsilon\sin\varepsilon)$, which expanded to second order is $(0, -\varepsilon^2)$.

Halving ε to 0.1 changes the sideways shift to ${{ex6_7.y01:.4f}}$ m; the ratio of the two is {{ex6_7.ratio:.2f}}, close to 4. And the ratio of the deviations of the total displacement from $e^{\varepsilon^2[\mathcal V_f, \mathcal V_r]}$ is {{ex6_7.err_ratio:.2f}}, close to 8, showing that the error is indeed of order ε³ (Figure 6.7.1).

This example is a typical case of a **nonholonomic constraint** in mobile robotics: the velocity of the AGV is subject to the constraint "no sideways slipping", and at each instant it can move in only two directions, yet through the Lie bracket it can reach any pose in the plane. In 1938 and 1939, Petr Konstantinovich Rashevsky and Wei-Liang Chow independently proved the general result: as long as the directions of motion, together with their repeated Lie brackets, span all directions, the system can get from any state to any other state. Chapter 19 returns to this question when discussing the kinematics of mobile robots.

**Example 6.7.2** In {s}, take two fixed axes: the axis $\mathcal S_1$ of joint 1 and the axis $\mathcal S_2$ of joint 2 of the UR5e in the zero position (Table 12.1.1). The arm holds a part and, by inverse kinematics, makes the part turn through ε about axis 1, through ε about axis 2, back through ε about axis 1 and back through ε about axis 2, in that order. What is the net motion?

Both axes are fixed in {s}, so every move premultiplies (the rule "space frame: premultiply; body frame: postmultiply" of Sections 6.2 and 6.4), and the total displacement is

$$
e^{-[\mathcal S_2]\varepsilon}\,e^{-[\mathcal S_1]\varepsilon}\,e^{[\mathcal S_2]\varepsilon}\,e^{[\mathcal S_1]\varepsilon} .
$$

The left-to-right order of the matrices is the reverse of the order of the moves. Comparing with Eq. (6.7.8), we must take $A = -\mathcal S_2$ and $B = -\mathcal S_1$, and the net effect is $\varepsilon^2[-\mathcal S_2, -\mathcal S_1] = \varepsilon^2[\mathcal S_2, \mathcal S_1] = -\varepsilon^2[\mathcal S_1, \mathcal S_2]$. By Eq. (6.7.4),

$$
[\mathcal S_1, \mathcal S_2] = {{ex6_7.L12}} .
$$

By Eqs. (6.3.11) and (6.3.12) we read off: this is a pure rotation about an axis in the x direction through the point (0, 0, 0.163) m, with zero pitch. This axis passes exactly through the intersection of the two joint axes (the centre of the shoulder), in the direction $\hat\omega_1 \times \hat\omega_2 = \hat z \times (-\hat y) = \hat x$. So by turning alternately about two fixed axes, the part turns through about ε² about a third direction, in the sense of $-\hat x$ (because the net effect is $-\varepsilon^2[\mathcal S_1, \mathcal S_2]$). With ε = 0.1 rad, Program 6.7.1 finds by the logarithm map that the axis of the net rotation is ${{ex6_7.s_net}}$ and the angle is about {{ex6_7.rot_deg:.3f}}°, while ε² corresponds to {{ex6_7.pred_rot_deg:.3f}}°. This is also the infinitesimal version of the book-turning example of Section 4.2 (Example 4.2.2: turning first about x and then about z gives a different result from turning first about z and then about x).

The two axes here must be fixed in space. If the UR5e simply turns its joints 1 and 2 back and forth in the same order, the arm returns exactly to the zero position: by the product-of-exponentials formula, the pose of the end-effector is determined by the joint angles alone, and after the four moves all the joint angles are back to zero (Program 6.7.1 checks this too). Geometrically, the axis of joint 2 is carried along by joint 1, and when "joint 1 turns back", the axis of joint 2 turns back with it. The differential AGV is different: its position is not determined by the angles of its two wheels, and when the wheels turn back to their original angles, the body does not necessarily return to where it was. This is exactly why the alternating motions in Example 6.7.1 can produce a net displacement, and it is also what a nonholonomic constraint means.

### 6.7.5 Two frequently used results

**The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula.** Section 6.2.5 said that "turning about this axis and then about that one" is not the same as "turning about the average of the two at once". Eq. (6.7.8) is just one special case of this fact. In general, the product of two exponentials is again an exponential, whose exponent is given by the following series:

$$
\log\big(e^{A}e^{B}\big) = A + B + \frac12[A, B] + \frac1{12}\big([A, [A, B]] + [B, [B, A]]\big) + \cdots \tag{6.7.9}
$$

The formula is named after the British mathematicians Henry Frederick Baker and John Edward Campbell and the German mathematician Felix Hausdorff, who studied it in turn between 1897 and 1906. Its main point is that, besides A + B, the exponent of the product contains a string of correction terms made up entirely of Lie brackets; when A and B commute, all the correction terms vanish and $e^Ae^B = e^{A+B}$ (the two rotations about the same axis in Exercise 4.4.3 are a special case). Program 6.7.1 checks the first three terms with random twists: the error after truncating beyond ½[A, B] is a third-order small quantity. In orientation estimation and motion planning, this formula is often used to approximate the composition of two small motions by a single motion.

**The derivative of the adjoint matrix.** Let the body twist be $\mathcal V_b$, that is, $\dot T = T[\mathcal V_b]$. Then

$$
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}[\mathrm{Ad}_{T}] = [\mathrm{Ad}_T]\,[\mathrm{ad}_{\mathcal V_b}] . \tag{6.7.10}
$$

**Theorem 6.7.3** Eq. (6.7.10) holds.

*Proof*　For any fixed $\mathcal X$, by Eq. (6.5.5), $[[\mathrm{Ad}_T]\mathcal X] = T[\mathcal X]T^{-1}$. Differentiating with respect to t and using $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}T^{-1} = -T^{-1}\dot TT^{-1}$:

$$
\dot T[\mathcal X]T^{-1} - T[\mathcal X]T^{-1}\dot TT^{-1} = T[\mathcal V_b][\mathcal X]T^{-1} - T[\mathcal X][\mathcal V_b]T^{-1} = T\,[[\mathcal V_b, \mathcal X]]\,T^{-1} = \big[[\mathrm{Ad}_T][\mathrm{ad}_{\mathcal V_b}]\mathcal X\big] . \qquad ∎
$$

This result is used often later on. When Chapter 16 finds the time derivative of the Jacobian matrix, whose columns have the form $[\mathrm{Ad}]\mathcal S_i$, differentiating requires it; in the Newton–Euler recursion of Chapter 25, the acceleration of a link contains an $[\mathrm{ad}_{\mathcal V}]$ term, while what acts on wrenches is its transpose $[\mathrm{ad}_{\mathcal V}]^{\mathsf T}$, for the same reason that wrenches transform by $[\mathrm{Ad}]^{\mathsf T}$ in Section 6.6.

### 6.7.6 Everyday examples

Parallel parking is the most familiar example of the Lie bracket. A car can neither move sideways nor turn on the spot; all it can do is "drive" and "steer". Steering and then driving gives "turning"; alternating turning with driving gives a "sideways shift". That is why parallel parking takes several shuffles back and forth, each moving the car sideways only a little. Players of the Rubik's Cube also know this structure: twist one face, then another face, then twist each of the two faces back; most of the small cubes return to their places and only a few change places. Such "commutator" sequences are the basic tool for solving the cube.

**Engineer's notes**

- The net effect of alternating motions is a second-order small quantity, so correcting a position by shuffling back and forth is very inefficient: to shift 4 cm sideways, one set is enough with ε = 0.2, but sixteen sets are needed with ε = 0.05. When an AGV docks, the more usual approach is to plan the approach curve in advance rather than shuffle afterwards.
- A robot composes small motions in every control cycle. By Eq. (6.7.9), adding the exponential coordinates of two small rotations directly gives an error of the order of ½[A, B]; the faster the rotation and the longer the cycle, the larger the error. High-accuracy orientation integration should multiply step by step as in Section 6.2.5.
- The Lie bracket matrix $[\mathrm{ad}_{\mathcal V}]$ has the same structure as the adjoint matrix, so in a program the two can share one function: given (R, p) it returns $[\mathrm{Ad}]$, given ([ω], v) it returns $[\mathrm{ad}]$.
