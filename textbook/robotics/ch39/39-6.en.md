### 39.6.1 The problem: conforming and stable

The WenQuest digital factory produces the WQR-105 gear reducer. The bearing seats of its output shaft SH-301 are ground on a grinding machine; a robot places each ground shaft on a measuring station, which measures the diameter, compares it with Ø35 k6 on the drawing ({{ex39_6.lo:.3f}}–{{ex39_6.hi:.3f}} mm), decides whether it conforms; the robot then puts the shaft in the bin for good parts or the scrap bin.

There are two questions here. First, the measurement itself has an uncertainty: if the measured value is within tolerance, is the true value necessarily within tolerance? How should the decision rule be set? Second, is it enough that every part is judged conforming? If the condition of the grinding machine is slowly getting worse, it is already too late to act once scrap appears. The first question is answered by the decision rule, the second by statistical process control.

### 39.6.2 Decision rules and the guard band

Let the measured value be y and the expanded uncertainty U (Section 39.1); the true value lies in $[y - U, y + U]$ with high probability. If the measured value is very close to a tolerance limit, for example y is only just below the upper limit, the true value may already be outside the tolerance. If the part is still judged conforming, a nonconforming part is let through; this is called a false acceptance.

To make sure that parts judged conforming also have true values within tolerance, the acceptance zone is narrowed by U at each end of the tolerance:

$$
L + U \le y \le H - U \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{accept} , \tag{39.6.1}
$$

where L and H are the lower and upper tolerance limits, and the strip cut off at each end is called the **guard band**. The price is that parts whose true value is within tolerance but whose measured value falls in the guard band are also judged nonconforming; this is called a false rejection. The wider the guard band, the fewer false acceptances and the more false rejections.

So the uncertainty of the measuring instrument must be much smaller than the tolerance. A common rule of thumb is that U should not exceed 1/10 of the tolerance, 1/4 at most; otherwise the guard bands eat up too much of the tolerance and many good parts are rejected.

### 39.6.3 Control charts

Judging part by part can only pick out scrap; it cannot prevent scrap from being made. The idea of **statistical process control** is to take a few parts from the line at regular intervals and judge by statistical methods whether the machining process is still in a stable state. As long as the process is stable, the scatter of part sizes is only random variation caused by many small factors; once a special cause appears, such as grinding-wheel wear, a chipped tool or a loose fixture, the data show a pattern different from random variation. Detecting such patterns early allows action before scrap appears.

The most widely used tool is the X̄–R **control chart** proposed by Shewhart. Each time, n consecutive parts (commonly n = 5) are taken as a subgroup, and the subgroup mean $\bar x$ and subgroup range R (largest minus smallest) are computed. After a number of subgroups have been collected, the grand mean $\bar{\bar x}$ and the average range $\bar R$ are found, and the control limits are

$$
\text{X̄ chart: } \ \bar{\bar x} \pm A_2 \bar R , \qquad \text{R chart: } \ \text{upper } D_4 \bar R ,\ \text{lower } D_3 \bar R , \tag{39.6.2}
$$

For n = 5, $A_2 = {{ex39_6.A2:.3f}}$, $D_3 = 0$ and $D_4 = {{ex39_6.D4:.3f}}$. These constants place the control limits at roughly ±3 standard deviations of the subgroup mean. When the process is stable, the probability that a subgroup mean falls outside the control limits is only about 0.3%.

Note that control limits and tolerance limits are two different things: tolerance limits are set by the designer, while control limits are calculated from the process's own data and describe the range of variation the process "should" show when it is stable.

To judge whether the process has gone out of control, the following rules for detecting special causes are commonly used:

1. One point beyond a control limit;
2. 6 points in a row steadily rising or falling;
3. 9 points in a row on the same side of the centre line.

The last two are aimed at slow trends and shifts: every point is still within the control limits, but random variation is almost never in the same direction that many times in a row.

### 39.6.4 Process capability

Whether a process is capable of producing conforming parts consistently is measured by two indices. The within-subgroup standard deviation $\sigma_w = \bar R / d_2$ ($d_2 = {{ex39_6.D2:.3f}}$ for n = 5) reflects short-term random variation; the standard deviation s of all the data also includes the drift between subgroups.

$$
C_p = \frac{H - L}{6\,\sigma_w} , \qquad P_{pk} = \frac{\min(H - \bar x,\ \bar x - L)}{3\,s} . \tag{39.6.3}
$$

$C_p$ looks only at the spread, not at the location; it says "how good the process could be if the mean were centred in the tolerance and there were no drift". $P_{pk}$ uses the actual mean and the full spread, and says how good the process actually is. Usually $P_{pk} \ge 1.33$ is required, and in any case no less than 1. A large gap between the two means that the problem lies not in random variation but in an offset or drift of the mean. By convention there are also $C_{pk}$, which takes location into account, and $P_p$, which does not; this section uses only $C_p$ and $P_{pk}$, the two indices at "either end", for comparison.

The retrospective analysis uses "future" data, which is impossible in real-time production. In real-time use, each time a subgroup is completed, the control limits can only be calculated from the subgroups so far. Checking in this way from subgroup 4 onwards, the first alarm comes at subgroup {{ex39_6.on_g}} (part {{ex39_6.on_part}}, {{ex39_6.on_rule}}), more than 30 parts before the first scrap part. Real factories first calculate the control limits from data of a stable period, fix them, and then use them for later monitoring.

By Eq. In fact $\bar R$ is also inflated by the {{ex39_6.r_out}} subgroups that contain a dressing; without them $\sigma_w$ is only {{ex39_6.sig_w_clean_um:.2f}} μm, so the short-term capability of the grinding machine is even better than $C_p$ suggests.

**Example 39.6.3** Change the dressing interval from "by wheel life" (about 28 parts) to once every 14 parts and produce another 100 parts: {{ex39_6.n_out2}} are out of tolerance, $s = {{ex39_6.s_all2_um:.2f}}$ μm and $P_{pk} = {{ex39_6.ppk2:.2f}}$, now above 1 but not yet 1.33.

More frequent dressing increases both wheel consumption and downtime. Another approach is wear compensation: the machine estimates wheel wear from the number of parts ground and corrects the infeed automatically, keeping the diameter near the centre of the tolerance. Which of the two is better depends on the costs of wheels, labour hours and scrap, and this is exactly the kind of question the digital factory can work out in simulation first.

### 39.6.5 Statistical process control in everyday life

How much water is actually in a supermarket bottle of mineral water labelled 550 mL? The valves on the filling line gradually build up scale, and the fill volume slowly changes with it. The factory weighs a few bottles at regular intervals and plots them on a control chart; when a trend appears it cleans or adjusts the valves, rather than waiting for consumers to complain that the bottles are "not full". National regulations set mandatory limits on the net content of prepackaged goods, and control charts like this are found everywhere in food and pharmaceutical factories.

**Engineer's notes**

- An automatic inspection station should store the measured value, the decision and the time for every part, not just "pass/fail". Only with the data can control charts be drawn and problems traced.
- Control limits should be calculated from data of a period when the process was stable, then fixed and used for later monitoring. When the process itself has a trend, as in Example 39.6.2, the first job of the control chart is to reveal that trend; once the cause has been found and removed, the control limits are recalculated.
- A control-chart alarm does not necessarily mean scrap has been made; it signals that "a special cause is at work". Responding to an alarm means finding the cause, not just resetting the machine and leaving it at that, or the problem will come back.
- Control charts are not only for part sizes. A robot's position repeatability, tightening torque and weld strength can all be sampled regularly and plotted to watch for slow deterioration.
