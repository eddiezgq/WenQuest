### 39.2.1 The problem: how to measure a change of a fraction of an ohm

The force sensor in a robot's wrist, an electronic scale and a torque sensor all have at their core a spring element with strain gauges bonded to its surface (Chapter 38). Under load the spring element deforms slightly; the strain gauges stretch or shorten with it, and their resistance changes. The relative change in resistance is proportional to the strain:

$$
\frac{\Delta R}{R} = K\,\varepsilon , \tag{39.2.1}
$$

where ε is the strain (the relative change in length) and K is called the gauge factor; for the usual constantan foil strain gauges K ≈ 2.

The trouble is that this change is too small.

**Example 39.2.1** A 350 Ω strain gauge with K = 2 is subjected to 500 µε (an elongation of 0.05%). By how much does its resistance change? If it is connected in a quarter bridge with 5 V excitation, what is the output voltage?

By Eq. (39.2.1), $\Delta R = 350 \times 2 \times 500 \times 10^{-6} = {{ex39_2.dR:.2f}}$ Ω, only one part in a thousand. If we tried to measure such a change directly with an ohmmeter, the lead resistance, the contact resistance and the effect of temperature would all be larger than the change itself. We shall see below that with the strain gauge connected in a bridge, the output is about {{ex39_2.vq_mV:.3f}} mV. A signal of a few millivolts, once amplified, can be fed to an analogue-to-digital converter.

### 39.2.2 The Wheatstone bridge

Figure 39.2.1(a) shows the **Wheatstone bridge**: four resistors form two voltage dividers connected in parallel across the excitation voltage $V_{ex}$, and the output is the voltage difference between the two divider points. The voltages at the left and right divider points are

$$
V_m = V_{ex}\frac{R_b}{R_a + R_b}, \qquad V_p = V_{ex}\frac{R_d}{R_c + R_d}, \qquad V_o = V_p - V_m . \tag{39.2.2}
$$

When $R_a/R_b = R_c/R_d$, the two divider points are at the same voltage and $V_o = 0$; the bridge is then said to be balanced. This is exactly where the cleverness of the bridge lies: the output reflects only the imbalance of the four resistors, and the large 5 V common level of the excitation cancels between the two sides. So we can amplify just the few millivolts of difference, instead of having to resolve a change of 1 mV within 5 V.

**Quarter bridge.** Of the four arms only $R_d$ is a strain gauge, $R_d = R(1 + x)$ with $x = K\varepsilon$; the other three are fixed resistors of value R. Substituting into Eq. (39.2.2),

$$
V_o = V_{ex}\left(\frac{1 + x}{2 + x} - \frac12\right) = V_{ex}\,\frac{x}{4 + 2x} \approx \frac{V_{ex}}{4}\,K\varepsilon . \tag{39.2.3}
$$

The final approximation holds for x ≪ 1. The 2x in the denominator makes the output not strictly proportional to the strain; this is the non-linearity of the quarter bridge. In Example 39.2.1 the approximate value is {{ex39_2.vq_lin_mV:.3f}} mV and the exact value {{ex39_2.vq_mV:.4f}} mV, so the approximation is {{ex39_2.nonlin_q:.3f}}% too large; at 1000 µε it is {{ex39_2.nonlin_q2:.2f}}% too large. For a high-precision sensor this can no longer be ignored.

**Half bridge and full bridge.** On a spring element one can often find one place in tension and another in compression, with strains equal in size and opposite in sign. Putting a gauge in tension at $R_d$ and one in compression at $R_c$ gives a half bridge; using strain gauges in all four arms, two in tension and two in compression, with opposite arms of the same sign, gives a full bridge. Let the gauges in tension have resistance $R(1+x)$ and those in compression $R(1-x)$; substituting into Eq. (39.2.2) gives

$$
V_o^{\text{half bridge}} = \frac{V_{ex}}{2}\,K\varepsilon , \qquad V_o^{\text{full bridge}} = V_{ex}\,K\varepsilon . \tag{39.2.4}
$$

Both are exact straight lines, with no approximation.

**Example 39.2.2** Continuing Example 39.2.1, with ε = 500 µε, compare the outputs of the three configurations.

The quarter, half and full bridges give {{ex39_2.vq_mV:.3f}} mV, {{ex39_2.vh_mV:.1f}} mV and {{ex39_2.vf_mV:.1f}} mV respectively; the sensitivity of the full bridge is four times that of the quarter bridge.

The full bridge has an even more important advantage: temperature compensation. A change in temperature increases the resistance of all four strain gauges by the same proportion, which amounts to multiplying all four arms by the same factor; both ratios in Eq. (39.2.2) stay the same, and so does the output. The quarter bridge cannot do this: only one arm changes with temperature, and temperature drift shows up directly as zero drift. That is why industrial force sensors almost all use a full bridge, and express their sensitivity as the **rated output**, that is, the output at full scale per volt of excitation, in mV/V.

### 39.2.3 The instrumentation amplifier

The output of a full bridge is still very small. A force sensor with a range of 100 N and a rated output of 2 mV/V gives only {{ex39_2.v_fs_mV:.0f}} mV at full scale with 5 V excitation. To feed it into an analogue-to-digital converter with a 2.5 V reference, it must be amplified about {{ex39_2.g_need:.0f}} times.

Amplifying a bridge signal has a particular difficulty. The voltages at both bridge outputs are near $V_{ex}/2 = {{ex39_2.cm:.1f}}$ V; what we want is their difference, not the voltages themselves. The part of the voltage common to both outputs is called the common-mode voltage, and the difference between them the differential-mode voltage. The amplifier must amplify a differential-mode voltage of a few millivolts several hundred times while turning a blind eye to a common-mode voltage of 2.5 V.

The job is done by the **instrumentation amplifier**, whose most common form consists of three operational amplifiers (Figure 39.2.2). An operational amplifier is a differential amplifier of extremely high gain; with negative feedback it obeys two simple rules: the two input terminals are at the same voltage (the "virtual short"), and the inputs draw no current (the "virtual open circuit"). With these two rules the circuit in the figure can be analysed:

- The first two op-amps, $A_1$ and $A_2$, form the input stage. By the virtual short, their inverting inputs are at $V_m$ and $V_p$ respectively, so the voltage across the gain resistor $R_g$ is just $V_p - V_m$, and the current through it is $(V_p - V_m)/R_g$. By the virtual open, all of this current flows through the upper and lower feedback resistors R, so the difference between the two op-amp outputs is $(V_p - V_m)(1 + 2R/R_g)$. The common-mode voltage appears on both outputs at once and is not amplified.
- The third op-amp, $A_3$, is connected as a difference amplifier with four equal resistors; it outputs the difference of its two inputs and removes the remaining common-mode voltage.

The gain of the whole circuit is

$$
G = 1 + \frac{2R}{R_g} . \tag{39.2.5}
$$

A single resistor $R_g$ sets the gain, and the inputs draw almost no current, so they do not load the bridge. Common monolithic instrumentation amplifiers have R built into the chip, for example with 2R = 49.4 kΩ.

**Example 39.2.3** For the force sensor above (100 N, 2 mV/V, 5 V excitation), choose the gain resistor of the instrumentation amplifier (2R = 49.4 kΩ) so that the full-scale output does not exceed 2.5 V, and find the force resolution with a 12-bit analogue-to-digital converter.

The gain needed at full scale is 2.5 V / 10 mV = {{ex39_2.g_need:.0f}}; from Eq. (39.2.5), $R_g = 49.4\ \text{k}\Omega / ({{ex39_2.g_need:.0f}} - 1) = {{ex39_2.rg_ideal:.1f}}$ Ω. Resistors can only be chosen from standard series. Near this value the E96 series has 196 Ω and 200 Ω. With 196 Ω the gain is greater than 250, the full-scale output exceeds 2.5 V and the analogue-to-digital converter overflows; so we choose {{ex39_2.rg_sel:.0f}} Ω, which gives $G = {{ex39_2.gain:.1f}}$ and a full-scale output of {{ex39_2.v_out_fs:.3f}} V, leaving a margin of about 1%.

A 12-bit analogue-to-digital converter divides 0 to 2.5 V into 4096 steps of {{ex39_2.lsb_mV:.3f}} mV each (Section 39.3). Converted to force, one code corresponds to {{ex39_2.f_res:.4f}} N.

**Common-mode rejection.** A real instrumentation amplifier cannot ignore the common-mode voltage completely. Its ability to do so is measured by the common-mode rejection ratio, CMRR: the ratio of the differential-mode gain to the common-mode gain, usually expressed in decibels. Taking CMRR = {{ex39_2.cmrr_db:.0f}} dB (that is, $10^5$), the 2.5 V common-mode voltage is equivalent to an extra differential signal of {{ex39_2.cm_rti_uV:.0f}} µV at the input, or {{ex39_2.cm_err_N:.2f}} N. Because the excitation voltage is constant, this error is a constant zero offset, which can be subtracted in calibration (Section 39.5) together with the imbalance of the bridge; but if the excitation voltage fluctuates, it turns into noise, so the excitation supply must be stable.

### 39.2.4 Bridges in everyday life

Under the pan of a kitchen scale there are usually one or four load cells, and inside them is again a full bridge of strain gauges. The scale's chip integrates the amplifier and the analogue-to-digital converter, so the circuit of the whole scale can be made very small. Pressing the "tare" key before weighing records the present output as the zero; what is subtracted is precisely the sum of the bridge imbalance, the common-mode error and the weight of the container.

**Engineer's notes**

- The excitation voltage of strain gauges cannot be raised at will: the higher the voltage, the larger the output, but also the more the gauges heat themselves, which causes drift. 5–10 V is usual for 350 Ω strain gauges.
- Fluctuations in the excitation voltage affect the output directly in proportion. High-precision measurement often uses ratiometric measurement: the reference voltage of the analogue-to-digital converter is taken from the same excitation supply, so that fluctuations of the excitation cancel in the ratio.
- The wiring from the bridge outputs to the amplifier should be shielded twisted pair. Interference then induces the same voltage on both wires, which is a common-mode voltage and can be rejected by the instrumentation amplifier.
- When choosing the gain resistor, calculate from full scale plus some margin; better to have full scale a little below the reference voltage than to let the output overflow.
- A single-supply analogue-to-digital converter can read only from 0 to the reference voltage; if the zero is offset slightly negative, the reading sticks at 0. Practical circuits often apply an offset voltage (for example half the reference voltage) to the reference pin (REF) of the instrumentation amplifier, so that the zero falls in the middle of the range; this also allows forces in both directions to be measured.
