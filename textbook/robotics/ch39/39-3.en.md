### 39.3.1 From continuous to discrete

The output of the amplifier is a voltage that varies continuously in time, but the program in the controller handles a sequence of numbers. Turning the first into the second is called **analogue-to-digital conversion**, and the device that does it is called an analogue-to-digital converter (ADC). Analogue-to-digital conversion consists of two actions:

- **Sampling**: take a value at fixed intervals $T_s$; $f_s = 1/T_s$ is called the sampling frequency;
- **Quantization**: assign the sampled voltage to one of a finite number of levels, represented by an integer (the code).

Each action brings its own kind of problem. Quantization decides how small a change can be resolved; sampling decides how fast a change can be followed. This section discusses them in turn.

### 39.3.2 Quantization and resolution

An N-bit analogue-to-digital converter divides the input range from 0 to the reference voltage $V_{ref}$ into $2^N$ levels. The voltage between two adjacent levels is called the least significant bit (LSB); it is the **resolution** of the converter:

$$
q = \frac{V_{ref}}{2^N} . \tag{39.3.1}
$$

Any input voltage falling within the range of one level gives the same code, so quantization inevitably brings an error, at most half an LSB (with rounding) or one LSB (with truncation). When the input varies in a sufficiently complicated way, the quantization error can be regarded as noise uniformly distributed on $[-q/2, q/2]$; by the result of Section 39.1, its root-mean-square value is

$$
e_q = \frac{q}{\sqrt{12}} . \tag{39.3.2}
$$

Measured with a full-scale sine signal, the ratio of the root-mean-square value of the signal to that of the quantization noise, expressed in decibels, is the signal-to-noise ratio of an ideal analogue-to-digital converter:

$$
\mathrm{SNR} = 6.02\,N + 1.76\ \text{dB} . \tag{39.3.3}
$$

Each extra bit raises the signal-to-noise ratio by about 6 dB, that is, halves the quantization noise. Because of circuit noise, non-linearity and so on, the effective number of bits of a real device is always one or two fewer than the nominal number.

### 39.3.3 The sampling theorem

After sampling, we know nothing of what the signal did between two samples. What, then, is the lowest sampling frequency that ensures no information is lost?

**Theorem 39.3.1 (Sampling theorem)** If a continuous signal contains no components of frequency higher than $f_{max}$, then provided the sampling frequency satisfies

$$
f_s > 2 f_{max} , \tag{39.3.4}
$$

the original signal can be recovered completely from its samples.

$f_s/2$ is called the Nyquist frequency. A rigorous proof of the theorem needs the Fourier transform; here we explain why it holds from the opposite side: what happens if the condition is not met.

### 39.3.4 Aliasing

Let the signal be a sine of frequency f, $\sin(2\pi f t)$, sampled at $f_s$; the n-th sample is $\sin(2\pi f n/f_s)$. Now replace f by $f - k f_s$ (k any integer):

$$
\sin\!\left(2\pi (f - k f_s)\frac{n}{f_s}\right) = \sin\!\left(2\pi f\frac{n}{f_s} - 2\pi k n\right) = \sin\!\left(2\pi f\frac{n}{f_s}\right) .
$$

Two sines of different frequencies have exactly the same samples! In the same way, $\sin(2\pi(k f_s - f)n/f_s) = -\sin(2\pi f n/f_s)$: the samples of a sine of frequency $k f_s - f$ differ only in sign (the phase is inverted). So after sampling, the frequency f cannot be told apart from any of the frequencies $|f - k f_s|$. The program can only interpret them as the one that falls between 0 and $f_s/2$:

$$
f_a = \left| f - k f_s \right| , \quad k \text{ the integer that makes } f_a \le f_s/2 . \tag{39.3.5}
$$

A high-frequency signal disguised as a low-frequency one: this phenomenon is called **aliasing**. The sampling theorem is there precisely to avoid aliasing: only when all components lie below $f_s/2$ is no frequency confused with another.

**Example 39.3.2** (1) The vibration of a robot joint motor is at 950 Hz and the sensor samples at 1 kHz. What will be seen in the readings? (2) What if a signal containing 50 Hz mains interference is sampled at 60 Hz?

(1) By Eq. (39.3.5), $f_a = |950 - 1000| = {{ex39_3.a1}}$ Hz. A 50 Hz ripple appears in the readings and looks just like mains interference; an engineer may well go and check the grounding, while the real cause is the motor vibration. (2) $f_a = |50 - 60| = {{ex39_3.a2}}$ Hz, which becomes a slow 10 Hz ripple, easily mistaken for a real change in force.

### 39.3.5 How to prevent aliasing

Once aliasing has happened, genuine and aliased components can no longer be distinguished in the samples, and no digital processing can help. So it must be dealt with before sampling: an analogue low-pass filter is placed in front of the analogue-to-digital converter to attenuate components above $f_s/2$ until they are negligible; this is called an anti-aliasing filter. In practical design the filter cannot cut off abruptly at $f_s/2$, so the sampling frequency is usually chosen as 5 to 10 times the highest signal frequency, leaving a transition band for the filter.

Modern Σ-Δ analogue-to-digital converters oversample internally at a very high frequency and then filter digitally and decimate, which greatly relaxes the requirements on the anti-aliasing filter. The 24-bit ADCs commonly used in force sensors and electronic scales are mostly of this kind.

The most familiar aliasing in everyday life is the wagon-wheel effect in films. A film camera takes 24 frames per second, that is, it samples at 24 Hz. When a wheel turns fast enough that the spokes move nearly one spoke spacing per frame, the wheel on the screen seems to turn slowly backwards.

**Engineer's notes**

- When choosing an analogue-to-digital converter, first work out the resolution needed: the smallest change of the measured quantity, converted to voltage via full scale, should be larger than several LSBs; also consider the noise of the sensor and amplifier themselves, since no number of bits can resolve changes below the noise.
- A higher sampling frequency is not always better: the amount of data, the computation and the power consumption all rise with it. Choose 5–10 times the signal bandwidth, together with anti-aliasing filtering.
- When an unexplained low-frequency ripple appears in the readings, first ask whether some high-frequency source (a motor, a switching power supply, PWM) has been aliased down. Change the sampling frequency: if the frequency of the ripple moves with it, it is aliasing.
