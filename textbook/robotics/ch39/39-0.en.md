title: Chapter Overview
---

A sensor delivers an electrical signal, but a robot needs a number it can trust. Between a tiny change in the resistance of a strain gauge and a reading of "20.1 N" in the controller lie a bridge, an amplifier, an analogue-to-digital converter and a digital filter, and each step adds error. How far a reading can be trusted is answered by the measurement uncertainty; whether a sensor is good or not is decided by calibration and automatic inspection; whether a production line is stable is watched with statistical process control. This chapter walks along this measurement chain from one end to the other.

The chapter follows the force sensor in a robot's wrist throughout, and each section takes up one link of the measurement chain:

- Section 39.1 explains how to judge a reading: the kinds of error, Type A and Type B uncertainty, and how to combine them and state a measurement result;
- Section 39.2 starts from the strain gauge and explains why the Wheatstone bridge is used, what the quarter, half and full bridge each offer, and how an instrumentation amplifier brings a signal of a few millivolts up to the range an analogue-to-digital converter needs;
- Section 39.3 deals with analogue-to-digital conversion: the resolution and noise that quantization brings, the sampling theorem and aliasing;
- Section 39.4 introduces the two most common digital filters, the moving average and the first-order low-pass, and the trade-off "the cleaner the filtering, the slower the response";
- Section 39.5 calibrates the whole measurement chain, finds the zero offset and the sensitivity by least squares, and gives linearity, hysteresis and repeatability;
- Section 39.6 deals with automatic inspection: how a test station judges against the specification automatically, and why a guard band is needed; then, with inspection data from the grinding operation of the digital factory, it shows how a control chart raises the alarm before scrap is made.

The chapter uses the basics of probability and statistics from Chapter 9 (mean, standard deviation, normal distribution) and the strain gauge introduced in Chapter 38. The circuits need only Ohm's law and the voltage-divider formula from school physics; what is needed about operational amplifiers is explained in Section 39.2. The chapter has two circuit labs, in which the measurement chain is built in CircuitJS, a circuit simulator in the browser, so that the circuit can be changed directly and the voltage at each point observed.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. tell systematic error from random error, evaluate standard uncertainty by Type A and Type B evaluation, combine them, and write a measurement result with an expanded uncertainty;
2. compute the output of a quarter, half and full bridge and explain the advantages of the full bridge; choose the gain of an instrumentation amplifier from the measuring range and the input range of the analogue-to-digital converter;
3. compute the resolution and ideal signal-to-noise ratio of an analogue-to-digital converter, use the sampling theorem to judge whether aliasing will occur, and compute the apparent frequency after aliasing;
4. design moving-average and first-order low-pass filters and explain the trade-off between cut-off frequency, residual interference and delay;
5. calibrate a measurement chain and find the zero offset, sensitivity, linearity, hysteresis and repeatability;
6. set automatic decision rules from the specification and the measurement uncertainty, draw an X̄–R control chart, use the rules for special causes to find abnormalities in a process, and propose improvements.
