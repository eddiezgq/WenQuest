### 39.5.1 The problem: why is the reading off?

In Section 39.1 the mean reading measured with a 20 N weight was about 0.12 N higher than the weight, far beyond the range of random scatter. The cause is not hard to guess: the rated output in the sensor data sheet is only a nominal value; the gauge factor of each strain gauge and the value of each gain resistor have tolerances, and the bridge can never be perfectly balanced. Together these deviations make the zero and the sensitivity of the measurement chain differ from their nominal values.

The remedy is **calibration**: apply standards of known magnitude (standard weights, a force standard machine) to the sensor, record the output, and find the actual input–output relation of this particular measurement chain. Later readings are converted using this relation, and the systematic error is corrected.

The standard itself also has an uncertainty. It is usually required that the uncertainty of the standard be no more than 1/3 of the maximum permissible error of the sensor being calibrated, preferably 1/10; otherwise the error introduced by the calibration takes up most of the error budget.

### 39.5.2 The calibration procedure

A common static calibration procedure is:

1. Preloading: load to full scale and unload, two or three times, to let the spring element and the bonding layer "settle";
2. Upscale run: load step by step from zero to full scale, for example in steps of 20%, reading after each step has settled;
3. Downscale run: unload step by step back to zero, reading at the same steps;
4. Repetition: repeat the upscale and downscale runs 3 times.

In this way each calibration point $F_i$ has 3 readings on the upscale run and 3 on the downscale run. The two runs are recorded separately so that hysteresis can be seen; the runs are repeated 3 times so that repeatability can be assessed.

### 39.5.3 Least-squares fitting

The output of an ideal measurement chain is proportional to the force, plus a zero offset:

$$
y = a + b\,F , \tag{39.5.1}
$$

where a is the zero offset and b the sensitivity. The calibration data will not lie exactly on a straight line, so we must find the line that fits them "most closely". The method of **least squares** (Section 9.4) takes the a and b that minimize the sum of the squares of the deviations of the points:

$$
b = \frac{\sum (F_j - \bar F)(y_j - \bar y)}{\sum (F_j - \bar F)^2} , \qquad a = \bar y - b\,\bar F , \tag{39.5.2}
$$

where the sums run over all readings (both runs, all repetitions), and $\bar F$, $\bar y$ are the means. With a and b known, the force is obtained from the output during measurement:

$$
F = \frac{y - a}{b} . \tag{39.5.3}
$$

This is the result of calibration. Once a and b are written into the controller, the zero and sensitivity errors in the reading are corrected.

### 39.5.4 Static characteristics

The fitted line is only an approximation. The difference between the actual characteristic and the line is described by a few indices, each expressed as a percentage of the full-scale output $y_{FS} = b\,F_{FS}$ (%FS). Different standards differ slightly in the details of the definitions; this book uses the following common ones.

**Linearity**: the largest deviation of the mean reading at a calibration point from the fitted line,

$$
\delta_L = \frac{\max_i |\bar y_i - (a + bF_i)|}{y_{FS}} \times 100\% . \tag{39.5.4}
$$

**Hysteresis**: the largest difference, at the same force, between the mean readings on unloading and on loading,

$$
\delta_H = \frac{\max_i |\bar y_i^{\,\downarrow} - \bar y_i^{\,\uparrow}|}{y_{FS}} \times 100\% . \tag{39.5.5}
$$

Hysteresis comes from internal friction in the material of the spring element and in the bonding layer: for the same force, the deformation is slightly different when the load is increasing and when it is decreasing.

**Repeatability**: the scatter of repeated measurements in the same direction at the same force. Take the largest standard deviation of the readings over the calibration points, $s_{\max}$:

$$
\delta_R = \frac{2\,s_{\max}}{y_{FS}} \times 100\% . \tag{39.5.6}
$$

Linearity and hysteresis are systematic and can be corrected further by a more elaborate fit (for example a quadratic polynomial, or separate fits for the upscale and downscale runs); repeatability is random and cannot be corrected, only reduced by averaging repeated measurements (Section 39.1).

### 39.5.5 The effect of temperature

Calibration is done at one particular temperature. When the temperature changes, the resistance of the strain gauges, the elastic modulus of the spring element and the offset of the amplifier all change, so the zero and the sensitivity drift. Two indices are commonly used to describe this: the zero temperature drift (how many %FS the zero changes per 1 K) and the sensitivity temperature drift.

The full bridge has a temperature-compensating effect of its own: when all four strain gauges warm up together, their resistances change by the same amount and the bridge stays balanced (Section 39.2). But the temperature coefficients of the four gauges can never be exactly equal, nor are the temperatures at the gauge positions exactly the same, so some zero temperature drift remains. High-precision sensors have compensating resistors inserted in the bridge, are adjusted one by one in a temperature chamber, and their data sheets state the temperature drift.

### 39.5.6 Automatic calibration and final inspection

In the factory, sensor calibration and inspection are done automatically by a test station: the program drives the loading mechanism step by step, samples once the reading has settled, fits the data and computes the indices automatically when calibration ends, then judges each item against the specification and prints a report. In the lab, the full-scale output from the fit is about 0.25% smaller than the 2.48 V calculated from Eq. (39.2.5). The reason is that the open-loop gain of the operational amplifiers in the simulator is finite ($10^5$), so the actual gain is slightly below the formula value. Differences of this kind between nominal and actual values are exactly what calibration is meant to find.

Calibration is everywhere in daily life. Supermarket electronic scales, fuel dispensers at petrol stations and taxi meters must all be verified regularly by the metrology authority: they are compared against standard weights and standard measures, and if the error exceeds the limit they must be adjusted or taken out of service. Instruments that pass carry a mark showing the period of validity. These instruments touch everyone's wallet, so national law requires that they be calibrated regularly.

**Engineer's notes**

- Calibrate under the sensor's actual mounting and operating conditions: the same mounting, the same excitation voltage, the same amplifier and analogue-to-digital converter. If any one of these is changed, the calibration result may no longer apply.
- The calibration points must cover the whole range and include zero. If only a few points are calibrated, non-linearity in the middle of the range goes unnoticed.
- Record every calibration: date, temperature, the standard used and its certificate number, the readings at each point, and the fit results. The zero and sensitivity of a sensor change slowly with time; comparing successive calibration records reveals problems early.
- The six-axis force/torque sensor at a robot's wrist has six outputs and six components, and what is calibrated is a 6×6 matrix. The principle is the same as in this section; only the loading must cover forces and moments in every direction.
