### 39.4.1 The problem: a jumping reading

While the gripper holds a workpiece, the clamping force read by the controller is not a smooth curve: it keeps jumping up and down around the true value. Circuit noise, switching interference from the motor driver and 50 Hz mains hum are all superimposed on the signal. The anti-aliasing filter (Section 39.3) can only remove components above the Nyquist frequency; it can do nothing about interference below it. The rest of the work is left to the program, and this is digital filtering.

This section introduces the two simplest and most widely used digital filters. Each has its own strengths, and they share a common law: **The cleaner the filtering, the slower the response.**

### 39.4.2 The moving average

The most obvious approach is to take the average of the most recent N samples:

$$
y_k = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} x_{k-i} . \tag{39.4.1}
$$

Random noise is positive in some of the N values and negative in others; after averaging, its standard deviation shrinks to $1/\sqrt N$ of its original value (Section 39.1). For periodic interference the moving average has a special property: if the N samples cover exactly a whole number of periods of the interference, its positive and negative parts cancel completely in the average.

Taking the input to be a sine wave of frequency f, we can find the factor by which the moving average amplifies it, that is, its magnitude response:

$$
|H(f)| = \left| \frac{\sin(N\pi f/f_s)}{N\sin(\pi f/f_s)} \right| . \tag{39.4.2}
$$

When $f = f_s/N$ or an integer multiple of it, the numerator is zero and $|H| = 0$: these frequencies are removed completely.

The price is delay. The output of the moving average corresponds to the "middle instant" of the N samples, $(N-1)/2$ sampling periods later than the newest sample. When the signal changes suddenly, the output needs N sampling periods to catch up completely.

### 39.4.3 The first-order low-pass filter

Another common filter makes only one correction step each time:

$$
y_k = y_{k-1} + \alpha\,(x_k - y_{k-1}) , \qquad 0 < \alpha \le 1 . \tag{39.4.3}
$$

The new output is the old output moved a small step towards the new reading; the step is α times the difference between the two. It only needs to remember the previous output and costs very little computation, so it is the filter most often used in microcontrollers.

Eq. (39.4.3) is the digital version of the resistor–capacitor low-pass filter. Its cut-off frequency $f_c$ (the frequency at which the magnitude response falls to $1/\sqrt2$) is related to α approximately by

$$
\alpha = 1 - e^{-2\pi f_c / f_s} \approx \frac{2\pi f_c}{f_s} \quad (f_c \ll f_s) . \tag{39.4.4}
$$

The exponential relation makes the step response of the digital filter agree exactly, at every sampling instant, with that of a resistor–capacitor circuit with the same time constant; for the cut-off frequency it is a good approximation when $f_c \ll f_s$. The corresponding time constant is $\tau = 1/(2\pi f_c)$: after a sudden step in the input, the output covers 63% of the gap in a time τ.

The first-order low-pass filter attenuates components above $f_c$ gradually: for every tenfold rise in frequency the amplitude falls to about one tenth, but it never becomes exactly zero at any frequency.

**Example 39.4.1** The clamping force rises smoothly from 0 to 30 N within 0.2 s. Superimposed on the signal are 50 Hz mains interference with an amplitude of 2 N and random noise with a standard deviation of 0.3 N; the sampling frequency is 1 kHz. Filter the signal with a moving average of N = {{ex39_4.N}} and with a first-order low-pass filter of cut-off frequency 10 Hz, and compare the results.

The N samples of the moving average cover {{ex39_4.N}} ms, exactly one period of 50 Hz, so by Eq. (39.4.2) its response at {{ex39_4.zero_f:.0f}} Hz is zero. It hardly attenuates the 5 Hz component of the signal ($|H| = {{ex39_4.H5_ma:.3f}}$), and its delay is {{ex39_4.delay_ma_ms:.1f}} ms.

For the first-order low-pass filter, Eq. (39.4.4) gives $\alpha = {{ex39_4.alpha:.4f}}$ and a time constant of {{ex39_4.tau_ms:.1f}} ms. At 50 Hz it can only reduce the interference to {{ex39_4.H50_lp:.2f}} of its amplitude.

Once the force has settled (after 0.35 s), the standard deviations of the differences between the three curves and the true value are:

| | Raw reading | Moving average | First-order low-pass |
|---|---|---|---|
| Residual ripple / N | {{ex39_4.res_raw:.2f}} | {{ex39_4.res_ma:.2f}} | {{ex39_4.res_lp:.2f}} |
| Lag during the rise / ms | — | {{ex39_4.lag_ma_ms:.0f}} | {{ex39_4.lag_lp_ms:.0f}} |

For this problem the moving average is better: it is "tuned" specifically to 50 Hz, so its residual ripple is smaller and its delay is shorter as well. To suppress 50 Hz to the same degree, the first-order low-pass filter would need a lower cut-off frequency and hence a longer delay. But if the frequency of the interference is not fixed, the zeros of the moving average lose their meaning, and the smooth attenuation of the first-order low-pass filter becomes the more reliable choice.

### 39.4.4 Filtering in everyday life

When something is put on an electronic scale, the digits jump a few times before they settle. The scale is filtering: it would rather be a little slow than give an unsteady reading. A step counter in a phone, on the other hand, has to count each step from the swing of the arm, so it cannot filter too hard, or the steps would be "averaged" away. In the end, designing a filter means finding the balance between "steady" and "fast" that the application needs.

---

**Engineer's notes**

- Find the frequency of the interference first, then choose the filter. Interference at a fixed frequency (mains, motor speed) is best removed by "tuning" to it with a moving average or a notch filter.
- Filter delay in a force-control loop directly reduces the stability margin (Chapters 42 and 45), so do not add filtering freely just to make the reading look nice. The reading for display and the reading for control can use different filters.
- A first-order low-pass filter takes only one line of code, but α depends on the sampling frequency: if you change the sampling frequency, you must recompute α.
