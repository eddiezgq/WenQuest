This chapter followed one measurement chain from the strain gauge to a reading in the controller, and on to the conformity decision in the factory. Every link answered the same question: how far can this number be trusted?

**A reading must carry its uncertainty.** Errors are divided into systematic and random errors. The true value is unknown, so the error cannot be known exactly; instead, the uncertainty expresses how far a measurement result can be trusted. Type A is evaluated by statistical methods, Type B from other information; the components are combined as a root sum of squares and multiplied by a coverage factor k = 2 to give the expanded uncertainty. In the combination, the largest one or two terms decide the result.

**The bridge turns a tiny change of resistance into a voltage.** The resistance of a strain gauge changes by only a few parts in ten thousand; the Wheatstone bridge turns this into a voltage difference of the order of millivolts. The output of a full bridge is 4 times that of a quarter bridge, strictly linear, and temperature-compensated as well. The instrumentation amplifier amplifies only the difference between its two inputs and rejects the common-mode voltage; its gain is set by a single resistor $R_g$. The gain should be chosen from the full scale and the input range of the analogue-to-digital converter.

**Sampling must satisfy the sampling theorem.** The resolution of an analogue-to-digital converter is set by its number of bits and its reference voltage; the ideal signal-to-noise ratio is about 6.02N + 1.76 dB. The sampling frequency must be more than twice the highest frequency in the signal, or high-frequency components alias into low frequencies. Once aliasing has happened it cannot be removed digitally; it can only be prevented before sampling with an anti-aliasing filter.

**The cleaner the filtering, the slower the response.** The moving average removes integer multiples of $f_s/N$ completely and suits interference at a fixed frequency; the first-order low-pass filter takes only one line of code and attenuates smoothly. Both pay with delay, so the readings used for control must not be filtered freely.

**Calibration corrects systematic error.** Standards are applied step by step, loading, unloading and repeating; a least-squares fit gives the zero and the sensitivity, and readings are converted back through the fitted line. Linearity, hysteresis and repeatability describe the deviations beyond the fitted line; the first two are systematic, repeatability is random.

**Leave guard bands in decisions, and watch the process with control charts.** When the measured value lies near a tolerance limit, the true value may already be out of tolerance, so the acceptance zone should be narrowed by U at each end. A control chart takes its control limits from the process's own data and uses the rules for detecting special causes to find trends and shifts, raising an alarm before scrap appears; the difference between $C_p$ and $P_{pk}$ tells us whether the problem is spread or drift.

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| Systematic and random error | The former stays constant or varies in a regular way in repeated measurements and can be corrected; the latter has no pattern and can only be reduced by repeated measurements |
| Measurement uncertainty | A parameter expressing the range over which the values of the measurand may be dispersed, expressed as a standard deviation |
| Common-mode rejection | The instrumentation amplifier amplifies only the difference between its two inputs and hardly responds to a voltage common to both |
| Nyquist frequency | Half the sampling frequency; components above it alias after sampling |
| Aliasing | A high-frequency signal appears after sampling as a low-frequency one and cannot be told apart from it |
| Calibration | Determining the actual input–output relation of a measurement chain with known standards |
| Hysteresis | The same input gives different outputs on loading and on unloading |
| Guard band | The strip cut off at each end of the tolerance so that parts judged conforming are also truly conforming |
| Control limits | The range of variation of the subgroup mean in a stable process, calculated from the process's own data; not the same as tolerance limits |

### Historical notes

| Year | People | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| 1833, 1843 | Christie, Wheatstone | Christie proposed the bridge circuit; Wheatstone popularized it for precise resistance measurement, and it came to be called the Wheatstone bridge |
| 1856 | Kelvin | Found that the resistance of a metal wire increases when it is stretched, the physical basis of the strain gauge |
| 1924 | Shewhart | Proposed the control chart at Western Electric (whose research department became Bell Laboratories the following year); published *Economic Control of Quality of Manufactured Product* in 1931 |
| 1928 | Nyquist | Showed that a signal of bandwidth B needs 2B independent values per second to be transmitted |
| 1938 | Simmons, Ruge | Independently invented the bonded wire strain gauge |
| 1949 | Shannon | Gave a rigorous proof of the sampling theorem (Kotelnikov had the same result in 1933) |
| 1993 | ISO and others | Published the *Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement* (GUM), unifying the methods of evaluating uncertainty |
