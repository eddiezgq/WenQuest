### 1.5.1 The problem

Where robots are used depends on a very plain calculation: is a robot better at this job than a person or a special-purpose machine? "Better" may mean safer, for example taking red-hot castings out of a die-casting machine; steadier, for example loading and unloading a lathe at the same cycle time for eight hours a day; more precise, for example suturing a blood vessel in a space a few millimetres across inside the body; or simply less trouble, for example sweeping the floor once a day.

This section introduces robot applications in four fields: manufacturing, logistics, medicine and the home. For manufacturing we take the workshop of the WenQuest digital factory as our example and use the factory's real data to work out a few figures: how many times a shaft is moved in the workshop and how far it travels; whether two AGVs are enough; whether a UR5e can load and unload a lathe. These computations need only high-school mathematics, but they stand for the questions an engineer must answer before bringing robots in.

### 1.5.2 Manufacturing: the workshop of the WenQuest digital factory

Manufacturing is the traditional field of industrial robots, and the automotive and electronics industries have always been their largest users. Typical tasks are welding, painting, handling and palletizing, machine-tool loading and unloading, assembly, inspection, and grinding and polishing. These tasks have one thing in common: they are highly repetitive, and the surroundings and the workpieces can be arranged quite regularly, which suits exactly the teach-and-playback described in Section 1.2.

The WenQuest digital factory makes only one product: the two-stage cylindrical-gear reducer WQR-105, with a transmission ratio of 10.5 (Example 1.4.2 in Section 1.4). A reducer consists of 13 kinds of parts and subassemblies made in-house: three shafts, three gears, the housing base and the housing cover, together with the housing assembly, the three shaft assemblies and the final assembly built from them. The workshop has 11 kinds of work centres, among them band saws, CNC lathes, machining centres, a gear hobbing machine, a heat-treatment furnace, a cylindrical grinder, an inspection station and assembly stations; the raw-material store and the finished-goods store are at the two ends of the workshop, and two AGVs carry parts between the machines along two transverse main aisles and one longitudinal aisle (Figure 1.5.1). The layout and data of the factory are the same as those of the simulated workshop of the WenQuest digital factory.

**Example 1.5.1** The process route of output shaft SH-301 is: cutting → rough turning → quenching and tempering → finish turning → keyway milling → cylindrical grinding → part inspection, {{ex1_5.n_ops}} operations in all, with a total machining and inspection time of {{ex1_5.mach_min}} min per piece. How many times is it moved in the workshop? How far does it travel? How much time does the handling take?

Each operation is done on a different machine; when one operation is finished, an AGV takes the part to the next, and after the last operation, inspection, the part goes to the finished-goods store. So there are {{ex1_5.n_mv}} moves in all. On every move the AGV starts from the docking point beside the machine, first drives into the nearest main aisle, follows the aisle to the position of the target machine, if necessary changes to the other main aisle by way of the longitudinal aisle on the left, and then drives into the docking point of the target. For example, the first move, from the band saw to CNC lathe A, has its corners at {{ex1_5.first_path}} (in m) and is {{ex1_5.first_L:.2f}} m long.

The AGV drives at v = 1.0 m/s, and loading and unloading each take $t_{\text{load}} = 30$ s. The time of one move is

$$
t = \frac{L}{v} + 2\,t_{\text{load}} , \tag{1.5.1}
$$

where L is the length of the route (m) and t is in s. Program 1.5.1 works out the seven moves with the same layout and routing rules as the factory simulation; they are listed in Table 1.5.1.

**Example 1.5.2** Are two AGVs enough? To answer this we first need to know how many reducers the workshop can make a day. Adding up the operation times of all the parts and subassemblies of one reducer by work centre, and converting them with an 8-hour single shift and the number of machines, gives the daily capacity of each work centre. The gear hobbing machine is occupied for {{ex1_5.need_hob}} min per reducer (the three gears and the pinion on input gear shaft SH-101, four sets of teeth to be hobbed), so in one shift it can do the work for only {{ex1_5.cap_hob:.1f}} reducers, the lowest in the whole workshop; it is the bottleneck. Next come the two CNC lathes, occupied for {{ex1_5.need_cnc}} min together, {{ex1_5.cap_cnc:.1f}} reducers per shift. The output of the workshop is set by its bottleneck (Chapter 60).

Now the AGVs. The 13 kinds of parts and subassemblies of one reducer each follow their own process route and need {{ex1_5.n_moves}} moves in all, covering {{ex1_5.agv_dist:.0f}} m; by Eq. (1.5.1) they occupy the AGVs for {{ex1_5.agv_min:.0f}} min. At the output of the bottleneck, {{ex1_5.cap_hob:.1f}} reducers per shift, the two AGVs are busy about {{ex1_5.agv_util_pct:.0f}}% of the 2 × 480 min of a shift. This figure leaves out the empty runs of the AGVs to the pick-up points, so the real utilization is somewhat higher, but there is still plenty of margin. So at the present output two AGVs are enough; to raise the output, what must be dealt with first is the gear hobbing machine, not the AGVs.

**Example 1.5.3** Can a UR5e load and unload a lathe? The **rated payload** of the UR5e is 5 kg, that is, the largest mass the end of the arm can carry, including the gripper itself. Let the gripper have a mass of {{ex1_5.grip:.1f}} kg.

Output shaft SH-301 is cut from Ø50 mm round bar; according to the bill of materials each shaft uses {{ex1_5.m301:.1f}} kg, which with the gripper makes {{ex1_5.tot301:.1f}} kg, within 5 kg. The gear blanks are forged discs, and their mass is volume times density. A disc is a cylinder of diameter d and thickness h:

$$
m = \rho\,\frac{\pi d^2}{4}\,h , \tag{1.5.2}
$$

with the density of steel ρ taken as {{ex1_5.rho:.0f}} kg/m³. The blank of the large gear GR-202 is Ø{{ex1_5.d202:.0f}} mm × {{ex1_5.h202:.0f}} mm; Eq. (1.5.2) gives m = {{ex1_5.m202:.2f}} kg, which with the gripper makes {{ex1_5.tot202:.2f}} kg, more than 5 kg. The blank of GR-302 is Ø{{ex1_5.d302:.0f}} mm × {{ex1_5.h302:.0f}} mm, m = {{ex1_5.m302:.1f}} kg, far beyond the limit.

So the UR5e can load and unload the shafts but cannot lift the gear blanks. The gear station needs an arm with a payload of about 20 kg, or a gantry robot. Note also that the rated payload is given for a load whose centre of gravity is close to the flange; the farther away the centre of gravity, the smaller the allowed load, and the exact figures must be read from the manufacturer's payload curve.

This example shows the basic approach to choosing a robot: first work out from the process data what has to be carried, how heavy it is, how far it goes and what the cycle time is, and then compare this with the robot's datasheet (Chapter 30).

### 1.5.3 Logistics

Warehouses and distribution centres have been the fastest-growing field for robots in the past decade. In a traditional warehouse, pickers push trolleys back and forth between the shelves, walking a dozen kilometres or more a day and spending most of their time on the way. After Amazon bought Kiva in 2012, the "goods-to-person" approach spread quickly: hundreds or thousands of flat little vehicles slip under the shelving units, lift them and carry them to fixed picking stations, and the pickers only have to take the goods from the shelves brought to them. A dispatching system directs all the vehicles at once to avoid congestion and collisions. In July 2025 Amazon announced that the robots in its operations network had passed one million.

Besides carrying shelves, logistics robots include sorting robots, palletizing robots, picking arms, autonomous picking carts, and outdoor delivery vehicles and drones. According to the IFR, nearly 200,000 professional service robots were sold worldwide in 2024, of which 102,900 were transport and logistics robots, about {{ex1_5.tl_share_pct:.0f}}%, the largest category by number (Figure 1.5.2).

The technical problems of logistics robots are of the same kind as those of the AGVs in the digital factory: finding their own position on a map (Chapter 47), planning routes and avoiding obstacles (Chapter 53), and coordinating and dispatching many robots (Chapter 60). Picking arms must also recognize the target among goods heaped in disorder and pick it up, which makes great demands on vision and grasping (Chapters 54 and 57); this is one of the most promising places for embodied intelligence to come into practical use first.

### 1.5.4 Medicine

Medicine is one of the most demanding and fastest-growing fields of robot application. Surgical robots are the most mature kind. Take the da Vinci surgical system: according to figures published by Intuitive in January 2026, about {{ex1_5.dv_2025_mio:.2f}} million procedures were performed worldwide with da Vinci systems in 2025, and about {{ex1_5.dv_2024_mio:.2f}} million in 2024, a growth of {{ex1_5.dv_growth_pct:.1f}}% in one year (the company says "about 18%"), or on average about {{ex1_5.dv_per_day:.0f}} procedures a day.

The value of a surgical robot lies not in replacing the surgeon but in extending the surgeon's abilities: once inside the body the instruments have wrist-like joints and are more dexterous than straight laparoscopic instruments; the surgeon's hand movements can be scaled down and hand tremor filtered out; a three-dimensional endoscope shows the operating field enlarged. On the other hand, surgical robots face the difficulties common to all medical robots: they must be registered as medical devices, and development and clinical trials take a long time; any malfunction bears directly on the patient's safety; and the equipment and consumables are expensive.

Besides surgery, medical robots include rehabilitation robots and exoskeletons (helping stroke patients learn to walk again), positioning robots for orthopaedics and neurosurgery, patient positioning systems in radiotherapy, and mobile robots that deliver medicines and specimens in hospitals. The IFR counted 16,700 medical robots sold in 2024.

### 1.5.5 The home

Robot vacuums are the robots found in the most homes. The IFR estimates that close to 20 million consumer service robots (for personal and domestic use) were sold in 2024, most of them sweeping, mopping and lawn-mowing robots doing household chores. A robot vacuum looks simple, yet it contains almost all the basic problems of mobile robots: it must know where it is, plan how to cover the whole room, avoid tables, chairs and steps, and go back by itself to recharge when its battery runs low.

Early robot vacuums had no map: on hitting a wall they turned through a random angle and drove straight again. Today's robot vacuums mostly use a lidar or a camera to build a map of the room and sweep it row by row along a planned path (Figure 1.5.3). How much do the two ways differ in efficiency? The following example gives a quantitative answer.

**Example 1.5.4** The area of the room to be cleaned is A = {{ex1_5.A:.0f}} m², the cleaning width of the robot vacuum is w = {{ex1_5.w:.2f}} m and its driving speed is v = {{ex1_5.v:.2f}} m/s. When cleaning row by row, neighbouring rows overlap by {{ex1_5.ov:.2f}} (that is, 15%) of the width so that nothing is missed.

Row by row, the new area the robot sweeps each second is $v\,w\,(1 - o)$, where o is the overlap fraction, so the time needed to clean the whole room is

$$
t_{\text{row}} = \frac{A}{v\,w\,(1 - o)} , \tag{1.5.3}
$$

which gives {{ex1_5.t_row_min:.1f}} min (not counting the turns at the ends of the rows).

In random cleaning, the robot's paths are scattered over the room independently of each other; the same spot may be swept several times or not at all. Let the total area swept by the robot (counting overlaps as many times as they occur) be n times the area of the room; n is called the number of "passes":

$$
n = \frac{v\,w\,t}{A} . \tag{1.5.4}
$$

What fraction of the floor is swept? Divide the cleaning into N very short stretches of path, each sweeping a small fraction δ of the room, so that $n = N\delta$. For a given small patch of floor, the probability that one stretch misses it is $1 - \delta$; the stretches are independent, so the probability that all N stretches miss it is $(1 - \delta)^N$. When δ is small and N is large,

$$
(1 - \delta)^N = (1 - \delta)^{n/\delta} \to e^{-n} ,
$$

where we have used the limit $\lim_{\delta\to 0}(1-\delta)^{1/\delta} = e^{-1}$ (e is the base of natural logarithms). The fraction swept, the **coverage**, is therefore

$$
c = 1 - e^{-n} . \tag{1.5.5}
$$

One pass (n = 1) covers only about 63% of the floor. To cover 95% we need $e^{-n} = 0.05$, that is, $n = \ln 20 \approx {{ex1_5.n95:.2f}}$ passes; by Eq. (1.5.4) this takes {{ex1_5.t_rand_min:.0f}} min, {{ex1_5.rand_ratio:.1f}} times as long as cleaning row by row, and 5% is still left unswept.

The derivation of Eq. (1.5.5) assumes that the stretches of path are independent. Program 1.5.1 tests this assumption with a random experiment: on a gridded floor of 60 m² it scatters {{ex1_5.strokes}} cleaning strips 0.5 m long at random, for a total of n = {{ex1_5.n_sim:.2f}} passes; the measured coverage is {{ex1_5.c_sim:.3f}}, while Eq. (1.5.5) gives {{ex1_5.c_formula:.3f}}, a difference of less than 2%. This example shows the value of maps and path planning: with the same machine and the same battery, the ability to "know where it is and where it has already swept" more than doubles the efficiency.

### 1.5.6 Other fields

Robot applications go far beyond these four fields. In agriculture there are milking robots, harvesting robots and self-driving farm machinery; in construction there are bricklaying, spraying and surveying robots; the energy industry uses robots to inspect substations, pipelines and power lines; the generations of Mars rovers since Sojourner are mobile robots working on another celestial body; underwater robots are used in ocean engineering, scientific surveys and salvage (Section 1.3). The surroundings in most of these fields are far less orderly than in a factory, so they demand more autonomy from robots, and they are where embodied-intelligence technology can show what it can do.

### 1.5.7 An everyday example

The time a food-delivery rider takes for one order can be split into two parts, just as in Eq. (1.5.1): the time on the road, and the time spent picking up the meal at the restaurant, waiting for the lift downstairs and handing it over. When the distance is short, the second part is often longer than the time on the road. This is exactly the situation of the AGV in Example 1.5.1: of ten minutes of handling, seven go into loading and unloading.

So there are two ways to make delivery more efficient: one is to travel faster and shorter, which depends on route planning; the other is to hand over faster, which depends on the design of the pick-up and drop-off steps, such as delivery lockers or automatic hand-over between AGV and robot arm. In engineering, to judge an automation scheme one must break the time of the whole process down, find the steps that really take the time, and not just stare at the most conspicuous figure, the "speed".

**Engineer's notes**

- When assessing a robot project, work out the cycle times first: the working time, handling time and waiting time per piece at each station, and find the bottleneck. Speeding up steps other than the bottleneck does nothing for output.
- The rated payload includes the end-of-arm tool. When choosing a robot, count in the masses of the gripper, the quick-change plate, sensors and cables, read the manufacturer's payload curve for the position of the load's centre of gravity, and keep a margin of about 20%.
- Estimate the load on the AGVs (the vehicle time needed per hour) as "average time per move × moves per hour", then add the time for empty runs and charging. Above about 70% utilization, queueing grows quickly, and more vehicles or a better hand-over are needed.
- When quoting market figures for medical and service robots, state whether they are sales or operational stock, worldwide or for one region, and whether the count includes medical robots.
