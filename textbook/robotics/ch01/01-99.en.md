The main thread of this chapter can be summed up in one sentence: **a robot is a programmable actuated mechanism that moves or works autonomously on the basis of its own perception**. Everything else revolves around this sentence.

Section 1.1 started from the definition in the international standard ISO 8373 and saw that the fundamental difference between a robot and ordinary automated equipment lies not in how complex the mechanism is but in autonomy: whether its decisions depend on its own measurements. The sense–decide–act loop expresses this clearly: an open-loop machine moves by a timetable, a closed-loop machine moves by watching its surroundings. The stopping-distance example showed that the ability to sense must match the ability to move: the faster a vehicle goes, the farther it must see.

The historical review of Section 1.2 showed that the ideas embodied in today's robots each have their own origins: from Hero's ropes and Jacquard's cards to rewritable programs; from the float-regulated water clock and Watt's governor to feedback control; from Devol's patent and the first Unimate to precise and reliable industrial robots; from Shakey and the subsumption architecture to today's embodied intelligence. Embodied intelligence has not changed the structure of the loop; what it has changed is where the decision rules come from: no longer written by people, but learned from data. The IFR figures show that the annual installations of industrial robots worldwide doubled in ten years, equivalent to growth of about 7% a year.

Section 1.3 divided robots by application into three classes, industrial, service and medical, and by structure and mode of locomotion into fixed-base serial and parallel manipulators and mobile robots: wheeled, legged, humanoid, aerial, underwater and others. Different structures have workspaces of different shapes, and the choice of robot depends on the task.

Section 1.4 took a robot apart into seven parts: mechanism, actuation, electrics, sensing, control, software and intelligence, with the flows of energy and information between them forming a complete loop. Degrees of freedom are a basic concept running through the whole book: a rigid body has 3 in the plane and 6 in space, and a fixed-base open-chain mechanism has as many degrees of freedom as the sum of those of its joints. A robot with fewer actuators than degrees of freedom is underactuated, and wheeled carts, quadruped robots, humanoid robots and quadrotors all are.

Section 1.5 used the data of the digital factory to work out a few figures: an output shaft is moved seven times in the workshop, and seven of the ten minutes of handling go into loading and unloading; the gear hobbing machine is the bottleneck, and two AGVs are enough; the UR5e can carry the shafts but not the gear blanks. The robot vacuum at home showed the value of maps and planning: random cleaning needs two and a half times as long as cleaning row by row to cover 95% of the floor.

Section 1.6 gave the road map of the book and introduced the five robots that run through it. Their parameters all come from the WenQuest parts library and stay the same in all later chapters.

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| Robot | A programmed actuated mechanism with a degree of autonomy to perform locomotion, manipulation or positioning (ISO 8373:2021) |
| Autonomy | The ability to perform intended tasks based on current state and sensing, without human intervention |
| Open loop and closed loop | Whether the commands depend only on time, or on measurements |
| Industrial, service and medical robots | Three classes by application; the applicable standards and statistical definitions differ |
| Serial and parallel | Whether there is only one path from the base to the end of the arm, or several branches connected at once |
| Collaborative robot | Designed to work directly with people in a shared space; whether it is safe depends on the whole application |
| Degrees of freedom | The smallest number of independent variables needed to fix the position |
| Underactuated | Fewer actuators than degrees of freedom |
| Teach-and-playback | A person leads the robot through the task once; the robot records it and repeats it automatically |
| Rated payload | The largest mass the end of the arm can carry, gripper included; it depends on the position of the centre of gravity |
| Pose repeatability | The spread of the end-effector position when the same command is executed repeatedly; not the same as absolute (pose) accuracy |
