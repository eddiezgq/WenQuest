### 1.6.1 The problem

The first five sections have introduced robots from several angles: definition, history, classification, components and applications. From the next chapter on, the book starts from the mathematical foundations and proceeds step by step through kinematics, dynamics, mechatronic design and control, ending with programming and embodied intelligence. With {{ex1_6.n_ch}} chapters the book contains a great deal, and different readers need to read it in different ways: an undergraduate taking a course in robotics, a graduate student preparing to enter a particular field, an engineer facing a specific problem on site — where should each of them begin?

Another feature of this book is that it uses the same five robots as examples from beginning to end. Why do it this way, and what are these five robots like? This section answers these two questions and explains how to use the programs, animations and virtual labs in the book.

### 1.6.2 The structure of the book

Apart from the introduction, which is this chapter, the book is divided into six parts (Figure 1.6.1).

Part I, Mathematical Foundations (Chapters 2–9, {{ex1_6.pc_math}} chapters), covers linear algebra, vectors and tensors, the rotation of rigid bodies, pose and homogeneous transformations, screws and Lie groups, differential equations and numerical methods, optimization, and probability and estimation. These are the tools shared by all the later parts. The book assumes only high-school mathematics from the reader; all other mathematics is developed from the beginning.

Part II, Kinematics (Chapters 10–22, {{ex1_6.pc_kin}} chapters), studies the geometry of robots: when the joints turn to given angles, where is the end-effector; to bring the end-effector to a given place, to what angles must the joints turn; how are the velocities related; how is a smooth trajectory planned. This is one of the cores of the book.

Part III, Dynamics (Chapters 23–29, {{ex1_6.pc_dyn}} chapters), studies the relation between force and motion: how much torque the motors must deliver for the mechanism to move as required, and how to simulate this motion on a computer.

Part IV, Mechanical and Mechatronic Design (Chapters 30–41, {{ex1_6.pc_mech}} chapters), has a mechanical half and an electrical half, covering overall design, structure, reducers, end-effectors and digital prototypes, as well as circuits, motor drives, controller hardware, sensors, signal conditioning, communication and electrical safety.

Part V, Control (Chapters 42–50, {{ex1_6.pc_ctrl}} chapters), goes from the foundations of control theory to single-joint control, multi-joint control, force control, state estimation, mobile-robot navigation, the control of legged robots and quadrotors, and visual servoing.

Part VI, Programming and AI (Chapters 51–61, {{ex1_6.pc_ai}} chapters), covers robot programming, ROS 2, motion planning, robot vision, machine learning, reinforcement learning, imitation learning, the principles and main technical approaches of embodied intelligence, system integration and the digital factory, and finally ethics, regulations and careers.

Each chapter is marked with a level: [Basic] is required undergraduate material, {{ex1_6.lv_basic}} chapters; [Advanced] suits elective undergraduate teaching or graduate students, {{ex1_6.lv_adv}} chapters; [Special topic] is the research frontier and may be skipped whole, {{ex1_6.lv_topic}} chapters; and another {{ex1_6.lv_mixed}} chapters are [Basic/Advanced], with the first half basic and the second half advanced. The book has {{ex1_6.n_sec}} sections in all.

The main thread of the kinematics, dynamics and control in this book is screw theory. As mentioned in Section 1.2, in 1984 Brockett proposed the product-of-exponentials formula for the kinematics of robot arms, which treats each joint as a screw axis; since then the language of screws and Lie groups has gradually become the common language of robotics. We follow this thread because it unifies revolute and prismatic joints, kinematics and dynamics, and velocity and force with one and the same mathematics, with few formulas in a tidy form that is also easy to program. The DH parameter method, widely used in industry and in many examinations, has a chapter of its own, Chapter 13, which also compares the two methods and shows how to convert between them.

Different readers can choose different paths. A one-semester undergraduate course in robotics can cover the [Basic] parts of Chapters 1, 2 (optional), 4, 5, 6, 10–16, 22–25 and 42–44; graduate students can add a selection from Chapters 17–21, 26–29 and 45–50 and Part VI; engineers with a specific problem can go straight to the relevant chapters, and the "Engineer's notes" at the end of each section and the appendices at the end of the book collect selection data and practical experience.

### 1.6.3 Why five robots throughout

The examples in a textbook are often self-contained chapter by chapter: an imaginary two-link arm for kinematics, another one for dynamics, yet another for control, with parameters that have nothing to do with each other. It is hard for the reader to connect the knowledge of the different chapters, or to get a feel for what this knowledge looks like when applied to a real robot.

This book does the opposite and uses five real robots as examples from beginning to end: the collaborative arm UR5e, the seven-axis arm Panda, the quadruped robot Go2, the differential-drive AGV of the digital factory and the quadrotor Skydio X2. All their models come from the WenQuest parts library: the models of the UR5e, Panda, Go2 and X2 come from MuJoCo Menagerie, an open-source model collection maintained by Google DeepMind, and the differential-drive cart is generated by a parametric program in the WenQuest parts library. The model parameters stay the same throughout the book: the end-effector position of the UR5e computed in Chapter 12, its joint torques computed in Chapter 25 and the controller designed for it in Chapter 43 all use the same set of parameters. The manufacturer's figures quoted in the book (payload, reach, weight and so on) are taken from the official datasheets recorded in the parts-library entries, with their sources given.

These five robots cover the main categories of Section 1.3: fixed-base six-axis and seven-axis serial arms, a free-base legged robot, a wheeled robot moving on the ground, and an underactuated aircraft moving in the air. Figure 1.6.2 shows their "link–joint" structures, drawn directly from the joint tables of the parts-library models.

### 1.6.4 UR5e: a collaborative arm

The UR5e is a six-axis collaborative arm in the e-Series launched by Universal Robots in 2018, and it is the main thread of this book's kinematics, dynamics, control and programming. Its six revolute joints are called, in order, the base, shoulder, elbow and three wrist joints (Figure 1.6.2); it has a rated payload of {{ex1_6.payload}} kg, a reach of 850 mm, a pose repeatability of ±{{ex1_6.rep:.2f}} mm, a maximum joint speed of {{ex1_6.v_joint}}°/s and a weight of {{ex1_6.m_ur_sheet:.1f}} kg. The masses of the seven links (including the base) in the parts-library model add up to {{ex1_6.m_ur_model:.2f}} kg, within 2% of the weight given by the manufacturer.

**Example 1.6.1** What exactly is the UR5e's 850 mm reach? When all joint angles are zero, the arm of the UR5e sticks out horizontally (this posture is called the zero position, Chapter 12). Accumulating joint by joint through the joint table of the parts-library model, the flange centre in the zero position is at $({{ex1_6.p0x:.3f}},\ {{ex1_6.p0y:.3f}},\ {{ex1_6.p0z:.3f}})$ m in the base frame. The {{ex1_6.L12:.3f}} m in the x direction is the sum of the upper arm, 0.425 m, and the forearm, 0.392 m; the {{ex1_6.off:.3f}} m in the y direction is the sum of several sideways offsets between shoulder, elbow and wrist. The horizontal distance from the flange centre to the vertical axis of the base is

$$
r_0 = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = \sqrt{ {{ex1_6.L12:.3f}}^2 + {{ex1_6.off:.3f}}^2 } \approx {{ex1_6.r0:.5f}}\ \mathrm{m} ,
$$

which is exactly the 850 mm reach given by the manufacturer. Program 1.6.1 finds $r_0$ in two ways, by hand from the dimensions and by accumulating through the joint table, and the two differ by less than $10^{-6}$ m.

The circular area on the floor that the UR5e can serve is about $\pi \times 0.85^2 \approx {{ex1_6.ur_area:.2f}}$ m², roughly a small desk plus the loading opening of a small machine tool.

When the arm sticks out horizontally, the load at its end produces the largest torque about the shoulder joint. A load of mass m is acted on by gravity m𝔤, the lever arm is the horizontal distance ℓ from the load to the axis of the shoulder joint, and the torque is

$$
\tau = m\,\mathfrak{g}\,\ell , \tag{1.6.1}
$$

where 𝔤 = 9.81 m/s² is the gravitational acceleration, ℓ is in m and τ in N·m. The rated payload of {{ex1_6.payload}} kg at ℓ = {{ex1_6.L12:.3f}} m produces τ = {{ex1_6.tau_payload:.1f}} N·m. This is only the part due to the load; the weight of the arm itself produces a larger torque, which must wait for the dynamic methods of Chapter 25.

### 1.6.5 Panda: a seven-axis arm

The Panda is a seven-axis arm developed by the German company Franka Emika, whose development team received the German Future Prize (Deutscher Zukunftspreis) for it in 2017. Each of its seven joints has a torque sensor, so it can sense and control contact forces with its surroundings, and it is widely used in research. The Panda has been discontinued, and Franka's current research model is the FR3; the book uses the Panda's parameters as given by the parts-library model. According to the manufacturer's datasheet it has a rated payload of {{ex1_6.pa_payload}} kg, a reach of {{ex1_6.pa_reach}} mm and a pose repeatability of ±{{ex1_6.pa_rep:.1f}} mm; joint 4 has a range of −176° to −4°, which means its "elbow" can bend only one way. The masses of the links in the model (including the gripper) add up to {{ex1_6.pa_mass:.2f}} kg.

The Panda has one joint more than the six needed for general spatial tasks. As Section 1.4 explained, this extra degree of freedom lets it change the posture of the arm while the end-effector stays still, which is used to avoid obstacles or keep away from joint limits. The book uses it to teach redundancy (Chapter 17), force control (Chapter 45) and learning (Chapter 57).

### 1.6.6 Go2: a quadruped robot

The Go2 is a quadruped robot released by Unitree in 2023. Its four legs have the same structure, each with three joints: the hip joint swings the leg outwards, and the thigh joint and calf joint swing the leg forwards and backwards. The thigh and the calf are both 0.213 m long. The manufacturer gives a weight of about {{ex1_6.go_sheet}} kg, and the masses of the body and the legs in the parts-library model add up to {{ex1_6.go_mass:.1f}} kg; the education version has a payload of about {{ex1_6.go_payload}} kg and a top speed of {{ex1_6.go_speed:.1f}} m/s.

**Example 1.6.2** The Go2 model gives the joint angles for standing: thigh joint $\theta_t = {{ex1_6.th_t:.1f}}$ rad, calf joint $\theta_c = {{ex1_6.th_c:.1f}}$ rad. How high above the ground is the body when standing?

With the hip joint at zero and the thigh and calf joints also at zero, the leg points straight down, and the foot is 0.426 m below the thigh joint. After the thigh joint turns through $\theta_t$, the thigh makes the angle $\theta_t$ with the vertical, and its vertical projection is $l_1\cos\theta_t$ long; the calf joint then turns through $\theta_c$ relative to the thigh, so the calf makes the angle $\theta_t + \theta_c$ with the vertical and its projection is $l_2\cos(\theta_t + \theta_c)$ long. Adding the two gives the depth of the foot below the thigh joint:

$$
h = l_1\cos\theta_t + l_2\cos(\theta_t + \theta_c) , \tag{1.6.2}
$$

where $l_1 = l_2 = 0.213$ m. Substituting $\theta_t + \theta_c = -0.9$ rad, and since cosine is an even function, the two terms are equal and h = {{ex1_6.h_stand:.4f}} m. The thigh joint is at the same height as the centre of the body, so when standing the body centre is about {{ex1_6.h_stand:.3f}} m above the ground. Program 1.6.1 also carries out a complete forward kinematics through the model's joint table and gets the same result. When standing, the leg is bent with its "knee" pointing backwards, so the body sits well below its straight-leg height of 0.426 m, which leaves the leg room to cushion the impact when it lands.

The body of the Go2 is a free rigid body with 6 degrees of freedom, which together with the 12 joints makes {{ex1_4.go_cfg}} degrees of freedom, yet it has only 12 motors. The motion of the body must be controlled through the forces the legs exert on the ground; this is the core of motion control for legged robots (Chapters 20, 28 and 48).

### 1.6.7 The differential-drive AGV

The AGVs of the digital factory use differential drive: the left and right drive wheels are driven separately by two motors, and a caster at the rear only provides support. In the parts-library model of the differential-drive cart, the drive-wheel radius is r = {{ex1_6.r_w:.2f}} m, the distance between the two wheels (the track) is b = {{ex1_6.b_w:.2f}} m, and the maximum wheel speed is {{ex1_6.w_max}} rad/s.

If the two wheels turn at the same speed, the cart goes straight; if their speeds differ, it turns while moving forwards; if the speeds are equal in size but opposite in direction, it turns on the spot. When the wheels roll on the ground without slipping, the forward speed of the point where a wheel touches the ground equals the wheel radius times the wheel's angular velocity: right wheel $v_R = r\,\omega_R$, left wheel $v_L = r\,\omega_L$. The centre of the cart is midway between the wheels, and its speed is the average of the two wheel speeds; the angular velocity of the cart equals the difference of the two wheel speeds divided by the track (the difference of the wheel speeds turns the cart about a vertical axis, and the two turning radii differ by exactly b):

$$
v = \frac{r(\omega_R + \omega_L)}{2} , \tag{1.6.3}
$$

$$
\omega = \frac{r(\omega_R - \omega_L)}{b} . \tag{1.6.4}
$$

Here ω is positive anticlockwise (seen from above), in rad/s; v is positive in the direction the cart faces, in m/s. Chapter 19 derives these two formulas rigorously.

**Example 1.6.3** When both wheels turn in the same direction at the maximum speed of {{ex1_6.w_max}} rad/s, Eq. (1.6.3) gives a cart speed of $v = {{ex1_6.v_max:.1f}}$ m/s, exactly the speed of the AGVs in the digital factory simulation. When the two wheels turn in opposite directions at maximum speed, Eq. (1.6.4) gives an angular velocity on the spot of $\omega = {{ex1_6.om_max:.2f}}$ rad/s, so one full turn takes only {{ex1_6.t_turn:.2f}} s. In practice the angular velocity is much smaller, or the parts on the cart would be thrown off.

The position and heading of the cart on the floor have 3 degrees of freedom, but it has only 2 drive wheels. It cannot move sideways directly, because the wheels cannot slide sideways; this is the nonholonomic constraint mentioned in Section 1.3 (Chapter 19).

### 1.6.8 The X2 quadrotor

The aerial robot of this book is the simulation model of the X2 drone released by Skydio in 2020. In the model it is a rigid body of mass m = {{ex1_6.m_x2:.3f}} kg with no joints; each of its four rotors produces a vertical upward thrust, each thrust ranging from 0 to {{ex1_6.f_max:.0f}} N. The horizontal distance from the rotor centres to the centre of the body is {{ex1_6.arm:.3f}} m.

**Example 1.6.4** When hovering, the sum of the four thrusts balances gravity. The four rotors are placed symmetrically about the centre of the body, so to produce no torque that would tip the body over, the four thrusts are equal, each being

$$
F_{\text{hover}} = \frac{m\,\mathfrak{g}}{4} = \frac{ {{ex1_6.m_x2:.3f}} \times 9.81}{4} = {{ex1_6.f_hover:.4f}}\ \mathrm{N} . \tag{1.6.5}
$$

The "hover" state in the model file gives exactly {{ex1_6.hover_key:.4f}} N as the four thrust commands, and Program 1.6.1 checks that the two agree. The ratio of the sum of the maximum thrusts of the four rotors to the weight is called the **thrust-to-weight ratio**; for the X2 model it is $4 \times {{ex1_6.f_max:.0f}} / {{ex1_6.W_x2:.2f}} \approx {{ex1_6.tw:.1f}}$. The thrust-to-weight ratio must exceed 1 for take-off; the larger it is, the more agile the craft. If all four rotors work at maximum thrust, then, ignoring air drag, the upward acceleration of the body is $(4F_{\max} - m\mathfrak{g})/m \approx {{ex1_6.a_up:.0f}}$ m/s², about 3 times the gravitational acceleration.

The body of a quadrotor has 6 degrees of freedom but only 4 adjustable thrusts, so it is underactuated. Its attitude and position are coupled: to fly forwards it must first pitch nose-down so that the thrust tilts forwards (Chapters 21, 28 and 49).

### 1.6.10 How to use the programs, animations and labs of this book

This book follows the principle "every computation has a simulation, every piece of programming has a lab". Every computed number in the text is computed by a program listed in the book: when the book is built, the results of the programs are filled into the text automatically, so the numbers in the book always agree with the numbers the programs compute. Readers can open each program to see how it computes, change the parameters themselves and run it again.

Animations show processes of change, such as the unfolding of the timeline in Figure 1.2.1 or an AGV driving through the workshop. Every animation comes with a static figure, so that a reader of the printed book can follow it too.

The virtual labs run in the browser and need no software to be installed. Each lab has several tasks, which are ticked off automatically when completed; each lab also comes with a lab guide and a lab report template that can be downloaded. We suggest that after reading the text of a section, readers first do the lab of that section and then the exercises.

### 1.6.11 An everyday example

The human arm is a fine "redundant robot arm". The shoulder joint can swing forwards and backwards and from side to side, and can also turn about the upper arm itself, which amounts to 3 revolute joints; the elbow joint is 1; the forearm and wrist together let the hand turn in three directions, which amounts to 3 more. That makes 7 in all, the same as the Panda.

Readers can try a small experiment: press the palm flat on a table, keep the palm still, and raise and lower the elbow. Neither the position nor the orientation of the hand changes, yet the posture of the arm does. This is the redundancy described in Section 1.4: the position and orientation of the hand take 6 numbers, while the arm has 7 degrees of freedom, and the extra one allows self-motion of the arm while the hand stays still. When people reach for something in a cramped place, they instinctively use this degree of freedom to get around obstacles; Chapter 17 explains how to make the Panda do the same.

**Engineer's notes**

- Before using the parameters of a robot in a project, check their source and version: the manufacturer's datasheet, the CAD model and the simulation model (URDF, MJCF) often differ at the millimetre level (Chapter 12), and different configurations of the same model may also differ (for example the various versions of the Go2, or the basic and education versions of the G1).
- Parameters given by manufacturers, such as reach and payload, all come with test conditions. Computing them yourself from the model, as in Example 1.6.1, is a good way to find out what a parameter really means.
- The top speed $r\,\omega_{\max}$ of a differential-drive cart can be reached only when driving straight; in a turn the outer wheel reaches its speed limit first, and the cart speed must be reduced accordingly.
- The thrust-to-weight ratio of a quadrotor determines its ability to resist wind and its payload margin. After fitting a gimbal or a battery, recompute the hover thrust; if the hover thrust takes too large a share of the maximum thrust, too little margin is left for attitude control.
