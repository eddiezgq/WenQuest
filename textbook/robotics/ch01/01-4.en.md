### 1.4.1 The problem

Press "Run" on the teach pendant and the arm of the UR5e reaches smoothly towards the chuck of the lathe, grips a shaft, turns round and places it on the rack. What happens between pressing the button and the arm starting to move? How does a program turn into the rotation of the joints? And how does the arm know how far it has turned?

To answer these questions we have to take the robot apart. By the definition in Section 1.1, a robot is a whole made up of a mechanism, actuators, sensors, a controller and software. This section divides it into seven parts: mechanism, actuation, electrics, sensing, control, software and intelligence. Using the UR5e as the example, it explains what each part does and how energy and information pass between them (Figure 1.4.1). While discussing the mechanism, we introduce a basic concept that runs through the whole book: degrees of freedom.

There are two flows in Figure 1.4.1. One is the flow of energy: electrical energy goes from the mains through the power supply and the servo drives to the motors, the motors drive the joints through the reducers, and the mechanism passes the motion on to the workpiece. The other is the flow of information: sensors measure the state of the mechanism and of the environment, the controller and the software compute commands from these measurements, and the commands in turn control the flow of energy. This is exactly the sense–decide–act loop of Section 1.1, laid out in hardware.

### 1.4.2 Mechanism and degrees of freedom

A **mechanism** is a device made of members connected by joints, in which the members can move relative to one another in a definite way. The members of a robot are usually treated as rigid bodies and are called **links**; a part that connects two links and restricts their relative motion is called a **joint**. The two most common joints in robots are the **revolute joint**, which only lets two links turn relative to each other about a fixed axis, like the human elbow, and the **prismatic joint**, which only lets two links slide relative to each other along a fixed line, like a drawer. A system of links connected one after another by joints is called a **kinematic chain**. A kinematic chain with only one path from the base to the end of the arm is called an **open chain**, and a serial robot is an open chain; one whose links are connected in a loop is called a **closed chain**, and a parallel robot is a closed chain. The end of the mechanism usually carries a device that acts directly on the workpiece, called the **end-effector**, such as a **gripper**, a suction cup or a welding torch.

How many numbers are needed to describe where a mechanism is? This question leads to the concept of degrees of freedom.

**Definition 1.4.1 (Degrees of freedom)** The least number of independent variables needed to determine the position of an object or a mechanism is called its number of **degrees of freedom** (DOF).

"Position" is meant here in a broad sense, including which way the object faces. The positions of all the parts of a mechanism taken together are called its **configuration**, so the number of degrees of freedom is also the least number of independent variables needed to determine the configuration. "Independent" is the key word: if there are relations among several variables, so that knowing some of them lets us work out the others, then the latter are not counted as degrees of freedom.

Start with the simplest cases. A point in the plane needs two coordinates (x, y), so it has 2 degrees of freedom; in space it needs three coordinates (x, y, z), so it has 3. What about a rigid body?

**Theorem 1.4.1** A rigid body moving in the plane has 3 degrees of freedom; a rigid body moving freely in space has 6 degrees of freedom.

*Proof*　First the plane. Take two points A and B on the rigid body; together they have 4 coordinates $(x_A, y_A, x_B, y_B)$. What makes a body rigid is that the distance between any two of its points does not change, so these 4 coordinates satisfy one constraint:

$$
(x_A - x_B)^2 + (y_A - y_B)^2 = L^2 , \tag{1.4.1}
$$

where L is the distance between A and B. Four variables subject to 1 constraint leave only 3 independent ones. Conversely, once A and B are fixed, the position of every point P of the body is fixed too: the distances from P to A and to B are known, and we know on which side of the line AB the point P lies, so P has only one possible position. Hence a rigid body in the plane has 4 − 1 = 3 degrees of freedom. The three variables commonly used are the coordinates (x, y) of a reference point and the angle φ through which the body has turned (Figure 1.4.2a).

Now space. Take three non-collinear points A, B and C on the body, with 9 coordinates in all. The three distances between pairs of points do not change, which gives 3 constraints:

$$
|AB| = L_1, \qquad |BC| = L_2, \qquad |CA| = L_3 . \tag{1.4.2}
$$

That leaves 9 − 3 = 6 independent variables. Once the three points are fixed, the position of every point of the body is fixed too (its distances to the three points are known, and we know on which side of the plane ABC it lies). Hence a rigid body in space has 6 degrees of freedom. The six variables commonly used are the three coordinates of a reference point and three angles describing the orientation (Figure 1.4.2c); Chapters 4 and 5 discuss them in detail.　∎

Taking one more point does not change the conclusion: a fourth point brings 3 coordinates, but also 3 distance constraints to the first three points, and the number of degrees of freedom is still 6. The count above, however, assumes that the constraints are independent of one another. To confirm this, Program 1.4.1 takes randomly chosen points and computes the rank of the matrix formed by the partial derivatives of the constraints with respect to the coordinates (called the Jacobian matrix of the constraints, Chapters 2 and 16), that is, the number of truly independent constraints: for two points in the plane the rank is {{ex1_4.plane_rank}}, for three points in space it is {{ex1_4.space_rank}}, and for four points in space the {{ex1_4.space4_cons}} distance constraints have rank {{ex1_4.space4_rank}}. In all three cases "the number of coordinates minus the rank" agrees with Theorem 1.4.1.

With the degrees of freedom of a rigid body in hand, we can work out the degrees of freedom of a mechanism. A revolute joint connects two links and only lets them turn relative to each other about one axis. One angle is enough to describe this relative motion, so we say that a revolute joint has 1 degree of freedom. Likewise, a prismatic joint has 1 degree of freedom. Seen another way: two rigid bodies in space have 6 relative degrees of freedom to begin with; a revolute joint removes 5 of them and leaves only 1.

For an open chain with a fixed base, starting from the base, the angle of the first joint fixes the position of the first link; once the first link is fixed, the angle of the second joint fixes the second link, and so on. The variable of each joint can be chosen independently, and once all the joint variables are fixed, the position of every link is fixed. Therefore:

**Theorem 1.4.2** The number of degrees of freedom of an open-chain mechanism with a fixed base is the sum of the degrees of freedom of its joints:

$$
m = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f_i , \tag{1.4.3}
$$

where n is the number of joints and $f_i$ is the number of degrees of freedom of the i-th joint (1 for both revolute and prismatic joints). If the base itself is a rigid body moving freely in the plane or in space, add the 3 or 6 degrees of freedom of the base.

The proof is the argument of the paragraph above. Note the two conditions behind Eq. (1.4.3): the chain is open, and the joint variables are independent of one another. In a closed chain (such as the Delta robot), the chains must close up at the moving platform, so there are constraints among the joint variables, and the number of degrees of freedom has to be computed with the Grübler formula of Chapter 11. Where joints are mechanically coupled, the coupled joints count as only one independent variable.

All four of these robots have fewer actuators than degrees of freedom.

**Definition 1.4.2 (Underactuation)** A robot with fewer actuators than degrees of freedom is called an **underactuated** robot.

Underactuation does not mean that some degrees of freedom cannot be controlled; it means that each degree of freedom cannot be controlled independently and arbitrarily. To fly forwards, a quadrotor must first tilt its body forwards so that the thrust has a forward component; to get to a point directly to its left, a cart must first turn. Dealing with underactuation is the central difficulty in motion planning and control for mobile, legged and aerial robots (Chapters 19–21, 48 and 49).

The degrees of freedom of a robot arm are directly related to the tasks it can do. The end-effector is a rigid body in space, and by Theorem 1.4.1 it takes 6 degrees of freedom to give it an arbitrary position and orientation, which is why most general-purpose industrial arms have 6 axes. With fewer than 6, position and orientation of the end-effector cannot be combined freely; a 4-axis SCARA, for example, can only turn its tool about a vertical axis. With more than 6, as in the 7-axis Panda, there are infinitely many arm poses for the same end-effector pose, and the extra degrees of freedom, called redundancy, can be used to avoid obstacles (Chapter 17).

### 1.4.3 Actuation

An **actuator** converts energy into motion of the mechanism. Early industrial robots, such as the first Unimate, were mostly hydraulically driven; today the great majority of robots are driven by electric motors. Hydraulic drives are used mainly where very large forces are needed, and pneumatic drives mostly for simple open-and-close motions, as in a pneumatic gripper.

An electric motor turns fast with a small torque; a joint needs just the opposite: slow rotation with a large torque. So a **speed reducer** is almost always fitted between the motor and the joint. The ratio of the input-shaft speed of the reducer to its output-shaft speed is called the **reduction ratio**, written i. Ignoring losses, power equals torque times angular velocity and input power equals output power, so a reducer that brings the speed down to 1/i multiplies the torque by i. A real reducer has friction losses, and the output torque must also be multiplied by the efficiency η:

$$
n_{\text{out}} = \frac{n_{\text{in}}}{i}, \qquad \tau_{\text{out}} = i\,\eta\,\tau_{\text{in}} , \tag{1.4.4}
$$

where n is speed (r/min), τ is torque (N·m) and η is an efficiency between 0 and 1.

**Example 1.4.2** The maximum speed of each joint of the UR5e is 180°/s, that is, half a turn per second, or {{ex1_4.n_joint:.0f}} r/min. If the rated speed of the joint motor is {{ex1_4.n_motor:.0f}} r/min, Eq. (1.4.4) gives the required reduction ratio as $i = {{ex1_4.n_motor:.0f}} / {{ex1_4.n_joint:.0f}} = {{ex1_4.i_need:.0f}}$. With a reducer efficiency of η = {{ex1_4.eta:.1f}}, a motor output of 1 N·m gives the joint {{ex1_4.tau_joint:.0f}} N·m.

A reduction ratio this large calls for a reducer that achieves it in a single stage, is compact and has no backlash, so collaborative robots mostly use harmonic drives (Chapter 32). For contrast, the WQR-105 made in the digital factory is an ordinary two-stage cylindrical-gear reducer: the first stage has a tooth ratio of 72/24 = 3 and the second 70/20 = 3.5, so the overall reduction ratio is the product of the two, {{ex1_4.i_wqr:.1f}}, and with an input of {{ex1_4.n_in}} r/min the output is {{ex1_4.n_out:.1f}} r/min. Both satisfy Eq. (1.4.4), but they are very different in construction and use: the WQR-105 serves continuously running equipment such as conveyors and mixers, which is not sensitive to backlash.

### 1.4.4 Electrics

For the actuators to work, a complete electrical system is needed: a power supply that turns alternating current from the mains into the direct current the robot uses; servo drives that turn the controller's commands into currents in the motor windings; the cables and connectors that link the parts; and the emergency-stop circuit and brakes that ensure safety. The typical power consumption of the UR5e is about 200 W, with a maximum of about 570 W, supplied from the control box.

The electrics are often regarded as "auxiliary", yet a large share of the faults on the shop floor arise here: cables break from repeated flexing, poor earthing brings in interference, an emergency-stop circuit is wired wrongly. The second half of Part IV of this book (Chapters 35–41) is devoted to circuits, motor drives, controller hardware, sensors, signal conditioning, communication buses and electrical safety.

### 1.4.5 Sensing

A **sensor** turns a physical quantity into an electrical signal for the controller to use. Robot sensors are of two kinds. One kind measures the state of the robot itself: encoders measuring joint angles, current sensors measuring motor currents, torque sensors measuring joint torques, and inertial measurement units measuring the attitude and acceleration of the body. The other kind measures the environment: cameras, lidars, force/torque sensors at the end of the arm, and tactile sensors.

The joint encoder is the most basic sensor of a robot arm. It gives a certain number of counts per revolution; the more counts, the smaller the angle it can resolve.

**Example 1.4.3** Suppose a joint encoder gives $2^{20} = {{ex1_4.counts}}$ counts per revolution (20 bits). The smallest angle it can resolve is 360°/{{ex1_4.counts}} ≈ ${{ex1_4.dtheta_deg:sci2}}$°. At the end of the arm, 0.85 m from the joint, this angle corresponds to an arc length of

$$
\Delta s = R\,\Delta\theta = 0.85\ \mathrm{m} \times \frac{2\pi}{2^{20}} \approx {{ex1_4.ds_um:.1f}}\ \mu\mathrm{m} . \tag{1.4.5}
$$

Here the arc length equals the radius times the angle in radians. The **pose repeatability** of the UR5e, that is, the spread of end positions when the same command is executed over and over, is ±0.03 mm, about {{ex1_4.rep_ratio:.0f}} times the arc length above. So the resolution of the encoder leaves ample margin, and the repeatability at the end-effector is limited mainly by such factors as backlash in the reducers, deformation of the structure and temperature.

### 1.4.6 Control

The **controller** is the "cerebellum" of the robot: it takes the motion requirements given by the program, works out where each joint should be at each moment, and drives the joints to follow. Control is usually divided into several layers. The innermost is the servo loop of each joint, which adjusts the motor current according to the encoder reading, with a period below a millisecond; the middle layer is trajectory interpolation, which turns a requirement such as "move in a straight line from point A to point B" into a dense sequence of joint angles; the outermost is the logic of the task program.

The controller updates its commands once every control cycle.

**Example 1.4.4** The real-time data interface of the UR e-Series controller exchanges data with the outside at {{ex1_4.f_ctrl:.0f}} Hz, so the control cycle is $T = 1/f = {{ex1_4.T_ctrl_ms:.0f}}$ ms. When the end-effector moves at 1 m/s, it travels $\Delta s = vT = {{ex1_4.ds_cycle_mm:.0f}}$ mm in each cycle.

This number determines how "fine" the influence of an external program (one that corrects the trajectory from vision, for instance) on the robot's motion can be. 2 mm is enough for handling, but too coarse for high-precision force-controlled assembly, so force-control loops are often run in a faster layer inside the controller. Chapters 42–45 discuss the theory and methods of control.

### 1.4.7 Software

Software organizes all the parts above into one whole. The software on a robot includes: the real-time operating system and low-level drivers; motion control and safety monitoring; task programs, which may be taught with the teach pendant, written in a robot language, or generated offline on a computer; interfaces for communicating with external equipment; and tools for simulation, debugging and monitoring. The Robot Operating System (ROS), developed from 2007 onwards, provides a general software framework that lets modules from different sources be used together (Chapter 52).

Every example in this book comes with a runnable Python program, and every programming topic comes with a virtual lab in the browser. While learning the principles, the reader is also practising the basic skills of robot software (Chapter 51).

### 1.4.8 Intelligence

"Intelligence" means the abilities of a robot in perception, decision-making and learning: recognizing a workpiece and its pose in an image, planning a path that avoids obstacles, breaking a natural-language instruction down into a sequence of actions, learning a new skill from demonstration or from trial and error. In terms of the model of Section 1.1, intelligence determines how strong the decision rule π is, and where it comes from.

In conventional industrial robots the intelligence layer is thin: the position of the workpiece is guaranteed by fixtures, paths are taught by people, and programs are fixed. As vision, planning and learning technologies advance, the intelligence layer grows thicker, and the robot can cope with more and more uncertainty. But we must also see that intelligence cannot do without the other six parts: however good the algorithm, it can only become action through a precise mechanism, powerful actuation, reliable electrics, accurate sensing and fast control. That is what "embodied" means, and it is why this book begins with mathematics and mechanisms and ends with embodied intelligence.

### 1.4.9 An everyday example

The human body offers a familiar comparison for the seven parts of a robot. Bones and joints are the mechanism, muscles are the actuators, nerves are the electrical system, and the receptors in the eyes, ears, skin and muscles are the sensors. The spinal cord and the cerebellum carry out reflexes and coordinate movements, which corresponds to the controller; the experience in the brain of "how to ride a bicycle" corresponds to software; and the ability to understand goals, plan actions and learn new skills corresponds to intelligence.

The analogy has its weak points. Human muscles need no reducers, and the degrees of freedom and compliance of human joints far exceed those of today's robot arms; people keep learning from infancy, while most of a robot's abilities still have to be designed by engineers. But it helps to remember one key point: the abilities of a robot depend on how well its parts work together, and a weakness in any one part limits the whole.

**Engineer's notes**

- When choosing motors and reducers, first work out the joint torques and speeds from the load and the motion requirements, then match them using Eq. (1.4.4). Be sure to check both the peak torque and the RMS torque (Chapter 36).
- The pose repeatability on a robot datasheet (with the test method laid down in ISO 9283) is not the same as absolute (pose) accuracy. The former says how precisely the robot "returns to the same place"; the latter says how precisely it "reaches the place that was computed". The latter is usually an order of magnitude worse and is improved by calibration (Chapter 26).
- When commissioning on site, track down problems layer by layer following Figure 1.4.1: first check the electrics (power supply, emergency stop, communication), then whether the sensor readings are reasonable, then the control parameters, and only last suspect the program logic.
- In an underactuated system you cannot "adjust whichever actuator belongs to the degree of freedom that is off"; the coupling between the commands has to be handled through a model.
