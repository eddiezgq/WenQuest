### 1.1.1 The problem

The WenQuest digital factory makes the WQR-105 speed reducer. In the workshop, once an output-shaft blank has been cut off on the band saw, it has to be taken to a CNC lathe a dozen or so metres away for rough turning. Imagine two ways of doing this. The first is to lay a section of roller conveyor between the two machines: a motor turns the rollers at a fixed rhythm, and the parts roll along an unchanging path. The second is to send an automated guided vehicle (AGV): the cart receives the job, plans a route along the aisle, and while driving it watches the way ahead with a laser scanner; if someone crosses in front of it, it slows down and stops, then carries on once the person has gone, and at the station it hands the part to the robot arm beside the lathe.

Both methods get the part there, and both are driven by motors and controlled by programs. Yet in everyday language people would call the cart a robot, but would rarely call the conveyor one. Where exactly is the difference?

This is not just a question of words. Whether a device counts as a robot decides which safety standards apply to it, whether it is included in industry statistics, and which questions have to be considered when it is designed. Engineering needs a definition clear enough to sort concrete devices into it; learners also need to know why the line is drawn where it is. This section first tells where the word "robot" came from, then gives the definition of the international standard, then uses a simple model of how a robot works to explain the most important part of the definition, autonomy, and finally discusses the boundaries of the definition.

### 1.1.2 Where the word "robot" comes from

The word "robot" comes from the play *R.U.R.* (*Rossum's Universal Robots*) by the Czech writer Karel Čapek. The play was published in 1920 and first performed at the National Theatre in Prague in January 1921. The "robots" of the play are artificial workers made in a factory by chemical and biological methods, not machines of metal. The word was derived from the Czech *robota*, which originally meant the forced labour a serf owed to his lord. According to Čapek's own later account, the word was suggested by his brother, the painter Josef Čapek.

Twenty years later, the American writer Isaac Asimov coined the word *robotics* in his science fiction, and in the 1942 short story *Runaround* he set out the famous "Three Laws of Robotics". *Robotics* later became the name of an engineering discipline.

We see that when the word "robot" appeared, there was not yet a single robot in the world in today's sense. It was first a literary figure, expressing people's imagination of, and worry about, "man-made things that labour in place of people". The first industrial robot was not put into service until 1961 (Section 1.2). And precisely because the word came before the thing, its everyday use is very broad: from robot arms in factories to the customer-service "bots" of chat software, all are called robots. Engineering has to narrow it down.

### 1.1.3 The definition in the standard

ISO 8373, *Robotics — Vocabulary*, of the International Organization for Standardization gives the basic terms of the robotics field; the current version is the 2021 edition. China's GB/T 12643—2025 *Robotics — Vocabulary* adopts this edition identically; it came into force on 1 September 2025 and replaced GB/T 12643—2013, which had identically adopted the 2012 edition. This book uses the definitions of the 2021 edition.

**Definition 1.1.1 (Robot)** A **robot** is a programmed actuated mechanism with a degree of autonomy to perform locomotion, manipulation or positioning.

**Definition 1.1.2 (Autonomy)** **Autonomy** is the ability to perform intended tasks based on current state and sensing, without human intervention.

Definition 1.1.1 is short, but every word carries weight. We explain them one by one.

A "mechanism" is a device made of a number of rigid members (links) connected by joints so that they can move relative to one another. Robot arms, wheeled bases and legs are all mechanisms. Without a mechanism there is no motion, so a piece of software that can only talk is not a robot.

"Actuated" means that the motion of the mechanism is produced by actuators. An actuator turns electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic or some other form of energy into motion of the mechanism; the most common is the electric motor. A mechanism cranked by hand is not a robot.

"Programmed" means that what motions the mechanism makes, and in what order, is laid down by a program, and that the program can be rewritten. The program here need not be a computer program; Section 1.2 will show that cams and punched cards are programs too. But note the point about rewriting: a machine that can only perform one fixed motion, and needs parts replaced to change that motion, is usually not called a robot.

"With a degree of autonomy" is the most important part of the definition. By Definition 1.1.2, autonomy means that the robot decides how to move on the basis of the current state it has measured itself (including its own state and that of its surroundings), without needing a person beside it to direct it step by step. "A degree of" says that autonomy can be higher or lower. The standard notes that the degree of autonomy can be judged from two sides: the quality of the decisions and the extent of dependence on people.

"To perform locomotion, manipulation or positioning" states what a robot is for: locomotion means moving itself through the environment, as carts and quadruped robots do; manipulation means changing the position and orientation of objects, as a robot arm does when it grasps a part; positioning means placing some object (a tool, a sensor, a patient) at a specified location.

The standard also notes that a robot includes its control system. In other words, a robot is not a "mechanical body" alone but a whole made up of mechanism, actuators, sensors, controller and software. Section 1.4 discusses these parts one by one.

Under this general definition, the standard distinguishes three kinds of robot by application.

**Definition 1.1.3 (Industrial robot)** An **industrial robot** is a manipulator that is automatically controlled, reprogrammable and multipurpose, with three or more axes that can be programmed; it may be mounted in a fixed place or on a mobile platform, and it is used for automated work in an industrial setting.

This definition has two key points. First, an industrial robot must be "multipurpose" and "reprogrammable": the same robot arm that handles parts today can weld tomorrow after a section of its program is changed and its end-effector is swapped. Second, "three or more axes" is a minimum requirement on its ability to move; an axis here means a joint motion that can be controlled independently, and it is closely related to the concept of degrees of freedom in Section 1.4.

**Definition 1.1.4 (Service robot and medical robot)** A **service robot** is a robot that does useful tasks for people or for equipment, in personal or professional settings. A **medical robot** is a robot used as medical electrical equipment or as a medical electrical system; under ISO 8373:2021 it is classed neither as an industrial robot nor as a service robot.

Medical robots are listed separately because they must meet the regulations and safety standards for medical devices, which are entirely different from those for factory equipment or household appliances. Section 1.3 discusses the classification of robots in detail along two threads, application and structure.

### 1.1.4 The sense–decide–act loop

Definition 1.1.2 says that autonomy is the ability to perform intended tasks "based on current state and sensing". To make this statement clear, we build the simplest possible model of how a robot works.

The situation of a robot at any moment can be described by a set of numbers, called its **state** and written x. For the cart of Section 1.1.1, the state includes its position, heading and speed, and whether there is an obstacle ahead and how far away it is; for a robot arm, the state includes the angles and angular velocities of its joints and where the workpiece is. Some of the quantities in the state belong to the robot itself, others to the environment.

A robot cannot "know" its state directly; it can only measure it with sensors. The set of numbers obtained by measurement is written y, and it is a function of the state:

$$
y = h(x), \tag{1.1.1}
$$

where h stands for the measuring relation of the sensors. For example, a laser scanner measures distances to obstacles, and an encoder measures a joint angle. Real measurements always carry errors; dealing with measurement errors is the subject of Chapters 9 and 46, and we ignore them here.

The program in the controller decides, from the measured values, the command sent to the actuators, written u:

$$
u = \pi(y), \tag{1.1.2}
$$

where π stands for the decision rule implemented by the program, for example "brake if an obstacle ahead is closer than a certain distance". The actuators carry out the command, the mechanism moves, and the state changes accordingly. The controller does not work continuously; instead it reads the sensors and computes a command once every fixed interval of time T, and this interval is called the **control cycle**. If the state at the start of the k-th cycle is written $x_k$ and the command $u_k$, then

$$
x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k), \tag{1.1.3}
$$

where f describes how the state changes during one control cycle under the command $u_k$. Eqs. (1.1.1) to (1.1.3) join end to end to form a cycle: measure, decide, act, measure again. This cycle is called the **sense–decide–act loop** (Figure 1.1.1).

caption: The sense–decide–act loop: the sensors measure y = h(x), the program gives the command u = π(y), the actuators move the mechanism and change the state x, and in the next control cycle the measurement starts again

The command is computed from the measured values, and the measured values in turn reflect the result of acting; this structure is called **feedback**. Control in which the command is determined by feedback is called **closed-loop control**. By contrast, if the command is a sequence $u_0, u_1, u_2, \ldots$ arranged in advance that depends only on time and not on the measured values, it is called **open-loop control**. The roller conveyor of Section 1.1.1 is open-loop: whether or not there are parts on it, and whether or not a part has jammed, the rollers keep turning just the same.

This lets us state autonomy more precisely: the autonomy of a machine lies in how far its decision π depends on its own measurements y, and in how wide a range of situations that dependence can cope with. A machine that only moves to a timetable has no autonomy; a machine that can adjust its motion according to its measurements has some autonomy; a machine that can choose its own goals, plan its route and handle the unexpected in an environment it has never seen has greater autonomy. What embodied intelligence (Section 1.2, Chapter 58) pursues is precisely to extend this range to the open real world.

**Example 1.1.1** The digital factory's AGV travels at v = {{ex1_1.v:.1f}} m/s. How far ahead must its laser scanner detect an obstacle for it to stop in time? Take the control cycle as $T = {{ex1_1.T_c:.2f}}$ s; from recognizing the obstacle and issuing the braking command until the brake acts takes another {{ex1_1.t_p:.2f}} s; when braking, the cart decelerates uniformly at a = {{ex1_1.a:.1f}} m/s², a deceleration small enough that the parts on the cart do not slide.

The time from the sensor detecting the obstacle until the cart begins to slow down is called the reaction time, written $t_d$. The worst case is that the obstacle appears just after a measurement and is only detected in the next cycle, so we take the reaction time as $t_d = T + {{ex1_1.t_p:.2f}}\ \mathrm{s} = {{ex1_1.t_d:.1f}}\ \mathrm{s}$. During this time the cart still moves at the constant speed v and covers the distance $v\,t_d$.

After that the cart decelerates uniformly. A time t after braking begins, its speed is $v - a t$, so it stops after $t_b = v/a$. In uniformly decelerated motion the speed falls evenly with time, so the mean speed over this time is the mean of the initial and final speeds, $v/2$, and the distance covered is the mean speed times the time:

$$
s_b = \frac{v}{2}\cdot\frac{v}{a} = \frac{v^2}{2a}.
$$

Adding the two parts gives the total distance covered from detecting the obstacle to standing still, that is, the stopping distance:

$$
d = v\,t_d + \frac{v^2}{2a}. \tag{1.1.4}
$$

Here v is in m/s, $t_d$ in s, a in m/s² and d in m. Substituting the values, the cart covers {{ex1_1.d_react:.1f}} m during the reaction time and {{ex1_1.d_brake:.1f}} m while braking, d = {{ex1_1.d:.1f}} m in all; from detecting the obstacle to standing still takes $t_d + v/a = {{ex1_1.t_stop:.1f}}$ s. Program 1.1.1 checks this result by another method: it divides the motion into small steps of $10^{-5}$ s and advances step by step, and the stopping distance it obtains differs from Eq. (1.1.4) by less than $10^{-4}$ m.

So the scanner must detect obstacles at least {{ex1_1.d:.1f}} m away. In engineering a margin is added as well, for example a factor of 1.5, giving {{ex1_1.d_margin:.2f}} m. Figure 1.1.2 shows how the stopping distance varies with speed. Since the braking distance is proportional to the square of the speed, raising the speed by half to {{ex1_1.v2:.1f}} m/s makes the braking distance {{ex1_1.ratio_brake:.2f}} times as long, and the stopping distance grows to {{ex1_1.d2:.1f}} m. This is why the AGVs in a workshop do not drive fast.

For comparison, imagine a cart that does not look at its sensors, programmed to drive {{ex1_1.L_prog:.0f}} m along the aisle. If someone pushing a trolley passes at {{ex1_1.x_obs:.0f}} m, it is bound to run into them, because its commands depend only on time and have nothing to do with what lies ahead. The two carts can have exactly the same mechanism, motors and speed; the only difference is whether the decisions depend on sensing. By Definition 1.1.1, the former is not a robot and the latter is.

### 1.1.5 The boundaries of the definition

With Definition 1.1.1 and the sense–decide–act loop, we can check item by item whether a device is a robot. Table 1.1.1 lists the results of checking some common devices.

::: Table 1.1.1
caption: Several devices checked item by item against Definition 1.1.1
:::

| Device | Actuated mechanism | Rewritable program | Acts autonomously on sensing | Locomotion, manipulation or positioning | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roller conveyor | Yes | Usually not | No | Conveying | Automated equipment |
| Washing machine | Yes (drum) | Yes | Yes (water level, speed) | Does not move itself or handle objects | Household appliance |
| CNC lathe | Yes | Yes | Yes (position feedback) | Positions the tool | By convention a machine tool |
| Remote-controlled toy car | Yes | No | No, entirely operated by a person | Locomotion | Remote-controlled device |
| Robot vacuum cleaner | Yes | Yes | Yes | Locomotion | Service robot |
| AGV in the digital factory | Yes | Yes | Yes | Locomotion, material handling | Mobile robot |
| UR5e robot arm | Yes | Yes | Yes (joint feedback, vision can be added) | Manipulation | Industrial robot |
| Smart speaker | No | Yes | Yes | None | Not a robot |
| Assistive exoskeleton | Yes | Yes | Yes (senses the wearer's movements) | Moves the wearer's limbs | Wearable robot |

For most of the devices in the table the classification is obvious; the ones worth discussing are a few examples that lie on the boundary.

A CNC lathe satisfies every item of the definition: it has an actuated mechanism, its program can be rewritten, each axis has position feedback, and its purpose is to position the tool. Yet it is usually not called a robot, and industry statistics count it as a machine tool. This shows that the definition in the standard is not a purely logical criterion; it also reflects the conventions of the industry: a machine tool is designed specifically for cutting, whereas an industrial robot stresses being "multipurpose", so that the same robot arm can go and do work of an entirely different nature.

An **exoskeleton** is a device worn on the human body, driven by motors, that moves or assists the movement of the wearer's limbs. The standard lists it as an example of a robot, calling it a wearable robot. Its "locomotion" is carried out together with the person, and its autonomy lies in adjusting the assistance according to the person's intended movement.

A remote-controlled toy car is operated entirely by a person, has no autonomy and is not a robot. But there is an important class of robots that is also largely operated by a person: the **surgical robot**. The surgeon sits at a console and works the hand controllers, and the instruments on the robot arms follow inside the patient's body. It is still regarded as a robot because, under the person's control, the machine does a great deal of work autonomously in every control cycle: it scales the large movements of the surgeon's hands down in proportion, filters out hand tremor, and makes sure the instruments pivot about the incision as a fixed point without tearing tissue. This shows that autonomy really does come in "degrees".

A self-driving car satisfies every item of the definition and, technically, is a kind of mobile robot. But it has its own body of regulations and its own statistical scope, and is usually discussed separately.

The discussion above shows that the purpose of the definition is communication and management: to make sure that different people mean the same class of things when they say "robot", and to give standards, regulations and statistics a common scope. It does not require that every concrete device can be classified without dispute. In its exposition this book follows Definition 1.1.1, and when a case on the boundary comes up, it says in which item the case differs from a typical robot.

### 1.1.6 What robotics studies

**Definition 1.1.5 (Robotics)** **Robotics** is the discipline that studies the design, modelling, sensing, planning and control of robots.

Robotics did not branch off from a single discipline; it is the product of mechanical engineering, electrical engineering, control, computing and mathematics coming together. Comparing with the loop of Figure 1.1.1, we can see the basic questions it has to answer.

First, the geometry of the mechanism. When the joints turn to certain angles, where is the end-effector and which way does it face; to make the end-effector reach a given place, to what angles should the joints turn? This is kinematics, the subject of Part II of this book, that is, the part of Eq. (1.1.3) that has to do with geometry.

Second, the relation between force and motion. How much torque must the motors deliver for the mechanism to move with the required velocities and accelerations? This is dynamics, Part III. The f in Eq. (1.1.3) must ultimately be supplied by dynamics.

Third, the design of the mechanism, actuation, electrics and sensing, Part IV.

Fourth, how to choose the command u so that the robot performs its task accurately and stably: control, Part V.

Fifth, how to infer the state x from noisy measurements y, how to plan motions and how to learn from data: sensing, estimation, planning and learning, spread over Parts V and VI.

Section 1.6 gives the road map of the whole book.

### 1.1.7 Everyday examples

A lift also has a complete sense–decide–act loop: it measures the position of the car and the calls from each floor, decides which floor to go to according to its scheduling program, drives the car with a motor, and slows down to level with the floor before arriving. Taken literally, it satisfies almost every item of Definition 1.1.1. A lift is usually not called a robot because its mechanism has only one motion along fixed guide rails, its task is a single one, and it never needs to be reprogrammed for another task.

A robot vacuum cleaner is a different case. Its mechanism is just as simple, only two drive wheels and a few brushes, but it has to find its own way in the different rooms of every household, get round tables and chairs, and remember where it has already cleaned; its decisions depend heavily on sensing. That is exactly what autonomy means. Looking at the two examples together, we can see that the difference between a robot and ordinary automated equipment lies mainly not in how complex the mechanism is, but in whether the decisions depend on sensing, and whether the machine can cope with situations that were not fully specified in advance.

**Engineer's notes**

- The classification of a device decides which safety standards apply. Industrial robots fall under ISO 10218-1/-2 (in China, the GB 11291 series); collaborative applications formerly also had to follow ISO/TS 15066, whose content the 2025 edition of ISO 10218 has taken in; personal care service robots fall under ISO 13482; surgical robots fall under the medical device standard IEC 80601-2-77. Settling the classification when a project starts avoids rework at acceptance.
- The protective field of a mobile robot's safety scanner has its length set by the stopping distance and is switched with the speed. When the speed, payload or floor conditions change, the deceleration changes, and the protective field has to be recalculated. Both terms of Eq. (1.1.4) must be computed: the reaction time is often underestimated, and it includes the sensor's refresh period, the program's computing time and the brake's response time.
- "Autonomous" is a matter of degree. When product literature says "fully autonomous", check it against the specific sensing abilities and the situations it can actually handle.
