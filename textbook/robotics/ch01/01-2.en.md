### 1.2.1 The problem

Very little of what goes into a collaborative robot arm today is a brand-new invention. Its motions are laid down by a program, and the program can be rewritten at any time; every one of its joints is under feedback control; its kinematics uses mathematics that took shape decades ago; its vision and grasping rely more and more on models learned from data. Each of these ideas has its own history, and some can be traced back more than two thousand years.

This section reviews the history of robots along four threads: automata and programs; feedback and control; industrial robots; and intelligence, mobility and learning. Figure 1.2.1 is a timeline organized by these four threads. Knowing this background helps us understand why today's robots are the way they are, and helps us judge which problems have been solved and which have not. At the end of the section, statistics published by the International Federation of Robotics (IFR) are used to show the scale and growth of robotics today.

### 1.2.2 Automata and "programs"

An **automaton** is a mechanical device that carries out a series of motions by itself in a predetermined order, without continuous operation by a person. Automata have a long history, and almost from the very beginning people looked for ways to make them "do different things".

In the 1st century AD, Hero of Alexandria described in his book *Automata* a small cart that moved by itself. Inside the cart a falling weight pulled on a rope wound round the axle and turned the wheels. The cleverness lay in the way the rope was wound: how many turns it made on the axle, where a length was left slack, and where it was looped round a wooden peg to wind in the opposite direction decided when the wheels turned, when they stopped and when they reversed. By changing the arrangement of rope and pegs, the cart could be made to follow different routes. Seen today, the rope and pegs were a program stored in the machine.

Ancient Chinese texts also record automata. The chapter "Tang Wen" of the *Liezi* tells of the craftsman Yan Shi, who presented King Mu of Zhou with a "performer" that could sing and dance; this is only a legend, but it reflects how people of old imagined artificial humans. What is firmly documented is the water-powered armillary-sphere and celestial-globe tower (an astronomical clock tower) built by Su Song and Han Gonglian in Bianjing in the third year of the Yuanyou era of the Northern Song dynasty (1088). It was driven by water: the steady flow from a clepsydra controlled the intermittent turning of the main driving wheel, which drove an armillary sphere, a celestial globe and the time-telling devices; in the time-telling section, wooden figures appeared on schedule to strike bells and beat drums. Su Song set down the whole design in *Xin Yixiang Fayao* (*New Design for an Astronomical Clock*), with detailed drawings.

In 1206 the Arab scholar al-Jazari completed his *Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices*, which records a great many water-powered automatic devices. Among them is an automatic band in which the rhythm struck by the drummers is set by pegs on a rotating drum; moving the pegs changes the rhythm. In eighteenth-century Europe, automata became the summit of precision mechanics: in 1739 the Frenchman Jacques de Vaucanson exhibited his "Digesting Duck", which could flap its wings and peck at food; around 1774 the Swiss Pierre Jaquet-Droz and his son completed humanoid automata such as "the Writer". The arm movements of "the Writer" are controlled by a set of cams, and by exchanging the letter cams on one wheel it can write different short sentences.

In 1804 the Frenchman Joseph Marie Jacquard invented the Jacquard loom, a figure-weaving loom controlled by punched cards. Each card corresponds to one row of the fabric, and whether or not there is a hole decides which warp threads are lifted. Changing the chain of cards weaves a different pattern, while the loom itself needs no alteration at all.

Putting these examples together, we can see a clear thread: the program gradually separated from the machine that executes it. On Hero's cart, the program was still the way the rope was wound on the axle; on "the Writer", the program was a set of exchangeable cams; on the Jacquard loom, the program was written entirely outside the machine, on cards. The meaning of "programmed" in Definition 1.1.1 is exactly this separation of program and machine: the same machine, given a different program, does a different job. All these automata, however, were open-loop: they moved in a predetermined order and paid no attention to what was happening around them.

### 1.2.3 Feedback and control

The idea of feedback is just as old. In the 3rd century BC, Ctesibius of Alexandria used a float to regulate the inflow of a water clock: when the water level rose, the float rose and blocked the inlet; when the level fell, the float dropped and let water in. In this way the water level stayed constant, the outflow was steady and the clock kept good time. This is one of the earliest recorded feedback regulators.

The most famous feedback device of the Industrial Revolution is the centrifugal governor. In 1788 James Watt applied it to the steam engine: two flyballs turned with the engine, and when the speed rose, centrifugal force swung the balls outwards and, through a linkage, partly closed the steam valve, so that the speed fell back. In operation the governor sometimes made the speed oscillate back and forth. In 1868 James Clerk Maxwell published *On Governors*, in which he analysed this phenomenon with differential equations and gave the conditions for the governor to work stably. This was the first systematic mathematical analysis of a feedback system and is regarded as the beginning of control theory. Chapter 42 starts from here.

In 1948 the American mathematician Norbert Wiener published *Cybernetics*, subtitled "or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine". **Cybernetics** studies living beings and machines in the same framework: both obtain information by sensing, and both regulate their own behaviour by feedback. The sense–decide–act loop of Figure 1.1.1 is a direct expression of this point of view. At almost the same time, in 1948–1949, the British neuroscientist W. Grey Walter built two "robot tortoises". Each tortoise had only one light-sensitive cell, one bump switch, two vacuum tubes and two motors, yet it showed behaviours such as moving towards light, avoiding obstacles and returning to its charging station. This showed that even when the decision rule π is extremely simple, as long as it depends on sensing, a machine can show behaviour that looks "purposeful".

### 1.2.4 The first industrial robot

In December 1954 the American inventor George Devol applied for a patent entitled *Programmed Article Transfer*, which was granted in 1961 (US Patent 2,988,237). The patent describes a mechanical arm that is first led through its motions by a person; it records the positions and afterwards repeats them automatically from the record. In 1956 Devol met the engineer Joseph Engelberger, and together they founded the company Unimation. In the spring of 1961 the first Unimate robot went into service at a General Motors plant in Ewing Township, New Jersey, taking hot castings out of a die-casting machine. The robot was hydraulically driven and weighed about 4,000 pounds (about 1.8 tonnes). Engelberger later came to be called "the father of robotics".

The way of working in Devol's patent has a name of its own.

**Definition 1.2.1 (Teach-and-playback)** **Teach-and-playback** is a programming method in which a person first guides the robot through the positions the task requires (teaching), the controller records them, and afterwards the robot repeats them automatically from the record (playback).

Teach-and-playback is simple and reliable, and to this day it remains the most common way of programming industrial robots (Chapter 51). From the point of view of the sense–decide–act loop, early teach-and-playback robots had feedback only at the joint level: every joint was controlled by a servo system that made sure it turned to the recorded angle; but at the task level the robot did not sense where the workpiece was. If the workpiece was placed out of position, it simply grasped thin air. So the success of early industrial robots depended largely on keeping the environment highly structured.

In 1968 Kawasaki Heavy Industries of Japan obtained a technology licence from Unimation and began producing industrial robots in Japan. For the next twenty years Japan was the largest producer and user of industrial robots.

### 1.2.5 Industrial robots come of age

It took industrial robots a dozen or more years to move from hydraulic to electric drives, and from special-purpose controllers to computer control. In 1969 Victor Scheinman of Stanford University designed the "Stanford Arm", which was driven entirely by electric motors and controlled by a computer; it is the direct forerunner of the modern robot arm. In 1973 the German company KUKA brought out FAMULUS, the first industrial robot with six axes driven by electric motors. In 1974 the Swedish company ASEA (one of the forerunners of today's ABB) brought out the IRB 6, generally regarded as the first all-electric, microprocessor-controlled commercial industrial robot. In 1978 Unimation, with the support of General Motors, brought out the PUMA arm, which for a long time afterwards was the standard research platform of university laboratories. In the same year Hiroshi Makino of the University of Yamanashi in Japan proposed the SCARA structure: two joints turning about vertical axes move the end-effector in a horizontal plane, stiff in the vertical direction and compliant in the horizontal, which makes it especially suitable for inserting electronic components (Section 1.3).

The mathematics of robot arms also took shape in this period. In 1955 Jacques Denavit and Richard S. Hartenberg proposed a method of describing the relation between neighbouring links by four parameters, which later became the standard way of describing robot arms in industry, the DH parameter method (Chapter 13). In 1984 Roger W. Brockett proposed expressing the forward kinematics of a robot arm by the product-of-exponentials formula. It rests on the screw theory of the 19th century and regards each joint as a screw axis (Chapters 6 and 12). This book takes screws and the product of exponentials as its main line; the reasons are explained in Section 1.6.

### 1.2.6 Intelligence, mobility and learning

While industrial robots were entering factories, researchers began to try to make robots understand their environment and decide what to do by themselves. From 1966 to 1972 the Stanford Research Institute developed the mobile robot Shakey. It sensed its room with a camera and a range finder and planned its actions by logical reasoning, for example "push that block to the doorway". The A* search algorithm invented for Shakey is still a basic method of path planning today (Chapter 53). Shakey, however, had to think for a long time before every step, and could only work in simple, specially arranged rooms.

In 1973 WABOT-1, developed under Ichiro Kato at Waseda University, appeared. It could walk on two legs, grasp objects with its hands and hold simple conversations in Japanese; it was the first full-scale humanoid robot.

In the 1980s researchers reconsidered Shakey's approach of "first build a complete model of the world, then reason". In 1986 Rodney A. Brooks proposed the subsumption architecture: instead of building a complete world model, the robot's behaviour is divided into several layers of simple sense-act loops, the lower layers handling reflexes such as obstacle avoidance and the higher layers wandering and exploring, with the higher layers able to suppress the lower ones. Six-legged robots built on this idea could walk nimbly over rough ground. In a 1991 paper Brooks argued further that intelligence cannot be separated from the real-time interaction of a body with its environment. This view later came to be called the embodied view, and it is one of the intellectual sources of today's term "embodied intelligence".

Over the next thirty years robots left the factory. In 1996 Honda showed the P2, a humanoid robot that could walk autonomously and go up and down stairs, followed by ASIMO in 2000. In 1997 the American rover Sojourner worked on the surface of Mars for nearly three months. In 2000 the da Vinci surgical system was cleared by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and surgical robots entered hospitals. In 2002 iRobot launched the Roomba robot vacuum cleaner, and robots entered the home. In 2005 Stanford University's driverless car Stanley won the DARPA Grand Challenge, a desert race for driverless vehicles held by the US Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency, a full demonstration of the power of probabilistic methods in perception and localization (Chapters 46 and 47). From 2007 on, the company Willow Garage, building on early work at the Stanford Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, developed the open-source Robot Operating System (ROS), which gradually became the common software platform of research and industry (Chapter 52).

The relation between robots and people was changing too. Traditional industrial robots are strong and fast and must be separated from people by fences. In 2008 the Danish company Universal Robots sold its first UR5. It was light, easy to program and able to detect collisions, and it could work with people at the same workstation without a fence; robots of this kind later came to be called **collaborative robots**. The UR5e, one of the protagonists of this book, is the successor of the UR5 in the e-Series launched in 2018.

Logistics was another turning point. In 2012 Amazon bought Kiva Systems for 775 million US dollars. Kiva's carts slip under a shelf and carry the whole shelf to the picker, so that "the person goes to the goods" became "the goods come to the person". By July 2025 Amazon announced that more than one million robots had been deployed across its operations network.

Also in 2012, deep neural networks pulled far ahead of traditional methods in an image-recognition competition. From then on deep learning moved quickly into robot visual perception, and later into the learning of motor skills such as grasping and walking (Chapters 55 to 57).

### 1.2.7 Embodied intelligence

**Embodied intelligence** is intelligence that senses, decides and acts through the interaction of a body with the physical world; it stresses that intelligence cannot exist apart from a body and an environment. Its ideas can be traced back to cybernetics and the work of Brooks; its rise as a technical direction, however, is directly linked to the large-scale neural network models of recent years.

As of 2026, the representative advances in this direction fall roughly into two kinds. One is reinforcement learning in large-scale simulation: thousands upon thousands of robots are simulated in a computer at the same time, made to learn walking, running, jumping and balancing by repeated trial and error, and the learned policy is then transferred to the real machine (Chapter 56). The Go2 quadruped robot released by Unitree in 2023 is a typical beneficiary of this kind of method, and it is also one of the robots that run through this book. The other kind is vision-language-action models: models trained on internet-scale image and text data are trained further on robot demonstration data, so that they can understand instructions such as "put the red cup on the plate" and output robot actions directly. RT-2, published by Google DeepMind in 2023, is an early representative of this kind of model; from 2024 on, Physical Intelligence's π0, Figure's Helix, Google DeepMind's Gemini Robotics and others have followed one after another (Chapters 58 and 59). This field changes very quickly, and the relevant chapters of this book are updated before each new edition.

From the point of view of the sense–decide–act loop, embodied intelligence has not changed the structure of the loop; what it has changed is where the decision rule π comes from: instead of being written rule by rule by people, it is learned from data. The history reviewed above can also be summed up with this loop: automata supplied the program, feedback control closed the loop, industrial robots made the loop precise and reliable, and intelligence and learning keep widening the range of situations the loop can cope with.

### 1.2.8 The scale in numbers

The scale of robotics is usually measured by the *World Robotics* report published every year by the IFR. The report distinguishes two quantities: annual **installations**, the number of industrial robots newly put into service in the year; and the **operational stock**, the total number of industrial robots still in operation. When estimating the operational stock, the IFR assumes an average service life of 12 years for an industrial robot.

According to the report published in September 2025, {{ex1_2.inst24_s}} industrial robots were installed worldwide in 2024, "more than double the number ten years ago"; the operational stock at the end of the year was {{ex1_2.stock24_s}} units. According to the report published in September 2026, installations in 2025 grew by 11% to more than 600,000 units, and the operational stock at the end of the year grew by 9% to a record of about 5 million units. How much growth per year does "doubling in ten years" amount to? Answering this needs a simple formula for compound growth. Suppose a quantity starts at $N_0$ and every year grows by the same proportion r of the year before (r is called the annual growth rate and is written as a decimal). Then after one year it is $N_0(1+r)$, after two years $N_0(1+r)^2$, and after t years

$$
N_t = N_0\,(1+r)^t . \tag{1.2.1}
$$

Given $N_0$, $N_t$ and t, we can solve for r in reverse: dividing both sides by $N_0$ and taking the t-th root gives

$$
r = \left(\frac{N_t}{N_0}\right)^{1/t} - 1 . \tag{1.2.2}
$$

The r computed from Eq. (1.2.2) is called the **compound annual growth rate**. It means: if the quantity grew by this proportion every year, after t years it would go from $N_0$ to exactly $N_t$.

**Example 1.2.1** For "doubling in ten years", $N_t/N_0 = 2$ and t = 10, so by Eq. (1.2.2), $r = 2^{1/10} - 1 = {{ex1_2.r10_pct:.2f}}\%$. Beginners often spread the 100% of doubling evenly over ten years and get 10% a year. This is wrong: 10% a year compounded for ten years gives $1.1^{10} = {{ex1_2.x_wrong:.2f}}$ times, not 2 times. The reason is that each year's growth is based on the number already grown in the year before.

The IFR forecasts {{ex1_2.fc26_s}} installations in 2026 and {{ex1_2.fc29_s}} in 2029. By Eq. (1.2.2), the compound annual growth rate over these three years is {{ex1_2.r_fc_pct:.2f}}%, roughly the same as the growth over the past ten years. Program 1.2.1 computes r in two ways: by taking the root directly, and by first taking logarithms, $\ln(1+r) = \ln(N_t/N_0)/t$, and then converting back. The two agree; and compounding the computed r year by year for t years, as in Eq. (1.2.1), returns exactly $N_t$.

**Example 1.2.2** China's share of the industrial robots installed worldwide. In 2024 China installed {{ex1_2.cn24_s}} units, {{ex1_2.share24_pct:.1f}}% of the world total of {{ex1_2.inst24_s}}. For 2025 the report gives only a world figure of "more than 600,000 units, up 11%". Estimating from the 2024 number plus 11%, the world total for 2025 is about {{ex1_2.inst25_s}} units; China installed {{ex1_2.cn25_s}} units, {{ex1_2.share25_pct:.1f}}% of the total. The five countries with the most installations together account for about {{ex1_2.top5_25_pct:.0f}}% of the world total.

### 1.2.9 An everyday example

The music box is the "program-controlled" automaton easiest to find today. Raised pins are arranged on a cylinder; as the cylinder turns, the pins pluck tuned teeth of different lengths one after another and play a tune. The arrangement of the pins is the program: change the cylinder and you change the tune, while the rest of the music box stays the same. This is the same principle as al-Jazari's drummers and the Jacquard loom.

But a music box is not a robot. It pays no attention to its surroundings: whether someone beside it claps, or tilts it, it plays on at the same tempo. Set beside a robot vacuum cleaner, it illustrates exactly the open-loop/closed-loop contrast of Section 1.1. The main line of robot history runs from "acting according to a program", like the music box, to "acting while watching the environment", like the robot vacuum cleaner, and on to "learning to act in environments never seen before", which embodied intelligence pursues.

**Engineer's notes**

- Teach-and-playback is still the most common programming method in factories because it does not require the engineer to work out kinematics, nor does it depend on an accurate model. Its limitation comes from the same place: whenever the position of the workpiece changes, the robot has to be taught again. When introducing vision-based positioning or offline programming, check at the same time whether the absolute (pose) accuracy is good enough (Section 1.6, Chapter 26).
- When quoting market data, state the source and the date of publication, and distinguish installations from operational stock, and industrial robots from service robots. The IFR updates its data around September each year, and the figures for the previous year are sometimes revised.
- New technology often takes many years to go from demonstration to mass production. When evaluating a new method, look at what kind of environment it was tested in, how many trials were run, and with what success rate it completed which task.
