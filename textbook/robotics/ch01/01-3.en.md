### 1.3.1 The problem

The digital factory wants to raise its output. The engineers have listed several places where they would like to use robots: loading and unloading a CNC lathe, with parts of a few kilograms each and a steady cycle time required; moving parts between a dozen or so machines, over distances of several tens of metres; pressing in bearings, where a worker and a robot must work together at the same station; and night-time inspection, which means moving along the aisles and between the machines, reading gauges and measuring temperatures. Every one of these places needs a "robot", but clearly not the same kind of robot.

Faced with so many kinds of robot, we first need a framework for classifying them. Robots can be classified by application, by mechanical structure, or by whether and how they move. Each way of dividing them captures only one aspect of a robot, much as projecting a solid object onto a plane shows only one view of it. This section first classifies robots by application, then by structure and mode of locomotion (Figure 1.3.1), and then introduces in turn the classes listed in the syllabus: industrial arms, collaborative arms, and mobile, legged, humanoid, aerial, underwater, medical and service robots. The eight robot models in the WenQuest parts library belong to different classes among these, and the reader can look at them one by one in Lab 1.3.

### 1.3.2 Classification by application

Section 1.1 has already given the three classes of ISO 8373:2021: industrial robots, service robots and medical robots (Definitions 1.1.3 and 1.1.4). Service robots are further divided into two kinds by who uses them: those for personal use, such as robot vacuums, robot lawn mowers and educational robots, which are usually operated by people without specialist knowledge; and those for professional use, such as logistics, cleaning, inspection and agricultural robots, which are operated by trained people in a commercial setting.

The main point of classifying by application is regulation. The three classes come under different safety standards and follow different certification routes to the market, and the IFR keeps its statistics separately for each of them. The same structure can belong to different classes: a six-axis robot arm on a car body-welding line is an industrial robot, while the same arm in a hospital, giving patients rehabilitation exercise, may be a medical robot. So classification by application cannot tell us what a robot "looks like"; for that we need classification by structure.

### 1.3.3 Fixed-base manipulators

The base of a manipulator (robot arm) is fixed to the floor or to a workbench, and the tool at its end is used to change the position and orientation of objects. By the way the links are connected, manipulators fall into two broad classes, serial and parallel.

In a **serial robot** the links follow one after another, and there is only one path from the base to the end of the arm, as in a human arm. When an earlier joint turns, all the links after it move with it. Its advantages are a large reach and a simple structure; its drawbacks are that the motors and links further out must all be carried by the joints before them, and that the errors of the individual joints add up at the end of the arm.

In a **parallel robot** the end platform is connected to the base through several chains at the same time, like several people carrying a table together. Each chain carries only part of the load, so the robot is stiff, its moving parts are light and it is fast; the price is a small reach and a more complicated kinematic analysis (Chapter 18).

Serial robots come in several common structures, depending on the types and arrangement of their joints.

An **articulated robot** has only revolute joints, usually six, and is the most numerous kind of industrial robot today. The UR5e (six joints) and the Panda (seven joints) in this book both belong to this class. Six joints is the least number that lets the end-effector take any position and orientation within the workspace (Section 1.4).

The SCARA robot was proposed in 1978 by Hiroshi Makino, mentioned in Section 1.2. It has two joints turning about vertical axes, which move the end-effector in a horizontal plane; a vertical sliding joint that raises and lowers the end-effector; and one more rotation of the end-effector about a vertical axis. Because all three rotation axes are vertical, it is very stiff in the vertical direction and can take the downward force of an insertion, while it has some compliance in the horizontal direction, which helps when fitting a part into a hole. SCARA robots are used in large numbers for electronics assembly and pick-and-place work.

A **Cartesian robot** consists of three mutually perpendicular prismatic joints, and its end-effector moves in straight lines along x, y and z. A large Cartesian robot mounted above the machines is called a gantry robot, and is often used for loading and unloading machine tools and for carrying large workpieces. Its kinematics is the simplest of all and its accuracy is easy to guarantee, but it takes up a lot of space.

The most common parallel robot is the Delta robot. It was proposed by R. Clavel of the École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne in Switzerland in the 1980s: all three motors are mounted on the fixed upper platform, and each of the three chains contains a parallelogram, so that the moving platform below can only translate, not rotate. Because its moving parts are extremely light, a Delta robot on a food or pharmaceutical packaging line can make more than a hundred picks per minute.

Different structures can reach regions of entirely different shapes. The region formed by all the positions that the end-effector of a robot arm (or a chosen point on it) can reach is called its **workspace**. Chapter 11 treats the workspace rigorously; here an example gives an intuitive comparison of two structures.

**Example 1.3.1** The SCARA model in the parts library has two horizontal arms of lengths $a_1 = {{ex1_3.a1:.2f}}$ m and $a_2 = {{ex1_3.a2:.2f}}$ m; joint 1 can turn through ±{{ex1_3.t1_deg:.0f}}° and joint 2 through ±{{ex1_3.t2_deg:.0f}}°; the vertical stroke is {{ex1_3.stroke:.2f}} m. Find the area the end-effector can reach in the horizontal plane, and the volume of the workspace.

First consider the values that the distance r from the end-effector to the axis of joint 1 can take. r is largest when the two arms are stretched out straight: $r_{\max} = a_1 + a_2 = {{ex1_3.r_max:.1f}}$ m. When joint 2 turns through $\theta_2$, the two arms and r form a triangle, and by the law of cosines

$$
r^2 = a_1^2 + a_2^2 + 2a_1a_2\cos\theta_2 . \tag{1.3.1}
$$

(The angle between the two arms is $180^\circ - \theta_2$, and $\cos(180^\circ - \theta_2) = -\cos\theta_2$.) The larger $\theta_2$, the smaller r. Joint 2 can turn at most to {{ex1_3.t2_deg:.0f}}°, and substituting this into Eq. (1.3.1) gives $r_{\min} = {{ex1_3.r_min:.3f}}$ m. If joint 1 could turn through a full circle, the region the end-effector can reach would be an annulus, of area

$$
A_{\text{annulus}} = \pi\,(r_{\max}^2 - r_{\min}^2) , \tag{1.3.2}
$$

equal to {{ex1_3.annulus:.3f}} m². But joint 1 can only turn through ±{{ex1_3.t1_deg:.0f}}°, so a piece is missing from the back of the annulus, and the shape of the gap has to be worked out. Program 1.3.1 computes this area in two ways: first, it divides the plane into small squares 5 mm on a side and, for each grid point, uses the inverse solution of the planar two-link arm to decide whether there is a set of joint angles within the limits; second, it draws {{ex1_3.N}} random sets of joint angles, computes the end-effector position by forward kinematics, and counts the squares that are hit. The two methods give {{ex1_3.area_grid:.3f}} m² and {{ex1_3.area_mc:.3f}} m², which differ by {{ex1_3.area_diff_pct:.1f}}% (the second is slightly larger because a point landing near the edge of a square counts the whole square as reachable). Multiplying by the vertical stroke, the volume of the workspace is about {{ex1_3.vol_sc:.3f}} m³.

For comparison, the manufacturer's reach of the UR5e is 0.85 m and every joint can turn through ±360°, so its workspace is roughly a sphere of radius 0.85 m, with a volume of about {{ex1_3.vol_ur:.2f}} m³, some {{ex1_3.vol_ratio:.0f}} times that of the SCARA (Figure 1.3.2). But bigger is not always better: the workspace of the SCARA covers exactly one workbench, and its stiffness in the vertical direction is hard for an articulated robot to match. When choosing a robot, look at what kind of workspace the task needs.

A good share of the sample points in the figure lies outside the circle of radius 0.85 m centred at the shoulder: Program 1.3.1 draws {{ex1_3.M}} random sets of joint angles, and in {{ex1_3.out_pct:.0f}}% of them the flange centre is more than 0.85 m from the shoulder, reaching as far as {{ex1_3.d_sh_max:.2f}} m. Those are the poses in which the wrist segments also stretch out along the direction of the arm. So the manufacturer's "reach" is a reference value given for an agreed reference point and pose (Example 1.6.1 in Section 1.6 will check where it comes from), not the farthest distance the flange centre can reach.

### 1.3.4 Collaborative robots

Conventional industrial robots are fast and strong, and a person who steps into their working range is in danger, so they must be kept apart by safety fences, and the robot stops when someone opens the gate. This is no problem for high-volume car production, but it is awkward for high-mix, low-volume production: many operations need people and robots to work in turn, or even at the same time, the fences take up floor space, and stopping the robot spoils the cycle time.

A **collaborative robot** is a robot designed to work directly with people in a shared workspace. Its design goal is that even if it does come into contact with a person, it causes no injury. ISO 10218 (2011 edition) and ISO/TS 15066:2016 lay down four modes of human-robot collaborative operation: safety-rated monitored stop, in which the robot halts without losing power when a person enters the collaborative area; hand guiding, in which a person moves the robot by pulling it directly by hand; speed and separation monitoring, in which the robot slows down as a person comes closer and stops when the person is within a certain distance; and **power and force limiting**, in which the speed, force and power of the robot are kept within set limits, so that if it does strike a person, the contact force does not exceed the limits laid down in the standard.

To limit power and force, the robot must be able to sense a collision. The UR5e has a six-axis force/torque sensor built into its end flange and estimates external forces from the currents in its joints; the Panda has torque sensors in all seven of its joints. As soon as the measured force exceeds what is expected, the controller stops the robot within a few milliseconds or lets it yield to the external force. This is yet another sense–decide–act loop, only its goal is safety.

It must be stressed that "collaboration" is a way of using a robot, not just a property of the robot itself. The 2025 revision of ISO 10218 takes the content of ISO/TS 15066 into the body of the standard, no longer speaks of "collaborative robots" or "collaborative operation" but of "collaborative applications", and renames "safety-rated monitored stop" as "monitored standstill", precisely because only a specific application can be designed, tested and validated as collaborative. If the same UR5e carries a sharp cutting tool at its end, or is handling hot castings, people cannot work close to it. Whether it is safe has to be decided by a risk assessment of the whole application. The safety of **human-robot collaboration** is the subject of Chapter 41.

### 1.3.5 Mobile robots

A **mobile robot** is a robot able to travel under its own control (ISO 8373:2021). Compared with a fixed-base manipulator, it has one more question to answer: where am I? By mode of locomotion, the common kinds are wheeled, legged, aerial and underwater.

Wheeled robots are simple and efficient, and are the most widely used on flat floors. Wheeled robots in factories and warehouses are of two kinds. An **automated guided vehicle** (AGV) runs along a guide path laid down in advance, such as magnetic tape, QR codes or reflectors on the floor; changing the route means laying the path again. An **autonomous mobile robot** (AMR) instead uses lidar and cameras to perceive its surroundings, locates itself in a map, plans its own route and steers round obstacles. The difference between the two is precisely a difference in the degree of autonomy. One of the book's recurring robots is the differential-drive cart in the digital factory: its left and right wheels are each driven by a motor, and a caster wheel at the back only gives support.

Legged robots walk on legs and can cross steps, rubble and ditches, which wheels cannot do. A **quadruped robot** has four legs; it is stable when standing still, and in motion it can keep its balance dynamically with gaits such as the trot. In recent years its use in inspection, rescue and research has grown rapidly. The Go2, one of the book's recurring robots, is a quadruped robot with three joints per leg.

The most common **aerial robot** is the multirotor drone, and one with four rotors is called a **quadrotor**. It controls its attitude and position by adjusting the speed of each rotor, which changes the lift and the reaction torque (Chapters 21 and 49). One of the book's recurring robots is a model of the Skydio X2 quadrotor.

**Underwater robots** are of two kinds: a remotely operated vehicle (ROV) is controlled through a cable by a person at the surface, and is used in offshore engineering and salvage; an autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV) has no cable and travels on its own according to a planned mission, and is used for seabed mapping and scientific survey. Under water there is no satellite positioning, communication is difficult, and currents push the robot about; all of this makes localization and control very challenging. Following the syllabus, this book does not give underwater robots a chapter of their own; the relevant principles are spread over the chapters on estimation and control.

Mounting a robot arm on a mobile base gives a **mobile manipulator**, which can both move and manipulate, and is an important direction in logistics and household service.

Different modes of locomotion give robots different motion properties. Here we point out two phenomena that will come up again and again in Section 1.4 and in later chapters. A wheeled cart needs three numbers to describe its position and heading on the floor, yet it has only two driven wheels; it cannot move sideways directly, and to shift sideways it must first turn. This restriction is called a nonholonomic constraint (Chapter 19). A quadrotor needs six numbers for its position and attitude in the air, yet it has only four rotors; to fly forwards it must first tilt its body forwards. Having fewer actuators than degrees of freedom in this way is called underactuation (Chapter 21).

### 1.3.6 Humanoid robots

A **humanoid robot** has a body structure like a person's: a head, a trunk, two arms and two legs. The main reason for building a robot in human form is that human environments are designed for the human body: the height of stairs, the position of door handles and the shape of tools all suit the human build and human hands. A robot of human form can, in principle, go straight into such environments and use tools designed for people.

The difficulties of humanoid robots are just as obvious. Standing on two legs, the support area is small and balance needs continuous control; there are dozens of joints throughout the body whose motions must be coordinated; and dexterous manipulation with two hands is a problem still far from solved. WABOT-1, P2 and ASIMO, mentioned in Section 1.2, represent the early explorations. In recent years, driven by large-scale training in simulation and by vision-language-action models, many companies in China and abroad have brought out humanoid robots (Chapters 58 and 59).

The parts library contains a model of the Unitree G1 humanoid robot. Its joint table lists {{ex1_4.g1_n}} joints, while the manufacturer's datasheet gives {{ex1_4.g1_dof_sheet}} degrees of freedom for the basic version; the education version can add joints in the waist, the wrists and dexterous hands. This is a reminder that one product name may cover different configurations, and when reading a datasheet one must be clear which version it describes.

### 1.3.7 Medical and service robots

The most mature medical robots are surgical robots. Take the da Vinci surgical system: the surgeon works two hand controllers at a console, and a few metres away, at the patient's side, robot arms carry long slender instruments through a few small incisions into the body to carry out the operation. The arms make sure that each instrument turns only about a fixed point at the incision, called the remote centre of motion, so that the tissue at the incision is not torn. There are also rehabilitation robots, exoskeletons, orthopaedic and neurosurgical robots that assist with positioning, and robots that deliver medicines inside hospitals. Medical robots must meet the regulations for medical devices, and their development and certification take a long time; this is the biggest difference between them and other robots.

Service robots are the most varied of all. Among professional service robots, the most numerous are transport and logistics robots; there are also cleaning, hospitality, agricultural, and inspection and security robots (Figure 1.5.2 in Section 1.5). Among personal service robots, the most numerous are robot vacuums, followed by robot lawn mowers and various educational and entertainment robots.

### 1.3.9 An everyday example

The classification of vehicles is much like the classification of robots. By power source, vehicles can be divided into human-powered, combustion-engined and electric; by where they move, into land, water and air; by use, into passenger, goods and special-purpose vehicles. An electric city bus is "electric", "land" and "passenger" in the three schemes respectively. No one scheme is the only correct one; each answers one question: what energy must be supplied to it, what kind of place it must travel in, and which regulations govern its safety.

So it is with robots. Classification by application answers "which standards apply to it, and in which statistics is it counted"; classification by structure answers "where can it reach, and how stiff is it"; classification by mode of locomotion answers "how does it find out where it is". When engineers discuss a robot, they should be clear along which dimension they are talking about it.

**Engineer's notes**

- Start a robot selection from the task: payload (including the gripper), workspace, cycle time, accuracy and environment (cleanroom, explosion-proof, waterproof); only then look at the type of structure. For the same payload, SCARA and Delta robots are often faster than six-axis arms at planar pick-and-place, while six-axis arms have the advantage in flexibility of orientation.
- A collaborative robot does not mean "no safety measures needed". Do a risk assessment according to ISO/TS 15066: whether the end-effector is sharp, whether the workpiece is heavy or hot, and where a person could be trapped between the robot and a fixed object all change the conclusion.
- The choice between AGV and AMR depends on whether the routes change often. Where the routes are fixed and high accuracy is required, an AGV is simple and reliable; where the layout changes often and people and vehicles share the floor, an AMR is more flexible, but building and maintaining the map takes effort.
- When reading a manufacturer's datasheet, first confirm the version and configuration (such as the basic and education versions of the G1), and the test conditions of each figure (such as the reference point for the reach, or the distance at which the payload is rated).
