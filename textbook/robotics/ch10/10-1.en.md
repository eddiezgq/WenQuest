### 10.1.1 The problem

In the workshop of the WenQuest digital factory, an AGV carrying a tote drives along an aisle, with a lidar mounted at the front to detect obstacles in the aisle. At a certain instant the AGV is turning left at 0.5 m/s on a circular arc of radius 2.0 m; on the floor beside the aisle lies a part at rest. The lidar reports that the part is circling a point to the left of the vehicle body at a speed of 0.625 m/s.

The part plainly does not move at all, so why does the lidar say it is moving? Is the lidar wrong, or does the word "motion" itself need a more careful explanation?

The lidar is not wrong. What it reports is the position of the part relative to the AGV, and the AGV itself is turning. "The part is at rest" and "the part is circling" describe the same thing, only with different bodies taken as the standard. In robot systems such situations are everywhere: a robot arm mounted on a mobile base sees the position of a workpiece change as the base moves; a camera mounted on a turntable sees the workshop rotate; the inertial measurement unit of a drone measures the acceleration of the airframe itself, whereas navigation needs the motion relative to the ground. Unless we first say clearly "relative to what", the values of velocity and acceleration have no definite meaning, and programs end up with errors such as using a velocity relative to the AGV as if it were relative to the ground.

This section explains what kinematics studies, distinguishes the two concepts of reference frame and coordinate system, which are often confused, and gives the basic language for describing the motion of a point: the equation of motion and the trajectory. At the end of the section we answer the opening question and compute the trajectory and the speed of the part as seen from the AGV.

### 10.1.2 What kinematics studies

The science of the motion of bodies falls into two parts, according to whether it asks about the causes of motion. The study of the geometric properties of motion alone, that is, where a body is at what time, how fast its position changes and how this rate of change itself changes, without asking which forces cause these changes, is called **kinematics**. The study of the relation between force and motion is called dynamics, and is discussed in this book from Chapter 23 on. The word "kinematics" (French *cinématique*, from the Greek κίνημα, meaning motion) was coined by André-Marie Ampère in his *Essai sur la philosophie des sciences* (Essay on the philosophy of the sciences), published in 1834. In classifying the whole of science, he proposed that the study of motion without reference to force should be set apart as a discipline of its own, as the foundation of the mechanics of machines. After that, the discipline developed rapidly in the theory of mechanisms and became the main tool for analysing linkages, gears, cams and other mechanisms; it is precisely this tradition that robotics has inherited.

The most basic object of study in kinematics is the point. What actually moves in a robot is bodies of finite size, but in many problems we care only about the motion of one point of a body: which route the tool centre point (Section 2.5) follows, when the centre of the AGV's body reaches a workstation, where the target point at which a camera's line of sight is aimed lies. Taking such a point out of the body and studying it on its own is the kinematics of a point. The point whose motion is being studied is called the **moving point**. The rotation of the body itself is described from Section 10.7 and in later chapters, by several points or by a frame attached to the body.

To describe the motion of a point we must answer three questions: where it is, that is, its **position**; how fast and in which direction its position is changing, that is, its **velocity**; and how fast and in which direction the velocity itself is changing, that is, its **acceleration**. All three quantities are vectors. Chapter 3 stressed that a vector itself does not depend on the coordinate system; the coordinate system only serves to read off its components. But before answering "where", there is a more fundamental question: "where" relative to what?

### 10.1.3 Reference frames

To say that a body is moving is always to say so relative to another body. To say that the part is at rest is to say that it does not move relative to the workshop floor; to say that the lidar sees the part circling is to say that the part circles relative to the body of the AGV. The body taken as the standard, which we agree to regard as being at rest, is the reference frame.

**Definition 10.1.1 (Reference frame)** A rigid body, or a set of bodies at rest relative to one another, that is agreed to be at rest and is used to describe the motion of other bodies, together with all the points rigidly attached to it and extending with it throughout space, is called a **reference frame**. To describe motion, it must also be provided with a clock to fix the instants of time.

The phrase "extending throughout space" in the definition needs explaining. The workshop floor is only a plane, but when we say "a point 1 m above the floor is at rest relative to the floor", we mean that the distances from this point to the points of the floor stay constant. A reference frame is therefore not only the body itself but all the points of space whose distances to it remain fixed. A reference frame is a physical concept: which body we choose as the reference frame depends on the problem we want to study, and the same motion may look completely different in different reference frames.

In kinematics the choice of reference frame is free; any body can serve as a reference frame. Not so in dynamics. Newton's laws of motion hold only in a special class of reference frames, called **inertial reference frames**. In his *Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy), published in 1687, Isaac Newton took "absolute space" and "absolute time" as the background of the laws of motion, and stated in Corollary V of the laws that the relative motions of the bodies within a system are the same whether the system is at rest or moves uniformly in a straight line. This conclusion had been made clear earlier by Galileo Galilei with the example of a ship's cabin: in his *Dialogo sopra i due massimi sistemi del mondo* (Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems) of 1632, he described a large ship sailing uniformly, in whose cabin flying insects, falling drops of water and thrown objects move exactly as when the ship lies at anchor, so that a person in the cabin cannot tell from these phenomena whether the ship is moving. This is the **Galilean principle of relativity**. The ground is not strictly an inertial reference frame, because the Earth rotates; in Section 10.6 the theorem of composition of accelerations will show that for a robot in a workshop the effect of the Earth's rotation is a few millionths of the gravitational acceleration, negligible in the vast majority of engineering problems. This book takes the workshop floor as an inertial reference frame unless stated otherwise.

In classical mechanics time is the same in all reference frames: whether two events happen at the same time, and how long apart they are, does not depend on the reference frame from which they are observed. This book discusses only this case. Robots move far more slowly than light, and relativistic corrections are far smaller than the resolution of any sensor.

### 10.1.4 Coordinate systems

Once a reference frame has been chosen, we still have to express the position of a point by numbers before we can calculate. The rule that assigns a set of numbers to each point of a reference frame is a coordinate system.

**Definition 10.1.2 (Coordinate system)** A rule that sets up a one-to-one correspondence between the points of space (or the points of a region of it) and ordered triples of numbers $(q_1, q_2, q_3)$ in a reference frame is called a **coordinate system** in that reference frame; $q_1$, $q_2$, $q_3$ are called the coordinates of the point.

The coordinate frame defined in Section 5.1 consists of an origin and three mutually perpendicular unit vectors forming a right-handed system (Definition 5.1.1), and the coordinates of a point are the components of its position vector along the three axes. This is the most common and the simplest kind of coordinate system in the sense of Definition 10.1.2, and its coordinates are called **Cartesian coordinates**. A Cartesian coordinate system has a special feature: it is itself a "scaffold" attached to the reference frame, with an origin and axes, which can be used to read off the components of any vector. In English such a scaffold is called a frame, so the word "frame" often means both the reference frame and the Cartesian coordinate system attached to it; the corresponding Chinese term is used in the same two ways. Where no confusion can arise this book follows the usual way of speaking: for example, "the AGV frame {b}" means both the AGV as a reference frame and the Cartesian coordinate system attached to the AGV; where the two must be distinguished, we say explicitly "reference frame" or "coordinates".

Definition 10.1.2 is much wider than Cartesian coordinates. For example, a point in the plane can also be numbered by its distance ρ from the origin and its angle φ from the x-axis, which gives polar coordinates; points in space can be numbered by cylindrical or spherical coordinates, which Sections 10.3 and 10.4 discuss in detail. The "coordinate lines" of these coordinates (the lines traced by a point when only one coordinate varies) are curves, and such coordinates are collectively called **curvilinear coordinates**. Curvilinear coordinates do not form a fixed scaffold: in polar coordinates "the direction in which ρ increases" points in different directions at different points. For this very reason, finding velocities and accelerations in curvilinear coordinates is more complicated than in Cartesian coordinates, and also more interesting.

Figure 10.1.1 shows three coordinate systems in the same reference frame. The reference frame is the workshop floor; the point P is a part at rest on the floor. The Cartesian frame {s} reads (x, y) = (2.0, 0.5) m; the polar coordinates with the origin of {s} as pole read ρ = {{ex10_1.rho:.4f}} m, φ = {{ex10_1.phi_deg:.2f}}°; another Cartesian frame {w}, with its origin in another corner of the workshop and its axes pointing in other directions, reads a third set of numbers. The three readings all differ, but they describe the same point; and because all three coordinate systems are attached to the floor, none of the readings changes with time, and the part is at rest in all three coordinate systems.

This simple observation can be written as a general conclusion. Let {a} and {b} be two Cartesian frames; the coordinates of a point P in the two frames satisfy Eq. (5.1.2):

$$
p_a = R_{ab}\,p_b + p_{ab} , \tag{10.1.1}
$$

where $R_{ab}$ is the orientation of {b} relative to {a} and $p_{ab}$ is the position of the origin of {b} in {a}. If {a} and {b} are attached to the same reference frame, $R_{ab}$ and $p_{ab}$ are both constant, and differentiating with respect to time gives

$$
\dot p_a = R_{ab}\,\dot p_b . \tag{10.1.2}
$$

By Eq. (5.1.4), this is exactly the relation between the components of one and the same free vector in two frames. In other words, the velocity found in {a} and the velocity found in {b} are the same vector, only with different components. If {b} moves relative to {a}, $R_{ab}$ and $p_{ab}$ change with time, and differentiation produces two more terms:

$$
\dot p_a = R_{ab}\,\dot p_b + \dot R_{ab}\,p_b + \dot p_{ab} . \tag{10.1.3}
$$

The last two terms come from the motion of the frame itself. Now $\dot p_a$ and $R_{ab}\dot p_b$ are no longer the same vector: the velocities the two frames "see" really are different.

**Theorem 10.1.1** Any two coordinate systems attached to the same reference frame give the same velocity vector and the same acceleration vector; coordinate systems attached to two reference frames in relative motion in general give different velocity vectors and acceleration vectors. In other words, velocity and acceleration depend on the reference frame, but not on the choice of coordinate system within the same reference frame.

*Proof*　The first half: by Eq. (10.1.2), $\dot p_a$ and $\dot p_b$ are the components of the same vector in {a} and {b}; differentiating once more, $\ddot p_a = R_{ab}\ddot p_b$, and the same holds for the acceleration. For curvilinear coordinates, it suffices to convert them into the coordinates of some Cartesian frame in the same reference frame before differentiating, and the conclusion is the same. The second half: by Eq. (10.1.3), as long as $\dot R_{ab}$ or $\dot p_{ab}$ is non-zero, there are points for which $\dot R_{ab}p_b + \dot p_{ab} \neq 0$, and the two give different velocities; the example of Section 10.1.6 is a case in point.　∎

Theorem 10.1.1 can be summed up in one sentence: **changing the coordinate system changes only the readings; changing the reference frame changes the motion**. Beginners often confuse the two, for example by treating "differentiating in the camera frame" as "writing the components another way" and forgetting that the camera itself is moving. Section 10.6 will expand the last two terms of Eq. (10.1.3) systematically and obtain the theorems of composition of velocities and accelerations.

### 10.1.5 Equations of motion and trajectories

Once a reference frame has been chosen and a point O in it fixed as the origin, the position of a moving point P can be represented by the position vector $\boldsymbol r = \overrightarrow{OP}$ pointing from O to P. As time passes, **r** changes and becomes a vector function $\boldsymbol r(t)$ of the time t.

**Definition 10.1.3 (Equation of motion and trajectory)** The position vector of a moving point as a function of time, $\boldsymbol r = \boldsymbol r(t)$, is called the **kinematic equation of motion** (or simply the equation of motion) of the moving point in that reference frame. The curve formed by joining the positions that the moving point passes through in turn during an interval of time is called the **trajectory** of the moving point.

In a coordinate system, the equation of motion takes the form of three coordinates as functions of time. In a Cartesian frame {s},

$$
r_s(t) = \begin{pmatrix} x(t) \\ y(t) \\ z(t) \end{pmatrix} , \tag{10.1.4}
$$

while in polar coordinates it is ρ(t), φ(t). The equation of motion contains all the information about the motion: from it we can find the position at any instant, differentiate to get the velocity and the acceleration, and eliminate the time t to get the equation of the trajectory. The trajectory records only which places were passed through, not when they were reached; the same trajectory can be travelled fast or slowly, or with a stop halfway.

Throughout this chapter we assume that the equation of motion is at least twice continuously differentiable with respect to time. This is a physically reasonable assumption: the position of a real body cannot jump, nor can its velocity, since that would require an infinite force. When planning robot motion in Chapter 22 we shall also see that in practice the acceleration should not jump either, otherwise it causes shocks and vibration. Note that in robot trajectory planning a geometric curve without time is often called a "path", and a motion with a time law a "trajectory"; this chapter follows the custom of mechanics, using "equation of motion" for a motion with time and "trajectory" for the curve it traces. Please note the difference between the two usages when reading Chapter 22.

### 10.1.6 Example: a part at rest seen from an AGV

We now answer the question of Section 10.1.1.

**Example 10.1.1** In the workshop floor frame {s}, the centre of the body of an AGV is at the origin at t = 0, with its front facing the +x direction; after that it turns left at v = {{ex10_1.v:.1f}} m/s on a circle of radius $R_0$ = {{ex10_1.R0:.1f}} m, whose centre C lies at (0, 2.0) m. The origin of the AGV frame {b} is at the centre of the vehicle body, its x-axis points towards the front, its y-axis to the left and its z-axis up. A part P on the floor is at rest at $p_s$ = (2.0, 0.5, 0) m. Find the equation of motion, the trajectory and the speed of the part in {b}, and its position in {b} at t = {{ex10_1.t1:.0f}} s.

The angular velocity of the AGV's circular motion is Ω = v/$R_0$ = {{ex10_1.Om:.2f}} rad/s, and the angle through which the front has turned at time t is ψ = Ωt. The position of the centre of the vehicle body in {s} and the orientation of the body are

$$
p_{sb}(t) = \begin{pmatrix} R_0\sin\Omega t \\ R_0 - R_0\cos\Omega t \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad R_{sb}(t) = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \Omega t) . \tag{10.1.5}
$$

By Eq. (5.1.3), the coordinates of the part in {b} are

$$
p_b(t) = R_{sb}(t)^{\mathsf T}\,\big(p_s - p_{sb}(t)\big) . \tag{10.1.6}
$$

This is the equation of motion of the part in the reference frame of the AGV. Substituting the centre C into the same expression gives $R_{sb}^{\mathsf T}(c_s - p_{sb}) = R_0\,R_{sb}^{\mathsf T}(-\sin\Omega t,\ \cos\Omega t,\ 0)^{\mathsf T} = (0,\ R_0,\ 0)^{\mathsf T}$, independent of time: as seen from the AGV, the centre C is a fixed point at a distance $R_0$ to the left of the vehicle body. Hence

$$
\big|p_b(t) - c_b\big| = \big|R_{sb}^{\mathsf T}(p_s - c_s)\big| = |p_s - c_s| = d , \tag{10.1.7}
$$

where the second equality uses the fact that rotation matrices preserve lengths (Theorem 4.1.1 and its three-dimensional generalization). Equation (10.1.7) shows that the trajectory of the part in {b} is a circle with centre $c_b = (0, R_0, 0)$ and radius d = |CP| = {{ex10_1.d:.1f}} m. As seen from the AGV, the whole workshop turns about C at −Ω, so the part moves clockwise along this circle with speed Ωd = {{ex10_1.speed_b:.3f}} m/s, taking 2π/Ω ≈ {{ex10_1.period:.1f}} s for one revolution. This is exactly the figure the lidar reported.

At t = {{ex10_1.t1:.0f}} s the front has turned through {{ex10_1.psi1_deg:.2f}}°, and the centre of the vehicle body is at ({{ex10_1.ox1:.4f}}, {{ex10_1.oy1:.4f}}) m. By Eq. (10.1.6), the part is at ({{ex10_1.pbx:.4f}}, {{ex10_1.pby:.4f}}) m in {b}, that is, behind the centre of the vehicle body and slightly to its right; its velocity in {b} is ({{ex10_1.vbx:.4f}}, {{ex10_1.vby:.4f}}) m/s, of magnitude {{ex10_1.speed_b:.3f}} m/s. Program 10.1.1 finds this velocity in two ways: first by numerically differentiating Eq. (10.1.6), and second by the formula $\dot p_b = -\Omega\hat z\times p_b - (v, 0, 0)^{\mathsf T}$, which will be derived in Section 10.6; the two agree. Figure 10.1.2 shows side by side what is seen in the two reference frames.

This example also reveals a noteworthy fact. On the ground the part is at rest, and its velocity and acceleration are both zero; seen from the AGV it moves on a circle, with a velocity and with an acceleration $\Omega^2 d$ towards C. The acceleration is not zero, yet no horizontal force acts on the part. This shows that in the rotating reference frame of the AGV, Newton's second law cannot be applied directly: the AGV is not an inertial reference frame. How to compute accelerations correctly in such a reference frame is the subject of Section 10.6.

### 10.1.7 Everyday examples

Sitting in a high-speed train moving uniformly, toss a coin straight up: it falls back into your hand, not behind you. With the carriage as the reference frame, the coin is thrown vertically upwards; with the ground as the reference frame, the coin moves forward with the carriage at several tens of metres per second and traces a parabola. Both descriptions are correct, and a person in the carriage cannot tell from the motion of the coin whether the train is moving; this is exactly what Galileo observed in the ship's cabin. It is different when the train speeds up, slows down or turns: the coin falls towards the rear, towards the front, or towards the outside of the curve. The carriage is then no longer an inertial reference frame, and Section 10.6 will explain where the extra acceleration comes from.

Another familiar phenomenon is driving in the rain. With no wind, raindrops fall vertically relative to the ground, yet from inside a moving car the streaks of rain are seen sweeping slantwise backwards across the windows, and the faster the car, the more slanted the streaks. The velocity of a raindrop relative to the car equals its velocity relative to the ground minus the velocity of the car relative to the ground, and the result of this vector subtraction points obliquely backwards. This, too, shows directly that velocity depends on the reference frame.

**Engineer's notes**

- The positions and velocities reported by sensors are always relative to the sensor itself. A lidar, a camera or an ultrasonic sensor measures quantities of the target relative to the vehicle carrying it; wheel odometry and an inertial measurement unit measure the motion of the vehicle itself. When fusing several sensors, the first thing to do is convert every quantity into the same reference frame.
- In programs, label every motion quantity with its reference frame and coordinate system, for example `v_part_in_b`, not just `v_part`. An error in the reference frame usually does not make the program fail; it only makes the robot veer off in the wrong direction.
- Treating the workshop floor as an inertial reference frame is accurate enough for both the kinematics and the dynamics of robots. The Earth's rotation needs to be taken into account only in navigation-grade inertial measurement, long periods of pure inertial dead reckoning, or on large rotating platforms (Section 10.6).
- When a mobile robot turns, all the stationary objects around it appear, as seen from the vehicle, to rotate about the centre of the turn, and the farther they are from the centre of the turn, the greater their apparent velocity. A velocity-based obstacle detection algorithm that does not subtract the vehicle's own motion will mistake stationary shelves for moving obstacles.
