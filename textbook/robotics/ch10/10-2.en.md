### 10.2.1 The problem

On the gearbox assembly line of the WenQuest digital factory, a SCARA robot dispenses sealant into the seal groove of a housing cover. The seal groove is an ellipse; the dispensing gun is mounted on the end-effector of the SCARA, and the sealant flow is constant. Every millisecond the controller issues a target position for the tool tip. The process engineer cares about two things: first, how fast the tool tip travels along the groove, because with a constant sealant flow the bead is thick where the tip moves slowly and thin where it moves fast; second, how sharply the velocity changes, because if the acceleration is too large the robot vibrates, and the end of the bead also flings off "sealant strings".

On the same line there is another problem. To shorten the cycle time, the robot arm does not stop completely when placing parts into a tote; it opens the gripper while still moving. Once released, a part flies some distance before landing in the tote. How far ahead should the gripper open so that the part lands exactly in the middle of the tote?

The mathematics of the two problems is one and the same: given how the position changes with time, find the velocity and the acceleration; or conversely, given the acceleration, find how the position changes with time. In this section we give the definitions of position, velocity and acceleration, and methods for computing them, in the simplest and most commonly used coordinates, Cartesian coordinates.

### 10.2.2 Position vectors and Cartesian coordinates

Choose a reference frame and fix in it a Cartesian frame {s}: an origin O and three mutually perpendicular unit vectors $\hat{\boldsymbol x}$, $\hat{\boldsymbol y}$, $\hat{\boldsymbol z}$ forming a right-handed system (Definition 5.1.1). The **position vector** of a moving point P is $\boldsymbol r = \overrightarrow{OP}$. By Chapter 3, it can be resolved along the three coordinate axes:

$$
\boldsymbol r = x\,\hat{\boldsymbol x} + y\,\hat{\boldsymbol y} + z\,\hat{\boldsymbol z} , \qquad r_s = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix} , \tag{10.2.1}
$$

where x, y, z are the Cartesian coordinates of P, in metres. As the moving point moves, x, y, z change with time and **r** becomes a vector function of time, $\boldsymbol r(t)$; this is the equation of motion of Section 10.1.

There is one assumption running through this whole section that is easy to overlook: the frame {s} is attached to the reference frame, and the three coordinate axes $\hat{\boldsymbol x}$, $\hat{\boldsymbol y}$, $\hat{\boldsymbol z}$ do not change with time. Section 10.3 will show that once this assumption is dropped, differentiation produces new terms.

### 10.2.3 Derivatives of vector functions

Velocity is the rate of change of position, and acceleration the rate of change of velocity. So we must first explain how to differentiate a vector function. Chapter 7 defined the derivative of a scalar function (Eq. (7.1.1)); for a vector function the definition is exactly the same, except that "difference" means the difference of vectors.

**Definition 10.2.1 (Derivative of a vector function)** Let $\boldsymbol u(t)$ be a vector function of time. If the limit

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol u}(t) = \frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol u}{\mathrm dt} = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\boldsymbol u(t+h) - \boldsymbol u(t)}{h} \tag{10.2.2}
$$

exists, $\dot{\boldsymbol u}(t)$ is called the derivative of **u** at the time t.

No coordinate system appears anywhere in this definition: vectors are subtracted by the parallelogram rule (Section 3.1), dividing by h is multiplication by a scalar, and the limit is a limit in the sense of "the length tends to zero". So the derivative, like the vector itself, does not depend on the coordinate system. But it does depend on the reference frame: the $\boldsymbol u(t+h) - \boldsymbol u(t)$ in the definition compares the vector at two instants in one particular reference frame, and in another reference frame rotating relative to it, the "difference" of the same arrow at the two instants is different. Section 10.6 will make this precise.

Derivatives of vector functions obey the same rules as ordinary derivatives.

**Theorem 10.2.1** Let $\boldsymbol u(t)$ and $\boldsymbol w(t)$ be differentiable vector functions and f(t) a differentiable scalar function. Then

$$
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(f\boldsymbol u) = \dot f\,\boldsymbol u + f\,\dot{\boldsymbol u} , \qquad
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\boldsymbol u\cdot\boldsymbol w) = \dot{\boldsymbol u}\cdot\boldsymbol w + \boldsymbol u\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol w} , \qquad
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\boldsymbol u\times\boldsymbol w) = \dot{\boldsymbol u}\times\boldsymbol w + \boldsymbol u\times\dot{\boldsymbol w} . \tag{10.2.3}
$$

*Proof*　The three formulas are proved in the same way; take the cross product as an example. By the distributive law of the cross product (Theorem 3.2.3),

$$
\boldsymbol u(t+h)\times\boldsymbol w(t+h) - \boldsymbol u(t)\times\boldsymbol w(t) = \big[\boldsymbol u(t+h) - \boldsymbol u(t)\big]\times\boldsymbol w(t+h) + \boldsymbol u(t)\times\big[\boldsymbol w(t+h) - \boldsymbol w(t)\big] .
$$

Divide both sides by h and let h → 0. The first term on the right tends to $\dot{\boldsymbol u}\times\boldsymbol w$ (**w** is differentiable and hence continuous), and the second to $\boldsymbol u\times\dot{\boldsymbol w}$. For the dot product and multiplication by a scalar, simply replace the cross product by the dot product or by scalar multiplication.　∎

The cross product is not commutative, so the order of the factors in the two terms of the third formula must not be reversed. The second formula has a frequently used corollary: if the length of **u** does not change, then $\boldsymbol u\cdot\boldsymbol u$ is constant, and differentiating gives $2\boldsymbol u\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol u} = 0$; that is, **the derivative of a vector of constant length is perpendicular to the vector itself**. The velocity of a point of a rotating rigid body being perpendicular to the radius in Section 3.6, and the derivatives of the local basis vectors in Section 10.3, are both instances of this corollary.

### 10.2.4 Velocity

**Definition 10.2.2 (Velocity and speed)** The derivative of the position vector of a moving point with respect to time,

$$
\boldsymbol v = \dot{\boldsymbol r} = \frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol r}{\mathrm dt} \tag{10.2.4}
$$

is called the **velocity** of the moving point, in m/s. The magnitude of the velocity, $v = |\boldsymbol v|$, is called the **speed**.

The direction of the velocity has a clear geometric meaning. The difference $\boldsymbol r(t+h) - \boldsymbol r(t)$ in Eq. (10.2.2) is the vector pointing from the position at time t to the position at time t + h, that is, a secant of the trajectory, called the **displacement** over this interval of time. As h → 0 the secant tends to the tangent to the trajectory at that point, so the velocity lies along the tangent to the trajectory and points to the side towards which the motion proceeds. The speed is the length of trajectory travelled per unit time; this will be made rigorous in Section 10.5, after the arc length has been introduced.

To compute the velocity in Cartesian coordinates, we need only differentiate the coordinates.

**Theorem 10.2.2** In a Cartesian frame attached to the reference frame, the components of the velocity are the derivatives of the coordinates with respect to time:

$$
\boldsymbol v = \dot x\,\hat{\boldsymbol x} + \dot y\,\hat{\boldsymbol y} + \dot z\,\hat{\boldsymbol z} , \qquad v_s = \begin{pmatrix} \dot x \\ \dot y \\ \dot z \end{pmatrix} . \tag{10.2.5}
$$

*Proof*　Differentiate Eq. (10.2.1) term by term using the first formula of Theorem 10.2.1: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(x\hat{\boldsymbol x}) = \dot x\,\hat{\boldsymbol x} + x\,\dot{\hat{\boldsymbol x}}$. The coordinate axes are attached to the reference frame, so $\dot{\hat{\boldsymbol x}} = 0$, leaving only $\dot x\,\hat{\boldsymbol x}$. The y and z terms are handled likewise.　∎

The step "$\dot{\hat{\boldsymbol x}} = 0$" in the proof looks trivial, but it is the whole reason why Cartesian coordinates are simple. In the polar coordinates of Section 10.3 the basis vectors turn with the position of the point, their derivatives are not zero, and the components of the velocity are no longer simply the derivatives of the coordinates.

### 10.2.5 Acceleration

**Definition 10.2.3 (Acceleration)** The derivative of the velocity with respect to time,

$$
\boldsymbol a = \dot{\boldsymbol v} = \ddot{\boldsymbol r} \tag{10.2.6}
$$

is called the **acceleration** of the moving point, in m/s².

By Theorem 10.2.2, differentiating once more (the coordinate axes still do not move),

$$
\boldsymbol a = \ddot x\,\hat{\boldsymbol x} + \ddot y\,\hat{\boldsymbol y} + \ddot z\,\hat{\boldsymbol z} , \qquad a_s = \begin{pmatrix} \ddot x \\ \ddot y \\ \ddot z \end{pmatrix} . \tag{10.2.7}
$$

Acceleration describes the change of velocity, and since velocity has both magnitude and direction, acceleration reflects both the change of speed and the change of the direction of motion. If the speed stays the same while the direction changes, there is still an acceleration; the centripetal acceleration of uniform circular motion (Eq. (3.6.8)) is an example. The relation between the two parts is given by the following theorem.

**Theorem 10.2.3** The rate of change of the speed equals the projection of the acceleration on the direction of the velocity:

$$
\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{\boldsymbol v\cdot\boldsymbol a}{v} \qquad (v \neq 0) . \tag{10.2.8}
$$

Hence the speed increases when the angle between the acceleration and the velocity is less than 90°, decreases when it is greater than 90°, and is momentarily unchanged when it equals 90°.

*Proof*　$v^2 = \boldsymbol v\cdot\boldsymbol v$; by Theorem 10.2.1, $2v\dot v = 2\boldsymbol v\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol v} = 2\boldsymbol v\cdot\boldsymbol a$. Divide both sides by 2v.　∎

Acceleration also has an intuitive picture. Move the velocity vectors at all instants to a common starting point; their tips trace a curve called the **hodograph**. By Definition 10.2.3, the acceleration is the velocity of the moving point on this curve, and lies along the tangent to the hodograph. The hodograph was introduced by William Rowan Hamilton in a paper presented to the Royal Irish Academy in December 1846 and published in 1847. He proved that for a planet under an inverse-square attraction the hodograph is a circle, which gives an extremely concise geometric proof of Keplerian motion.

Writing the components of velocity and acceleration in Cartesian coordinates and taking them as the starting point of mechanics is the contribution of Leonhard Euler. Before him, from Christiaan Huygens and Newton up to the 1740s, curvilinear motion was mostly studied by resolving along the tangent and the normal at each point of the trajectory, that is, in the natural coordinates of Section 10.5; this approach depends on the geometry of the particular problem and has difficulty with three-dimensional motion. In his paper "Découverte d'un nouveau principe de mécanique" (Discovery of a new principle of mechanics), presented to the Berlin Academy in 1750 and published in 1752, Euler systematically used three Cartesian coordinate axes fixed in space and wrote Newton's second law as three component equations along the three axes. This approach freed the calculations of mechanics from dependence on specific geometry; it became the foundation of analytical mechanics, and it is also the standard way in which robot controllers compute motion today.

### 10.2.6 Example: dispensing sealant with a SCARA

**Example 10.2.1** In the base frame {s} of the SCARA, the z-axis points vertically upwards. The seal groove is an ellipse in the horizontal plane z = {{ex10_2.z0:.2f}} m, with centre $r_c$ = ({{ex10_2.xc:.2f}}, {{ex10_2.yc:.2f}}, {{ex10_2.z0:.2f}}) m, semi-major axis a = {{ex10_2.a:.2f}} m along the x direction and semi-minor axis b = {{ex10_2.b:.2f}} m. The controller moves the tool tip according to

$$
x = x_c + a\cos\omega t , \qquad y = y_c + b\sin\omega t , \qquad z = z_0 \tag{10.2.9}
$$

with ω = 2π/T, where the time for one loop is T = {{ex10_2.Tp:.0f}} s, that is, ω = π/2 ≈ {{ex10_2.w:.4f}} rad/s. Find the velocity and the acceleration of the tool tip at t = {{ex10_2.t1:.1f}} s, the largest and smallest speeds over one loop, and decide whether the tool tip is speeding up or slowing down at that instant.

By Theorem 10.2.2, differentiate Eq. (10.2.9) term by term:

$$
v_s = \begin{pmatrix} -a\omega\sin\omega t \\ b\omega\cos\omega t \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad
a_s = \begin{pmatrix} -a\omega^2\cos\omega t \\ -b\omega^2\sin\omega t \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = -\omega^2\,(r_s - r_c) . \tag{10.2.10}
$$

At t = 0.5 s, ωt = π/4, and the tool tip is at ({{ex10_2.x1:.5f}}, {{ex10_2.y1:.5f}}, {{ex10_2.z0:.2f}}) m;

$$
v_s = \begin{pmatrix} {{ex10_2.vx1}} \\ {{ex10_2.vy1}} \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{m/s} , \qquad
a_s = \begin{pmatrix} {{ex10_2.ax1}} \\ {{ex10_2.ay1}} \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} ,
$$

the speed is v = {{ex10_2.speed1}} m/s and the magnitude of the acceleration is |**a**| = {{ex10_2.acc1}} m/s². The angle between the velocity and the acceleration is {{ex10_2.ang_va:.2f}}°, less than 90°, so by Theorem 10.2.3 the speed is increasing, at the rate $\boldsymbol v\cdot\boldsymbol a/v$ = {{ex10_2.dspeed}} m/s².

The second equality of Eq. (10.2.10) shows that the acceleration always points towards the centre of the ellipse, with a magnitude proportional to the distance from the centre (Figure 10.2.1(a)). This is a feature of the parametrization (10.2.9): it is the combination of two mutually perpendicular simple harmonic oscillations of the same frequency. The square of the speed is $v^2 = \omega^2(a^2\sin^2\omega t + b^2\cos^2\omega t)$; it reaches its largest value aω = {{ex10_2.vmax}} m/s at the ends of the minor axis ($\sin\omega t = \pm1$) and its smallest value bω = {{ex10_2.vmin}} m/s at the ends of the major axis, the ratio of the two being a/b = 1.5. With a constant sealant flow, the bead at the two ends of the major axis is 50% thicker than at the two ends of the minor axis, which is unacceptable for the process. The magnitude of the acceleration is largest at the ends of the major axis, where it equals $a\omega^2$ = {{ex10_2.amax}} m/s².

The root of the problem is that Eq. (10.2.9) makes the parameter ωt increase uniformly, rather than the distance travelled. To make the bead uniform, the tool tip must move along the ellipse at constant speed, which requires the length along the trajectory itself as the parameter. The natural coordinates of Section 10.5 are designed for exactly this, and there we shall also find where the largest acceleration occurs, and how large it is, when the sealant is dispensed at constant speed.

Figure 10.2.1(b) is the hodograph of this motion. By Eq. (10.2.10), the tip of the velocity vector, $(\dot x, \dot y) = (-a\omega\sin\omega t,\ b\omega\cos\omega t)$, traces an ellipse with semi-axes aω and bω; the acceleration at time t lies along the tangent to this ellipse.

### 10.2.7 Velocity and acceleration from sampled positions

What controllers and sensors provide is not an analytical expression for the position but a sequence of samples, for example one tool-tip position every millisecond. The velocity and acceleration must then be obtained by numerical differentiation. As discussed in Section 7.4, the most common choice is the central difference quotient (7.4.3); for the second derivative the corresponding formula is

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol r}(t) \approx \frac{\boldsymbol r(t+h) - \boldsymbol r(t-h)}{2h} , \qquad
\ddot{\boldsymbol r}(t) \approx \frac{\boldsymbol r(t+h) - 2\boldsymbol r(t) + \boldsymbol r(t-h)}{h^2} , \tag{10.2.11}
$$

where h is the sampling interval. For a vector function this means forming the difference quotient of each Cartesian coordinate separately; once again we rely on the coordinate axes being fixed. Expanding **r** in both formulas by Taylor's formula up to the fourth order shows that both truncation errors are proportional to h². Program 10.2.1 takes h = 1 ms and computes the velocity and the acceleration at t = 0.5 s from positions sampled from Eq. (10.2.9); compared with Eq. (10.2.10), the largest error of the velocity components is ${{ex10_2.err_v:sci1}}$ m/s and the largest error of the acceleration components is ${{ex10_2.err_a:sci1}}$ m/s². This is the ideal case without measurement noise. As pointed out in Section 7.4, when the measured positions contain noise, the second difference quotient amplifies the noise by a factor of the order of $1/h^2$, so numerical differentiation for acceleration must be combined with filtering, or the acceleration measured directly with an accelerometer.

### 10.2.8 Motion from acceleration

Conversely, if the acceleration is known as a function of time, together with the position and velocity at some instant, the equation of motion can be found by integration. Integrating Eq. (10.2.6) twice:

$$
\boldsymbol v(t) = \boldsymbol v(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t}\boldsymbol a(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau , \qquad
\boldsymbol r(t) = \boldsymbol r(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t}\boldsymbol v(\tau)\,\mathrm d\tau . \tag{10.2.12}
$$

In Cartesian coordinates, integrating a vector means integrating its three components separately. $\boldsymbol r(t_0)$ and $\boldsymbol v(t_0)$ are called the initial conditions. Without initial conditions, the acceleration determines the motion only up to a "uniform motion in a straight line": two motions with the same acceleration can have different starting points and initial velocities. In general the acceleration also depends on the position and the velocity, and Eq. (10.2.12) becomes an initial value problem of Chapter 7, which has to be solved by numerical integration.

**Example 10.2.2** A robot arm holding a part moves horizontally at $v_0$ = {{ex10_2.vx0:.1f}} m/s and opens the gripper when the underside of the part is h = {{ex10_2.h:.2f}} m above the bottom of the tote. Neglecting air resistance, find the time the part takes to reach the bottom of the tote, how far ahead of the release point it lands, and its speed on landing; take g = {{ex10_2.g:.2f}} m/s².

Take the x-axis along the direction of motion and the z-axis vertically upwards, with the origin on the bottom of the tote directly below the centre of the underside of the part at the moment of release. After release the part is acted on only by gravity, and its acceleration is $a_s = (0,\ 0,\ -g)^{\mathsf T}$; the initial conditions are $r_s(0) = (0, 0, h)^{\mathsf T}$ and $v_s(0) = (v_0, 0, 0)^{\mathsf T}$. Integrating Eq. (10.2.12) component by component:

$$
x(t) = v_0 t , \qquad z(t) = h - \tfrac12 g t^2 . \tag{10.2.13}
$$

Setting z = 0 gives the fall time $t_1 = \sqrt{2h/g}$ = {{ex10_2.t_land:.4f}} s, the forward shift of the landing point $x = v_0 t_1$ = {{ex10_2.x_land_mm:.1f}} mm, the vertical velocity on landing $-g t_1$ = {{ex10_2.vz_land:.3f}} m/s, and the speed {{ex10_2.v_land:.3f}} m/s (Figure 10.2.2). So the gripper should open about {{ex10_2.x_land_mm:.0f}} mm before the middle of the tote. Program 10.2.1 also integrates the same initial value problem numerically in 100 steps with the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (7.2.6) of Section 7.2; the result is the same as Eq. (10.2.13), the difference being only of the order of rounding errors: when the acceleration is constant, the solution is a quadratic polynomial in t, for which the fourth-order method is exact.

The conclusions of this example have two engineering implications. First, the forward shift of the landing point is proportional to $v_0$ and to $\sqrt h$: the faster the cycle and the higher the release point, the larger the shift, and jitter of the release instant at the millisecond level turns directly into an error of the landing point at the millimetre level: in this example a jitter of 1 ms corresponds to 0.6 mm. Second, the part lands with a speed of about 2.3 m/s, so "on-the-fly placement" is not suitable for fragile or precision parts.

### 10.2.9 Everyday examples

After a basketball is released, if air resistance is neglected, its acceleration is always g vertically downwards, whatever the magnitude and direction of the release velocity. By Eq. (10.2.12), the horizontal component of the velocity stays constant and the vertical component decreases uniformly, so the hodograph of the basketball is a vertical straight line, along which the tip of the velocity vector moves downwards uniformly at the rate g. When shooting, all one adjusts are the initial conditions: the release position, and the magnitude and angle of the velocity.

The speedometer on a car's dashboard shows the speed, not the velocity: when a car takes a bend at constant speed, the speedometer reading does not change, yet the direction of the velocity changes continually, and the passengers clearly feel pushed towards the outside of the bend. This shows that a constant speed does not mean zero acceleration. Section 10.5 will separate out this "acceleration due to the change of direction" quantitatively.

**Engineer's notes**

- Controllers usually sample positions every 1–8 ms. When estimating velocity from position differences, the central difference quotient is more accurate than the forward difference quotient, but requires waiting one more sampling period; real-time control often uses the backward difference quotient with a low-pass filter, while offline analysis uses the central difference quotient.
- The acceleration obtained by taking second difference quotients of measured positions usually has noise far larger than the true value. When a reliable acceleration is needed, fit an accelerometer, or compute it analytically from the controller's commanded trajectory.
- Processes such as dispensing sealant, spray painting and welding require a constant tool-tip speed. Trajectories interpolated by the controller from a few path points given by teaching or programming often slow down automatically at corners and where the curvature is large, so the uniformity of the sealant bead or weld seam should be measured on sample pieces.
- In "on-the-fly placement", the landing point of the part is affected by the release instant, the opening speed of the gripper, and the friction between the part and the fingers. Before series production the distribution of landing points should be measured, rather than relying only on the projectile formula.
