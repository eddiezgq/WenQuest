### 10.4.1 The problem

Several gimbal cameras are mounted under the ceiling of the digital factory workshop to watch the AGVs at work. A **pan-tilt gimbal** is a mechanism that turns about two axes: the base pans about a vertical axis, and the camera bracket then tilts about a horizontal axis; together the two axes can point the optical axis of the camera in any direction of the space below. From the position of the AGV, the controller computes in real time the angles the two axes should reach and the angular velocities they should have, and drives the motors to follow.

A gimbal is mounted under the workshop ceiling 3.0 m above the floor, and an AGV passes in a straight line at 1.0 m/s, at its closest 0.5 m to the side of the point directly below the camera. The highest angular velocity of the pan axis of the gimbal is 90°/s. Can the camera keep the AGV in view all the time? And what happens if the AGV passes exactly below the camera?

To describe "which way the camera is looking" takes two angles: how far it has panned and how far it has tilted; to describe where the target is takes, in addition, a distance. These three numbers are the spherical coordinates. This section extends the method of Section 10.3 to spherical coordinates, derives the components of velocity and acceleration on the local basis of spherical coordinates, and uses them to answer the question about the gimbal. We shall see that the answer depends on a singularity of the coordinates, and that this singularity is the same kind of phenomenon as the gimbal lock of Chapter 4.

### 10.4.2 Spherical coordinates

**Definition 10.4.1 (Spherical coordinates)** In a Cartesian frame {s}, the **spherical coordinates** (r, θ, φ) of a point P are defined by

$$
x = r\sin\theta\cos\varphi , \qquad y = r\sin\theta\sin\varphi , \qquad z = r\cos\theta \tag{10.4.1}
$$

where r ≥ 0 is the distance from P to the origin; θ ∈ [0, π] is the angle between the position vector and the +z axis, called the **polar angle**; and φ, as in cylindrical coordinates, is the angle between the x axis and the projection of the position vector on the xy plane, called the **azimuthal angle**, positive anticlockwise seen from above. Angles are in radians.

Conversely,

$$
r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} , \qquad \theta = \mathrm{atan2}\big(\sqrt{x^2 + y^2},\ z\big) , \qquad \varphi = \mathrm{atan2}(y,\ x) . \tag{10.4.2}
$$

θ is found with atan2 rather than arccos(z/r) because, when θ is close to 0 or π, the derivative of arccos tends to infinity and numerical errors are magnified. Compared with cylindrical coordinates, ρ = r sin θ and z = r cos θ: spherical coordinates are just cylindrical coordinates with (ρ, z) expressed in turn by polar coordinates.

A special warning about the convention for the symbols is needed here. Following the international standard ISO 80000-2, this book uses θ for the polar angle and φ for the azimuthal angle, as most physics books do; but many mathematics books and much of the computer-graphics literature swap the two, using θ for the azimuthal angle and φ for the polar angle. In addition, surveying, navigation and gimbal manuals often use the **elevation angle**, the angle between the line of sight and the horizontal plane, positive upwards, which equals π/2 − θ; when the line of sight points downwards the elevation angle is negative, and it is often called the angle of depression instead and taken as positive. In surveying, the azimuth is also often measured clockwise from true north. When quoting a formula or reading an equipment manual, the first thing to do is to find out which convention the other side uses. Figure 10.4.1 shows the convention of this book.

Fixing a position in space by two angles and a distance is the oldest method of astronomy and surveying: observers have fixed the directions of stars by altitude and azimuth for more than two thousand years. In robotics, spherical coordinates also have a direct mechanical counterpart. Unimate, the first industrial robot, put to work in 1961 in a General Motors plant in New Jersey in the United States, positioned its arm by three motions, swinging, pitching and extending, so that the position of the end of the arm was given exactly by an azimuthal angle, an elevation angle and an extension; the first three joints of the Stanford Arm, designed in 1969 by Victor Scheinman of Stanford University, also had the structure "revolute, revolute, prismatic". Robots of this kind are called spherical robots, or also polar robots.

### 10.4.3 The local basis and the orientation of the gimbal

**Definition 10.4.2 (Local basis of spherical coordinates)** At a point P, the unit vectors in the directions of increasing r, increasing θ and increasing φ are written $\boldsymbol e_r$, $\boldsymbol e_\theta$, $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$, and are called the local basis of spherical coordinates at P.

As in Section 10.3, taking the partial derivatives of Eq. (10.4.1) with respect to r, θ and φ and dividing each by its length (1, r and r sin θ respectively) gives

$$
\boldsymbol e_r = \begin{pmatrix} \sin\theta\cos\varphi \\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi \\ \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} , \qquad
\boldsymbol e_\theta = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta\cos\varphi \\ \cos\theta\sin\varphi \\ -\sin\theta \end{pmatrix} , \qquad
\boldsymbol e_\varphi = \begin{pmatrix} -\sin\varphi \\ \cos\varphi \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{10.4.3}
$$

$\boldsymbol e_r$ points outwards along the position vector; $\boldsymbol e_\theta$ points along the meridian towards increasing polar angle (towards "south" in the northern hemisphere); $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ points along the parallel towards increasing azimuthal angle, and is the same as $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ of cylindrical coordinates. The three are mutually orthogonal and satisfy $\boldsymbol e_r\times\boldsymbol e_\theta = \boldsymbol e_\varphi$, forming a right-handed set in the order (r, θ, φ). The position vector has only one component on the local basis:

$$
\boldsymbol r = r\,\boldsymbol e_r . \tag{10.4.4}
$$

All the information about the direction, that is, θ and φ, is hidden in the direction of $\boldsymbol e_r$.

Arranging the three basis vectors as the columns of a matrix in the order $(\boldsymbol e_\theta, \boldsymbol e_\varphi, \boldsymbol e_r)$ and comparing with the elementary rotations of Chapter 4, one can verify that

$$
\big(\boldsymbol e_\theta\ \ \boldsymbol e_\varphi\ \ \boldsymbol e_r\big) = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat{\boldsymbol z},\ \varphi)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat{\boldsymbol y},\ \theta) . \tag{10.4.5}
$$

Take the third column, for example: $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat{\boldsymbol y}, \theta)$ turns $\hat{\boldsymbol z}$ to $(\sin\theta, 0, \cos\theta)^{\mathsf T}$, and $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat{\boldsymbol z}, \varphi)$ then turns it to $\boldsymbol e_r$. Equation (10.4.5) has a direct engineering interpretation. By the composition rule of Section 4.2, multiplying on the right means turning about the body's own axes: first turn through φ about the vertical z axis, then through θ about the turned y axis. This is exactly what a gimbal does: the pan axis turns through the azimuthal angle φ, and the tilt axis (which is carried round by the pan) then turns through the polar angle θ. If the z axis of the camera frame is taken along the optical axis (Section 5.1) and the x and y axes along the two directions of the image, then when the gimbal is at (θ, φ) the orientation of the camera frame relative to {s} is the matrix of Eq. (10.4.5), and the optical axis points along $\boldsymbol e_r$, straight at the target. **The local basis of spherical coordinates is the camera frame on a two-axis gimbal.**

### 10.4.4 Derivatives of the local basis

Following the idea of Section 10.3.4, we first find the angular velocity of the local basis as a whole.

**Theorem 10.4.1** When a point P moves, the local basis of spherical coordinates turns with angular velocity

$$
\boldsymbol\omega = \dot\varphi\,\hat{\boldsymbol z} + \dot\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi \tag{10.4.6}
$$

where $\hat{\boldsymbol z} = \cos\theta\,\boldsymbol e_r - \sin\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\theta$. The derivatives of the three basis vectors are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\boldsymbol e}_r &= \boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol e_r = \dot\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\theta + \dot\varphi\sin\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi , \\
\dot{\boldsymbol e}_\theta &= \boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol e_\theta = -\dot\theta\,\boldsymbol e_r + \dot\varphi\cos\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi , \\
\dot{\boldsymbol e}_\varphi &= \boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol e_\varphi = -\dot\varphi\,\big(\sin\theta\,\boldsymbol e_r + \cos\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\theta\big) .
\end{aligned} \tag{10.4.7}
$$

*Proof*　Write $R = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat{\boldsymbol z}, \varphi)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat{\boldsymbol y}, \theta) = R_1R_2$. By the product rule,

$$
\dot R R^{\mathsf T} = \dot R_1 R_1^{\mathsf T} + R_1\big(\dot R_2 R_2^{\mathsf T}\big)R_1^{\mathsf T} .
$$

The elementary rotations about fixed axes satisfy $\dot R_1R_1^{\mathsf T} = [\dot\varphi\hat z]$ and $\dot R_2R_2^{\mathsf T} = [\dot\theta\hat y]$ (Eq. (3.6.9)). By the transformation rule of cross-product matrices, $R_1[a]R_1^{\mathsf T} = [R_1a]$ (Eq. (3.4.6)), the second term equals $[\dot\theta R_1\hat y]$, and $R_1\hat y$ is exactly $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ (the third expression of Eq. (10.4.3)). Thus $\dot RR^{\mathsf T} = [\dot\varphi\hat z + \dot\theta\boldsymbol e_\varphi]$, which by Definition 6.2.1 is the spatial angular velocity of R, that is, Eq. (10.4.6). The columns of R turn with this angular velocity, $\dot{\boldsymbol e} = \boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol e$.

What remains is to compute the cross products. Use $\boldsymbol e_r\times\boldsymbol e_\theta = \boldsymbol e_\varphi$ and its cyclic permutations $\boldsymbol e_\theta\times\boldsymbol e_\varphi = \boldsymbol e_r$, $\boldsymbol e_\varphi\times\boldsymbol e_r = \boldsymbol e_\theta$, and write $\hat{\boldsymbol z}$ as $\cos\theta\,\boldsymbol e_r - \sin\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\theta$ (by Eq. (10.4.3), the projections of $\hat{\boldsymbol z}$ on $\boldsymbol e_r$ and $\boldsymbol e_\theta$ are cos θ and −sin θ):

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol z}\times\boldsymbol e_r = \sin\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi , \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol z}\times\boldsymbol e_\theta = \cos\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi , \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol z}\times\boldsymbol e_\varphi = -\cos\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\theta - \sin\theta\,\boldsymbol e_r ,
$$

$$
\boldsymbol e_\varphi\times\boldsymbol e_r = \boldsymbol e_\theta , \qquad \boldsymbol e_\varphi\times\boldsymbol e_\theta = -\boldsymbol e_r , \qquad \boldsymbol e_\varphi\times\boldsymbol e_\varphi = 0 .
$$

Substituting into $\boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol e = \dot\varphi\,\hat{\boldsymbol z}\times\boldsymbol e + \dot\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi\times\boldsymbol e$ gives Eq. (10.4.7).　∎

Equation (10.4.7) can also be verified by differentiating the components of Eq. (10.4.3) directly; the result is the same, only the computation is more tedious. The proof above has two advantages. First, it shows where each term of Eq. (10.4.7) comes from: the terms in $\dot\varphi$ come from turning about the vertical axis, and the terms in $\dot\theta$ from tilting about $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$. Second, it also gives the angular velocity of the gimbal camera: Eq. (10.4.6) is the spatial angular velocity of the camera frame; the angular velocity of panning lies along the vertical axis, that of tilting along the horizontal axis that is carried round with the pan, and the two add as vectors (Theorem 3.6.2). The controller of a gyro-stabilized gimbal uses exactly this relation to convert the angular velocity measured by the gyroscope on the camera into the rates of the two axes.

### 10.4.5 Components of velocity and acceleration

**Theorem 10.4.2** When a point P moves with spherical coordinates (r(t), θ(t), φ(t)), its velocity is

$$
\boldsymbol v = \dot r\,\boldsymbol e_r + r\dot\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\theta + r\sin\theta\,\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi , \tag{10.4.8}
$$

and the three components of the acceleration $\boldsymbol a = a_r\boldsymbol e_r + a_\theta\boldsymbol e_\theta + a_\varphi\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ are

$$
a_r = \ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 - r\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2 , \tag{10.4.9}
$$

$$
a_\theta = r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta - r\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\varphi^2 , \tag{10.4.10}
$$

$$
a_\varphi = r\sin\theta\,\ddot\varphi + 2\dot r\dot\varphi\sin\theta + 2r\dot\theta\dot\varphi\cos\theta . \tag{10.4.11}
$$

*Proof*　Differentiate Eq. (10.4.4), using the first expression of Eq. (10.4.7): $\boldsymbol v = \dot r\,\boldsymbol e_r + r\,\dot{\boldsymbol e}_r = \dot r\,\boldsymbol e_r + r\dot\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\theta + r\dot\varphi\sin\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi$, which is Eq. (10.4.8). Then differentiate its three terms one by one:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\dot r\,\boldsymbol e_r\big) &= \ddot r\,\boldsymbol e_r + \dot r\dot\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\theta + \dot r\dot\varphi\sin\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi , \\
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(r\dot\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\theta\big) &= (\dot r\dot\theta + r\ddot\theta)\,\boldsymbol e_\theta - r\dot\theta^2\,\boldsymbol e_r + r\dot\theta\dot\varphi\cos\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi , \\
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(r\sin\theta\,\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi\big) &= \big(\dot r\sin\theta\,\dot\varphi + r\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\dot\varphi + r\sin\theta\,\ddot\varphi\big)\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi - r\sin\theta\,\dot\varphi^2\big(\sin\theta\,\boldsymbol e_r + \cos\theta\,\boldsymbol e_\theta\big) .
\end{aligned}
$$

Add the three and collect along $\boldsymbol e_r$, $\boldsymbol e_\theta$, $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$: the coefficient of $\boldsymbol e_r$ is $\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2 - r\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2$; the coefficient of $\boldsymbol e_\theta$ is $\dot r\dot\theta + \dot r\dot\theta + r\ddot\theta - r\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\varphi^2$; the coefficient of $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ is $\dot r\dot\varphi\sin\theta + r\dot\theta\dot\varphi\cos\theta + \dot r\dot\varphi\sin\theta + r\dot\theta\dot\varphi\cos\theta + r\sin\theta\,\ddot\varphi$. Simplifying gives Eqs. (10.4.9) to (10.4.11).　∎

These formulas can be compared with the results for cylindrical coordinates. Let θ = π/2 and $\dot\theta = 0$, so that the point moves in the xy plane and r is just ρ; then Eqs. (10.4.9) and (10.4.11) reduce to the radial and transverse components of Eq. (10.3.9), and Eq. (10.4.10) is zero. In $a_\varphi$, the two terms $2\dot r\dot\varphi\sin\theta$ and $2r\dot\theta\dot\varphi\cos\theta$ together make $2\dot\rho\dot\varphi$ (because $\dot\rho = \dot r\sin\theta + r\dot\theta\cos\theta$): the term of Section 10.3 appears once again; and $2\dot r\dot\theta$ in $a_\theta$ is a term of the same kind in the plane of tilting. In Eq. (10.4.9), $-r\dot\theta^2$ and $-r\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2$ are centripetal terms in two directions, the radius of the latter being the distance r sin θ to the z axis.

### 10.4.6 Gimbal angular velocities from the velocity

The control problem of a gimbal is the reverse of the above: given the position and velocity of the target relative to the gimbal, find the angular velocities the two axes should have. By Eq. (10.4.8), the three components of the velocity on the local basis are $\dot r$, $r\dot\theta$ and $r\sin\theta\,\dot\varphi$, and these three components are also the dot products of the velocity with the corresponding basis vectors. Therefore

$$
\dot r = \boldsymbol e_r\cdot\boldsymbol v , \qquad \dot\theta = \frac{\boldsymbol e_\theta\cdot\boldsymbol v}{r} , \qquad \dot\varphi = \frac{\boldsymbol e_\varphi\cdot\boldsymbol v}{r\sin\theta} . \tag{10.4.12}
$$

The dot products can be computed in any coordinate system, for example in {s} with the components of Eq. (10.4.3). Equation (10.4.12) has a clear physical meaning: the component of the target's velocity along the line of sight changes only the distance and needs no turning of the gimbal; only the components perpendicular to the line of sight have to be tracked by the gimbal. The tilt rate is the "meridian" component divided by the distance, and the pan rate is the "parallel" component divided by the distance r sin θ from the vertical axis. This last denominator will cause trouble in Section 10.4.8.

### 10.4.7 Example: a gimbal tracking an AGV

Take the gimbal frame {g}: its origin at the intersection of the two axes of the gimbal (approximately the optical centre of the camera), its z axis vertically up, and its x axis parallel to the direction of travel of the AGV. The camera is H = {{ex10_4.H:.1f}} m above the floor. The AGV runs at constant speed along the straight line x = vt, y = d, z = −H, with v = {{ex10_4.v:.1f}} m/s and d = {{ex10_4.d:.1f}} m, and is closest to the camera at t = 0 (Figure 10.4.2(a)). The target is below, so the polar angle θ is greater than 90°.

**Example 10.4.1** Find, at t = {{ex10_4.t1:.0f}} s, the spherical coordinates of the target, the two angular velocities of the gimbal, and the terms of the velocity and acceleration on the local basis.

At this moment the target is at (−1.0, 0.5, −3.0) m in {g}. By Eq. (10.4.2), r = {{ex10_4.r:.4f}} m, θ = {{ex10_4.th_deg:.2f}}° and φ = {{ex10_4.ph_deg:.2f}}°; the angle of depression of the line of sight is θ − 90° = {{ex10_4.elev_deg:.2f}}°. The velocity is $v_g = (1.0, 0, 0)^{\mathsf T}$ m/s. By Eqs. (10.4.12) and (10.4.3),

$$
\dot r = {{ex10_4.rdot}}\ \mathrm{m/s} , \qquad \dot\theta = {{ex10_4.thdot}}\ \mathrm{rad/s}\ ({{ex10_4.thdot_deg:.2f}}^\circ/\mathrm s) , \qquad \dot\varphi = {{ex10_4.phdot:.4f}}\ \mathrm{rad/s}\ ({{ex10_4.phdot_deg:.2f}}^\circ/\mathrm s) .
$$

$\dot r < 0$: the target is approaching; $\dot\theta > 0$: the line of sight is turning towards straight down; $\dot\varphi < 0$: the gimbal is panning clockwise seen from above. The three components of the velocity on the local basis are $(\dot r,\ r\dot\theta,\ r\sin\theta\,\dot\varphi)$ = ({{ex10_4.v_r:.5f}}, {{ex10_4.v_th:.5f}}, {{ex10_4.v_ph:.5f}}) m/s, and the sum of their squares is 1, which is the square of the speed of the AGV. The angular velocity of the gimbal camera at this moment is given by Eq. (10.4.6) as ({{ex10_4.om_x:.4f}}, {{ex10_4.om_y:.4f}}, {{ex10_4.om_z:.4f}}) rad/s, with magnitude {{ex10_4.om_mag:.4f}} rad/s.

The AGV runs at constant speed in a straight line, so its acceleration is zero. But in spherical coordinates none of the second derivatives of r, θ and φ is zero: Program 10.4.1 finds $\ddot r$ = {{ex10_4.rdd}} m/s² and $\ddot\varphi$ = {{ex10_4.phdd:.2f}} rad/s². Substituting them into Eqs. (10.4.9) to (10.4.11), the terms are

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_r &= ({{ex10_4.ar1}}) + ({{ex10_4.ar2}}) + ({{ex10_4.ar3}}) , \\
a_\theta &= ({{ex10_4.at1}}) + ({{ex10_4.at2}}) + ({{ex10_4.at3}}) , \\
a_\varphi &= ({{ex10_4.ap1}}) + ({{ex10_4.ap2}}) + ({{ex10_4.ap3}}) ,
\end{aligned}
$$

all in m/s². None of the three terms in any row is zero, yet each row adds up to zero (the program checks this to within $10^{-6}$, the error of numerical differentiation). This example illustrates a key point about curvilinear coordinates: the second derivatives of the coordinates are not the components of the acceleration. That the gimbal must turn at varying angular velocities does not mean that the target is accelerating; conversely, if $\ddot r$ and $r\ddot\theta$ were taken as accelerations, one would "see" accelerations in a target that is at rest or moving uniformly.

**Example 10.4.2** Find the largest pan rate and the largest pan angular acceleration of the gimbal over the whole pass. If the pan rate of the gimbal must not exceed 90°/s, how far at least must the path of the AGV be from the point directly below the camera?

The azimuthal angle depends only on the projection of the target on the horizontal plane: φ = atan2(d, vt), independently of the height H. Differentiating,

$$
\dot\varphi = -\frac{v\,d}{v^2t^2 + d^2} , \qquad \ddot\varphi = \frac{2v^3d\,t}{(v^2t^2 + d^2)^2} . \tag{10.4.13}
$$

$|\dot\varphi|$ is largest at t = 0, that is, when the target is closest to the camera:

$$
|\dot\varphi|_{\max} = \frac{v}{d} = {{ex10_4.phd_max:.1f}}\ \mathrm{rad/s} = {{ex10_4.phd_max_deg:.1f}}^\circ/\mathrm s . \tag{10.4.14}
$$

Setting $\dddot\varphi = 0$ shows that $|\ddot\varphi|$ is largest at $t = \pm d/(\sqrt3\,v)$ = ±{{ex10_4.t_star:.3f}} s, where it equals $\frac{3\sqrt3}{8}\frac{v^2}{d^2}$ = {{ex10_4.phdd_max:.3f}} rad/s².

The largest angular velocity, {{ex10_4.phd_max_deg:.1f}}°/s, exceeds the limit of 90°/s, so the gimbal loses the target for about half a second around the moment the AGV passes the closest point. By Eq. (10.4.14), meeting the limit requires d ≥ v/(π/2) = {{ex10_4.d_min:.3f}} m. The tilt axis has a much easier time: over the whole pass the largest $|\dot\theta|$ is about {{ex10_4.thd_max_deg:.0f}}°/s. Figure 10.4.2(b) shows $\dot\varphi$ against time for d = 0.25 m, 0.5 m and 1.0 m: halving d doubles the peak and also makes it narrower.

### 10.4.8 The keyhole problem: a singularity of the coordinates

If the AGV passes exactly below the camera, then d = 0, and by Eq. (10.4.14) $|\dot\varphi|_{\max}$ is infinite. In fact, when d = 0 the azimuthal angle jumps from 180° to 0° at the instant t = 0, and the gimbal would have to pan through half a turn in no time at all. Even if d is not zero but small, say 0.1 m, the peak reaches {{ex10_4.phd_max_d01_deg:.0f}}°/s, which no real gimbal can follow.

The cause is the denominator r sin θ of Eq. (10.4.12). When θ = 0 or π, the point is on the z axis, where the azimuthal angle is undefined, and so are $\boldsymbol e_\theta$ and $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ of the local basis; this is the same phenomenon as the singularity of cylindrical coordinates on the z axis in Section 10.3.8, except that in spherical coordinates the singular directions correspond to "straight up" and "straight down" from the gimbal. When the target approaches one of these two directions, the azimuthal angle must change very fast even if the target moves slowly in space. In tracking antennas, telescopes and gimbals this phenomenon is called the **keyhole problem**: for an azimuth–elevation mount, a target passing overhead (or directly below) leaves near the zenith a "keyhole" region within which it cannot be tracked continuously.

The keyhole problem is the same kind of phenomenon as the gimbal lock of Section 4.5. Gimbal lock is the singularity of describing an orientation by three angles; the keyhole is the singularity of describing a direction by two angles. It can be proved that the directions on a sphere cannot be described by a pair of angles that is smooth and one-to-one everywhere; however the coordinate axes are chosen, some directions are always singular: the sphere is a bounded closed surface, and a single set of plane coordinates cannot be continuous and one-to-one and cover the whole sphere at the same time. All engineering can do is to put the singular directions where they are not needed. Common ways are:

- mount the vertical axis of the gimbal tilted, so that the keyhole moves away from the region the target passes most often;
- use an X–Y mount instead, with both axes horizontal, which moves the keyhole to the horizon;
- add a third axis and use the redundant degree of freedom to steer round the keyhole when approaching it;
- plan routes so that the target does not pass directly below the camera, which is what Example 10.4.2 does.

### 10.4.9 Everyday examples

Standing at the roadside watching a car go by, you turn your head to follow it. When the car is far away, your head turns slowly; when the car speeds past a metre in front of you, your head can hardly turn fast enough. By Eq. (10.4.14), the largest angular velocity of the head is the speed of the car divided by the distance from the lane: for a car at 15 m/s and a distance of 1.5 m it is about 10 rad/s, far beyond the speed at which the head can turn comfortably. So people instinctively step back, or simply watch the car coming and going.

The altazimuth (azimuth–elevation) telescopes common among amateur astronomers meet the same difficulty when tracking stars passing near the zenith: the azimuth axis has to turn faster and faster. Professional telescopes adopted the equatorial mount, with one axis parallel to the Earth's axis, so that the diurnal motion of a star needs only this one axis turning at a constant rate; this moves the singularity to the direction of the celestial pole. Modern large telescopes are mostly altazimuth again, but they usually leave a small region near the zenith where they do not observe.

**Engineer's notes**

- The readings of gimbals, tracking antennas, laser rangefinders and many other sensors are themselves spherical coordinates: one distance and two angles. Before using them, make sure of three things: whether the angle is a polar angle or an elevation angle; from which axis the azimuth is measured and which direction is positive; and whether angles are in degrees or radians.
- When choosing a gimbal, estimate the largest pan rate by Eq. (10.4.14) as v/d and the largest angular acceleration as $\frac{3\sqrt3}{8}(v/d)^2$, then leave a margin. Where the target may pass directly below, change the mounting position or choose a three-axis design.
- The two angular velocities of a gimbal cannot simply be added to give the angular velocity of the camera: the angular velocity of panning lies along the vertical axis, and that of tilting along the horizontal axis carried round with the pan; the angular velocity of the camera is their vector sum, Eq. (10.4.6). The controller of a gyro-stabilized gimbal must convert according to this relation.
- Spherical coordinates are numerically ill-conditioned when θ is close to 0 or π. If a program has to filter or interpolate the direction of a target, it is better to work directly with the unit vector $\boldsymbol e_r$ and convert to angles only at the end.
