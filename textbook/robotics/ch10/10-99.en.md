The main thread of this chapter can be summed up in one sentence: **kinematics studies position, velocity and acceleration in a chosen reference frame; a vector itself does not depend on the coordinate system, the coordinate system decides only along which directions its components are read, while the reference frame decides the motion itself.**

We began by distinguishing reference frames from coordinate systems. A reference frame is a body agreed to be at rest; a coordinate system is a rule for numbering points. Changing the coordinate system within the same reference frame leaves the two arrows, velocity and acceleration, unchanged and changes only the readings; changing to a reference frame in relative motion changes the arrows themselves. A part at rest that is seen circling from an AGV is a case of the latter.

In fixed Cartesian coordinates, the components of velocity and acceleration are simply the first and second derivatives of the coordinates, because the axes do not move. The motion can be found by integrating the acceleration with initial conditions, and the velocity and acceleration can be estimated from sampled positions by difference quotients. In curvilinear coordinates the local basis turns as the point moves, and differentiation produces an extra term, the rate of change of the basis vectors themselves. The local basis of cylindrical coordinates turns about the z axis at $\dot\varphi$, which gives the radial and transverse components; the transverse term $2\dot\rho\dot\varphi$ cannot be neglected when a SCARA arm turns and extends at the same time. The local basis of spherical coordinates is the frame of the camera on a two-axis pan-tilt gimbal, turning at $\dot\varphi\hat{\boldsymbol z} + \dot\theta\boldsymbol e_\varphi$; near the pole the rate of the azimuthal angle tends to infinity, which is the keyhole problem of the gimbal and, like the gimbal lock of Chapter 4, a singularity of the coordinates. Natural coordinates take the trajectory itself as reference: the velocity has only a tangential component, and the acceleration splits into a tangential part that changes the speed and a normal part that changes the direction, the latter equal to the curvature times the square of the speed. All four kinds of coordinates resolve the same arrow; the unified way of obtaining the formulas of curvilinear coordinates is to write down the angular velocity of the local basis: the derivative of a basis vector equals the angular velocity crossed with the basis vector.

When the reference frame itself rotates, the derivative of a vector in the fixed frame equals its relative derivative in the moving frame plus $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol q$. From this follow the theorems of composition of velocities and of accelerations: the absolute acceleration equals the sum of the transport acceleration, the relative acceleration and the Coriolis acceleration $2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}}$. Of the two halves of the factor 2, one comes from the relative velocity being turned with the moving frame and the other from the change of coincident point. For an AGV driving straight at constant speed on a turntable, the sideways acceleration read by its accelerometer is exactly the Coriolis acceleration; the effect of the Earth's rotation on a robot in a workshop, by contrast, is only a few millionths of the acceleration due to gravity.

Finally, the distance between any two points of a rigid body does not change, so three non-collinear points determine the whole rigid body, and the count 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 gives the six degrees of freedom of a rigid body. The velocities of the points of a rigid body are determined by six numbers, the velocity of one point and the angular velocity, and the formula for their accelerations is the same as that for the transport acceleration. From the point to the rigid body, the next step is several rigid bodies connected by joints: the mechanisms of Chapter 11.

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| Reference frame | A body agreed to be at rest and used to describe motion; motion depends on the reference frame |
| Coordinate system | A rule for numbering points; changing the coordinate system within one reference frame changes only the readings |
| Equation of motion and trajectory | Position as a function of time; the curve it traces |
| Local basis | The unit vectors along the coordinate lines of curvilinear coordinates at a point; they turn as the point moves |
| Radial and transverse components | The components along $\boldsymbol e_\rho$ and $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$; the transverse acceleration contains $2\dot\rho\dot\varphi$ |
| Keyhole problem | Near the pole direction of an azimuth–elevation mount, the azimuth rate tends to infinity |
| Tangential and normal acceleration | The former changes the speed, the latter changes the direction and equals $\kappa v^2$ |
| Curvature | The angle turned by the tangent per unit arc length; the curvature of a circle is the reciprocal of its radius |
| Absolute, relative and transport motion | Motion relative to the fixed frame, relative to the moving frame, and the motion of the moving frame itself; the coincident point is the point of the moving frame that coincides with the moving point |
| Coriolis acceleration | $2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}}$; perpendicular to the relative velocity, it changes only the direction of the relative motion |
| Inertial forces | The centrifugal force, the Coriolis force and so on, which must be added when Newton's laws are written in a non-inertial reference frame |
| Six degrees of freedom of a rigid body | Three non-collinear points: 3 + 2 + 1 = 6; or 3 for position plus 3 for orientation |
