title: Chapter Overview
---

Every action of a robot comes down in the end to the motion of certain points: the tool centre point travels along a weld seam, the centre of the gripper approaches a workpiece, the centre of an AGV's body drives along an aisle, the line of sight of a camera on a pan-tilt gimbal follows a target. To plan, control and check these actions, we must first be able to say precisely where a point is, how fast it is going and how its velocity is changing. This is exactly the task of **kinematics**, namely to study position, velocity and acceleration in a chosen reference frame, without asking which forces cause the motion. This is the first chapter of Part II, Kinematics; it studies the simplest object of all: the point.

The motion of a point looks simple, yet it contains two questions that beginners confuse most easily and that cause errors most easily in engineering. The first question is "relative to what?". The same part is at rest when seen from the workshop floor, yet it circles when seen from a turning AGV; the same AGV drives in a straight line when seen from a turntable, yet traces a spiral when seen from the floor. Motion is always relative to some reference frame, and a reference frame and a coordinate system are two different things: a reference frame is a physical body agreed to be at rest, while a coordinate system is a mathematical rule for numbering points. Changing the coordinate system changes only the readings, not the motion; changing the reference frame changes the motion itself. The second question is "which components?". The same velocity vector can be resolved along Cartesian axes, but also along the radial and transverse directions, along the line of sight and the directions of two angles, or along the tangent and the normal. Chapter 3 showed that a vector itself does not depend on the coordinate system while its components change with the axes; this chapter will show that when the axes themselves turn as the point moves, differentiation produces new terms, and that these terms have a clear geometric meaning as well as very real engineering consequences.

The chapter unfolds in the following order:

- Section 10.1 distinguishes reference frames from coordinate systems and explains what kinematics studies;
- Section 10.2 defines position, velocity and acceleration in Cartesian coordinates, the basis of all calculation;
- Sections 10.3 and 10.4 describe the motion of a point in cylindrical (polar) coordinates and in spherical coordinates respectively, derive the derivatives of the local basis vectors, obtain formulas for the radial, transverse and other components, and apply them to a SCARA robot and a camera on a pan-tilt gimbal;
- Section 10.5 sets up natural coordinates along the trajectory itself, splits the acceleration into tangential and normal parts, and introduces the curvature;
- Section 10.6 studies rotating reference frames, gives a complete derivation of the theorem of composition of accelerations, that is, Coriolis' theorem, and verifies it with an AGV on a turntable;
- Section 10.7 passes from the point to the rigid body, explains why it takes six numbers to describe a rigid body, and prepares for the degrees of freedom of Chapter 11 and the forward kinematics of Chapter 12.

The chapter uses the vectors, components, cross product and $\boldsymbol v = \boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol r$ of Chapter 3, the rotation matrices of Chapter 4, the coordinate transformation $p_a = R_{ab}p_b + p_{ab}$ of Chapter 5, the angular velocity $\dot R = [\omega_s]R$ of Chapter 6, and the derivatives, numerical differentiation and numerical integration of Chapter 7. Its results will be used again and again later: the Jacobian matrix of Chapter 16 maps joint velocities to end-effector velocities; Chapter 19 studies the motion of mobile robots as they turn; trajectory planning in Chapter 22 has to control the tangential and normal accelerations; and in the dynamic equations of Chapters 23–25 the Coriolis and centrifugal terms are exactly the acceleration terms of Section 10.6 multiplied by mass.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. distinguish a reference frame from a coordinate system, and decide relative to which reference frame the motion in a problem is meant;
2. find the velocity and acceleration from the equation of motion in Cartesian coordinates, find the motion by integrating the acceleration with initial conditions, and check the results numerically;
3. write down the local bases of cylindrical and spherical coordinates and their derivatives, derive and apply the formulas for the components of velocity and acceleration on these bases, and point out the engineering meaning of the places where the coordinates are singular;
4. compute the tangential acceleration, the normal acceleration and the curvature of a trajectory, and use them to check the acceleration limits of the motion of a robot's end-effector;
5. write down correctly the absolute, relative and transport velocities and accelerations in a rotating reference frame, compute the Coriolis acceleration, and explain the readings of an accelerometer;
6. explain why a rigid body needs six numbers to describe it, reconstruct the pose of a rigid body from three marker points, and write down the velocities and accelerations of the points of a rigid body.
