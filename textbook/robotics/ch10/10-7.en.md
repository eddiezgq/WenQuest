### 10.7.1 The problem

The previous six sections all studied a single point. But the objects a robot handles have size: a gripper must approach a part from a definite direction, a welding torch must point at the seam at a definite angle, and a camera needs to know not only where a workpiece is but also which way it faces. Knowing the position of one point is not enough.

A common engineering scenario is measuring the pose of a UR5e gripper with a vision or motion-capture system: a few reflective marker points are stuck on the gripper, the cameras measure the three-dimensional coordinates of each marker, and the position and orientation of the gripper are then worked out from these coordinates. How many points must be stuck on, at the least? How many of the measured coordinates are independent? When the gripper moves, can the velocity of any point of the gripper be worked out from the velocities of a few markers?

The answers to these questions come down to one number: 6. The position of a rigid body moving freely in space is determined by six independent numbers; its velocity is determined by six numbers; and the reason industrial robot arms usually have six joints is precisely so that they can place the end-effector at any position and orientation. This section explains where this "6" comes from and, from the kinematics of a point, derives the velocities and accelerations of the points of a rigid body, preparing for the degrees of freedom of Chapter 11 and the forward kinematics of Chapter 12.

### 10.7.2 The rigid body: a set of points at fixed distances

Section 3.6 has already given the definition of a rigid body: a body in which the distance between any two points stays constant. In the language of this chapter, if P and Q are any two points of a rigid body, then

$$
|\boldsymbol r_P(t) - \boldsymbol r_Q(t)| = \text{constant} . \tag{10.7.1}
$$

Every real body deforms under load, and the rigid body is an idealization. The deformations of the links of a robot arm, a gripper or a part under normal loads are usually of the order of micrometres to millimetres and can be neglected when studying motion; Chapter 31 discusses the deformation itself.

A rigid body has infinitely many points, and each point has three coordinates. Describing the position of a rigid body would seem to need infinitely many numbers. But Eq. (10.7.1) is a very strong constraint: the points cannot each go their own way. We now prove that once three non-collinear points are known, all the other points are determined.

### 10.7.3 Three points determine a rigid body

**Theorem 10.7.1** Let A, B, C be three non-collinear points of a rigid body. If the positions of A, B, C are known at some instant, then the position of every point of the rigid body at that instant is uniquely determined.

*Proof*　Build a Cartesian frame from A, B, C, with its origin at A:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol x} = \frac{\boldsymbol r_{AB}}{|\boldsymbol r_{AB}|} , \qquad
\hat{\boldsymbol y} = \frac{\boldsymbol r_{AC} - (\boldsymbol r_{AC}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol x})\,\hat{\boldsymbol x}}{\big|\boldsymbol r_{AC} - (\boldsymbol r_{AC}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol x})\,\hat{\boldsymbol x}\big|} , \qquad
\hat{\boldsymbol z} = \hat{\boldsymbol x}\times\hat{\boldsymbol y} , \tag{10.7.2}
$$

where $\boldsymbol r_{AB} = \boldsymbol r_B - \boldsymbol r_A$, and so on. This is the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization of Section 2.7, with the third axis supplied by a cross product; A, B, C being non-collinear ensures that the denominators are not zero. This frame is completely determined by the positions of the three points, and it is fixed to the rigid body: when the body moves, the frame moves with it.

Let P be any point of the rigid body and $\boldsymbol r_{AP} = \boldsymbol r_P - \boldsymbol r_A$. It suffices to show that the three components of $\boldsymbol r_{AP}$ in this frame do not change as the body moves. By the law of cosines,

$$
\boldsymbol r_{AP}\cdot\boldsymbol r_{AB} = \tfrac12\big(|AP|^2 + |AB|^2 - |BP|^2\big) ,
$$

and the right-hand side contains only distances between points of the rigid body, so it is constant; hence $\boldsymbol r_{AP}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol x}$ is constant. Likewise $\boldsymbol r_{AP}\cdot\boldsymbol r_{AC}$ is constant, and $\hat{\boldsymbol y}$ is a linear combination of $\boldsymbol r_{AB}$ and $\boldsymbol r_{AC}$ with constant coefficients (the coefficients, too, contain only distances), so $\boldsymbol r_{AP}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol y}$ is constant. The third component satisfies $(\boldsymbol r_{AP}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol z})^2 = |AP|^2 - (\boldsymbol r_{AP}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol x})^2 - (\boldsymbol r_{AP}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol y})^2$ and is also constant, except that its sign is not yet determined. If it is zero, P lies in the plane ABC and the component is already determined. If it is not zero, it is a continuous function of time whose absolute value is constant and non-zero, so it cannot change sign. All three components are constant, so the position of P, $\boldsymbol r_P = \boldsymbol r_A + p_x\hat{\boldsymbol x} + p_y\hat{\boldsymbol y} + p_z\hat{\boldsymbol z}$, is uniquely determined by A, B, C.　∎

The sign of the third component in the proof deserves attention. Going by distances alone, P could lie on either side of the plane ABC, the two positions being mirror images of each other. It is precisely the condition that "the rigid body moves continuously" that rules out the mirror image: however a right hand is moved and turned, it never becomes a left hand. This is the same reason why, in Section 4.1, a matrix with determinant −1 represents a reflection and not a rotation.

Writing the three components as a column $p_b$ and the three axes as a matrix $R = (\hat{\boldsymbol x}\ \hat{\boldsymbol y}\ \hat{\boldsymbol z})$, Theorem 10.7.1 reads

$$
r_P = r_A + R\,p_b , \qquad p_b\ \text{constant} . \tag{10.7.3}
$$

This is exactly the coordinate transformation (5.1.2) of Chapter 5: the coordinates of the points of the rigid body in the attached frame {b} do not change, and the motion of the rigid body is completely described by the position of the origin of {b} and its orientation, (R, $r_A$), that is, by the pose of the rigid body.

### 10.7.4 Counting: 3 + 2 + 1 = 6

Theorem 10.7.1 shows that describing a rigid body needs only the nine coordinates of three points. But these nine numbers are not independent.

**Theorem 10.7.2** The position of a rigid body moving freely in space is determined by six independent numbers; that of a rigid body moving in a plane, by three independent numbers.

*Proof*　By Theorem 10.7.1 it suffices to determine the positions of three non-collinear points A, B, C. Count them one at a time (Figure 10.7.1):

- A can be anywhere in space, which takes three numbers;
- once A is fixed, the distance from B to A is fixed, so B can only lie on the sphere centred at A with radius |AB|, which takes two numbers, for example the two angles of spherical coordinates;
- once A and B are both fixed, the distances from C to A and to B are both fixed, so C can only lie on the intersection of two spheres, that is, on a circle whose axis is the line AB, which takes one number: the angle turned about AB.

That makes 3 + 2 + 1 = 6 numbers. One can also count like this: nine coordinates, minus three distance constraints (|AB|, |AC|, |BC| constant), leaves 9 − 3 = 6. In the planar case, A takes two numbers, B on a circle centred at A takes one number, and C is completely determined by its two distances and by "not being allowed to flip to the other side", giving 2 + 1 = 3 in all.　∎

The number of independent numbers needed to determine the position of a body is called its number of **degrees of freedom**. Theorem 10.7.2 says that a free rigid body in space has six degrees of freedom and one in the plane has three. Chapter 11 will extend this concept to several rigid bodies connected by joints, that is, to mechanisms, and give Grübler's formula for counting degrees of freedom.

Another way of splitting the six degrees of freedom is more common: three numbers for the position of a point, plus three numbers for the orientation. The orientation is described by the rotation matrix R, which has nine entries; but its three columns are mutually orthogonal unit vectors and satisfy six constraints (the six independent equations of $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$), leaving 9 − 6 = 3 degrees of freedom. This is why Chapter 4 describes orientation with three Euler angles or with the three exponential coordinates of axis–angle. Chapter 4 also pointed out that any representation of orientation using only three numbers is bound to be singular somewhere; the quaternion uses four numbers plus one constraint, and in return is free of singularities everywhere. This is the same kind of fact as the keyhole of spherical coordinates in Section 10.4: the number of degrees of freedom is definite, but using that many numbers as coordinates always leaves somewhere they cannot cover.

**Background and history.** The insight that the general motion of a rigid body can be decomposed into a translation of a point and a rotation about that point goes back to Leonhard Euler. In his *Theoria motus corporum solidorum seu rigidorum* (Theory of the motion of solid or rigid bodies) of 1765 he studied the motion of rigid bodies systematically, and in the paper "Formulae generales pro translatione quacunque corporum rigidorum" (General formulas for any displacement of rigid bodies), submitted in 1775 and published in 1776, he gave the general expression for the displacement of a rigid body, in which the translation takes three numbers and the rotation is represented by nine coefficients satisfying orthogonality conditions (Section 4.3). In 1830 Michel Chasles went further and proved that every rigid-body displacement is a screw motion (Section 6.1).

### 10.7.5 Example: the pose of a gripper from three markers

**Example 10.7.1** Three marker points A, B, C are stuck on the gripper of a UR5e, with coordinates (0, 0, 0), (0.10, 0, 0), (0.02, 0.06, 0) m in the gripper frame {b}. At a certain instant the motion-capture system measures the coordinates of the three points in the base frame {s} as

$$
r_A = ({{ex10_7.Ax:.4f}},\ {{ex10_7.Ay:.4f}},\ {{ex10_7.Az:.4f}}) , \quad
r_B = ({{ex10_7.Bx:.5f}},\ {{ex10_7.By:.4f}},\ {{ex10_7.Bz:.4f}}) , \quad
r_C = ({{ex10_7.Cx:.5f}},\ {{ex10_7.Cy:.5f}},\ {{ex10_7.Cz:.4f}}) \ \ (\mathrm m) .
$$

Find the pose of the gripper.

First check the distances: |AB| = {{ex10_7.dAB:.4f}} m, |AC| = {{ex10_7.dAC:.5f}} m, |BC| = {{ex10_7.dBC:.4f}} m, the same as the values in {b}, which shows that the measurements are consistent. Build the frame by Eq. (10.7.2): $\hat{\boldsymbol x}$ along AB, $\hat{\boldsymbol y}$ from AC with its component along $\hat{\boldsymbol x}$ removed and then normalized, and $\hat{\boldsymbol z} = \hat{\boldsymbol x}\times\hat{\boldsymbol y}$. Since in {b} the point B lies exactly on the x axis and C lies in the xy plane with a positive y coordinate, the frame built in this way is exactly {b}, and the matrix obtained is $R_{sb}$; the origin of the gripper is $p_{sb} = r_A$. Program 10.7.1 finds $R_{sb} = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat{\boldsymbol z}, 30^\circ)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat{\boldsymbol x}, 180^\circ)$ and $p_{sb}$ = (−0.45, −0.20, 0.30) m, the same as the true values from which this set of measurements was generated, with errors at the level of rounding error. The nine measured values have become the six numbers of the pose: three for position, plus the three independent parameters contained in R.

There is one place where it is easy to go wrong in building the frame: the third axis must be taken as $\hat{\boldsymbol x}\times\hat{\boldsymbol y}$. If it is wrongly taken as $\hat{\boldsymbol y}\times\hat{\boldsymbol x}$, the three axes are still mutually orthogonal and of unit length, and all the distances still match, but they form a left-handed frame, and the resulting matrix has determinant −1 and represents a reflection rather than a rotation (Program 10.7.1 checks this). This is exactly the mirror-image solution that was ruled out by "continuous motion" in the proof of Theorem 10.7.1. In real systems the markers are usually deliberately placed in an asymmetric pattern and one or two extra markers are added, and the pose is found by least squares, to improve accuracy and rule out mismatches; the step that finds the rotation comes down to finding the rotation matrix closest to a certain 3×3 matrix, which uses precisely the singular value decomposition method of Section 4.8 (Kabsch, 1976).

### 10.7.6 The velocities of the points of a rigid body

When a rigid body moves, its points have different velocities, but there is a definite relation between them.

**Theorem 10.7.3** When a rigid body moves, there is a unique vector **ω** such that the velocities of any two points A, P of the body satisfy

$$
\boldsymbol v_P = \boldsymbol v_A + \boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol r_{AP} . \tag{10.7.4}
$$

**ω** does not depend on the choice of A and P, and is called the angular velocity of the rigid body.

*Proof*　By Eq. (10.7.3), $r_P = r_A + Rp_b$ with $p_b$ constant. Differentiating with respect to time: $\dot r_P = \dot r_A + \dot Rp_b$. Section 6.2 proved that $\dot RR^{\mathsf T}$ is a skew-symmetric matrix (Eqs. (6.2.1) to (6.2.3)), written $[\omega_s]$, so $\dot Rp_b = [\omega_s]Rp_b = \omega_s\times(r_P - r_A)$, which is Eq. (10.7.4). $\omega_s$ is determined by R(t) and does not depend on P; for another reference point A′, writing $\boldsymbol v_P$ and $\boldsymbol v_{A'}$ by Eq. (10.7.4) and subtracting gives $\boldsymbol v_P = \boldsymbol v_{A'} + \boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol r_{A'P}$, with **ω** unchanged.

Uniqueness: if $\boldsymbol\omega_1$ and $\boldsymbol\omega_2$ both satisfy Eq. (10.7.4), then for every P of the rigid body $(\boldsymbol\omega_1 - \boldsymbol\omega_2)\times\boldsymbol r_{AP} = 0$, that is, $\boldsymbol\omega_1 - \boldsymbol\omega_2$ is parallel to every $\boldsymbol r_{AP}$. Taking P to be B and C, $\boldsymbol r_{AB}$ and $\boldsymbol r_{AC}$ are not parallel, so $\boldsymbol\omega_1 - \boldsymbol\omega_2 = 0$.　∎

Equation (10.7.4) has the same form as the transport velocity (10.6.5) of Section 10.6: take the rigid body itself as the moving frame; the points of the rigid body are then points at rest relative to the moving frame, and their velocities are transport velocities. It is also the starting point of the twist of Section 6.3 (Eq. (6.3.1)). From this we obtain the second "6" of this section: **the velocities of all the points of a rigid body are determined by six numbers: the velocity of one point and the angular velocity**. Chapter 6 combines these six numbers into a twist $\mathcal V = (\omega, v)$.

Taking the dot product of both sides of Eq. (10.7.4) with $\boldsymbol r_{AP}$, the cross-product term is perpendicular to $\boldsymbol r_{AP}$ and drops out:

$$
\boldsymbol v_P\cdot\boldsymbol r_{AP} = \boldsymbol v_A\cdot\boldsymbol r_{AP} . \tag{10.7.5}
$$

That is, the velocities of any two points of a rigid body have equal projections on the line joining them; this is called the velocity projection theorem. It can also be obtained directly from the constancy of distance: $|\boldsymbol r_{AP}|^2$ is constant, and differentiating gives $2(\boldsymbol v_P - \boldsymbol v_A)\cdot\boldsymbol r_{AP} = 0$. Intuitively, if two points had different velocities along the line joining them, the distance between them would change, and the rigid body would be stretched or compressed (Figure 10.7.2).

Conversely, the angular velocity can be found from the velocities of a few points. By Eq. (10.7.4), $\boldsymbol v_B - \boldsymbol v_A = \boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol r_{AB} = -\boldsymbol r_{AB}\times\boldsymbol\omega$, which in cross-product-matrix form is $v_B - v_A = -[r_{AB}]\,\omega$. Writing the same equation for C, the two together give six linear equations in ω:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} -[r_{AB}] \\ -[r_{AC}] \end{pmatrix}\omega = \begin{pmatrix} v_B - v_A \\ v_C - v_A \end{pmatrix} . \tag{10.7.6}
$$

The coefficient matrix is 6×3, and when A, B, C are not collinear its rank is 3, so the system can be solved by the least-squares method of Section 2.5; when the data are exact, the least-squares solution is the exact solution. Two points alone are not enough: $[r_{AB}]$ has rank only 2, and a rotation about the line AB itself does not change the velocities of A and B, so it cannot be seen from the velocities of two points. This confirms once again that "three points" are needed.

**Example 10.7.2** For the gripper of Example 10.7.1, the angular velocity at this instant is ω = (0.2, −0.1, 0.5) rad/s and the velocity of point A is $v_A$ = (0.05, 0.10, −0.02) m/s. Find the velocities of B and C, and recover the angular velocity from the velocities of the three points.

By Eq. (10.7.4), $v_B = v_A + \omega\times r_{AB}$ = {{ex10_7.vB}} m/s, of magnitude {{ex10_7.vB_mag:.4f}} m/s; $v_C$ = {{ex10_7.vC}} m/s. Program 10.7.1 verifies the velocity projection theorem, Eq. (10.7.5), for the three pairs of points, and then substitutes the three velocities into Eq. (10.7.6) and solves by least squares, obtaining ω = {{ex10_7.w_rec}} rad/s, the same as the given value. The program also lets the gripper move with this ω and $v_A$ and differentiates the position of B numerically; the result agrees with Eq. (10.7.4).

### 10.7.7 The accelerations of the points of a rigid body

**Theorem 10.7.4** Let the angular velocity of a rigid body be **ω** and its angular acceleration $\boldsymbol\alpha = \dot{\boldsymbol\omega}$. Then the accelerations of any two points A, P of the body satisfy

$$
\boldsymbol a_P = \boldsymbol a_A + \boldsymbol\alpha\times\boldsymbol r_{AP} + \boldsymbol\omega\times\big(\boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol r_{AP}\big) . \tag{10.7.7}
$$

*Proof*　Differentiate Eq. (10.7.4): $\boldsymbol a_P = \boldsymbol a_A + \dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\boldsymbol r_{AP} + \boldsymbol\omega\times\dot{\boldsymbol r}_{AP}$. And $\dot{\boldsymbol r}_{AP} = \boldsymbol v_P - \boldsymbol v_A = \boldsymbol\omega\times\boldsymbol r_{AP}$; substituting gives the result.　∎

This is exactly the same as the transport acceleration (10.6.8) of Section 10.6, and for the same reason: a point of a rigid body is at rest relative to the body, so its acceleration is the transport acceleration when the body is taken as the moving frame, and the Coriolis term does not appear because the relative velocity is zero. Of the last two terms of Eq. (10.7.7), one comes from the speeding up of the rotation and is perpendicular to $\boldsymbol r_{AP}$; the other points towards the axis through A along **ω** and is the centripetal acceleration. For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, taking A on the axis gives $\boldsymbol a_A = 0$, and the two terms are exactly the tangential and normal accelerations of Section 10.5.

**Example 10.7.3** The gripper turns about the vertical axis through A, with angular velocity ω = 0.5 rad/s and angular acceleration α = 0.3 rad/s² at this instant; A is at rest. Find the acceleration of B.

The distance from B to the axis of rotation is {{ex10_7.rho:.2f}} m (AB is horizontal). By Eq. (10.7.7), the tangential part is $\alpha\rho$ = {{ex10_7.a_t:.3f}} m/s² and the centripetal part is $\omega^2\rho$ = {{ex10_7.a_n:.3f}} m/s²; the resultant acceleration is {{ex10_7.aB}} m/s², of magnitude {{ex10_7.aB_mag:.5f}} m/s². Program 10.7.1 checks this result by numerical differentiation.

### 10.7.8 From the point to the rigid body

The conclusions of this section link this chapter with the chapters that follow.

A point has three degrees of freedom; its motion is described by the position vector **r**(t), and its velocity and acceleration are derivatives of **r** that can be resolved in different coordinate systems. A rigid body has six degrees of freedom; its motion is described by the pose (R(t), p(t)) of an attached frame, which is the homogeneous transformation T ∈ SE(3) of Chapter 5; its velocity is described by six numbers, which is the twist of Chapter 6; and the velocity and acceleration of any point of the rigid body are given by Eqs. (10.7.4) and (10.7.7). All the tools of the kinematics of a point continue to be used in the kinematics of rigid bodies: the forward kinematics of Chapter 12 finds the pose of the end-effector frame; the Jacobian matrix of Chapter 16 maps joint velocities to the twist of the end-effector; and the recursive algorithm of Chapter 25 starts at the base and passes velocities and accelerations on link by link using Eqs. (10.7.4) and (10.7.7) and the composition theorems of Section 10.6.

Six degrees of freedom also explain the structure of industrial robot arms. To place the end-effector at any position and orientation within the workspace, a robot arm needs at least six independent joints; six-axis arms such as the UR5e are designed for exactly this. A robot with fewer than six joints can only do restricted tasks: a SCARA has four joints, and its end-effector can only point vertically down and turn in the horizontal plane, which suits assembly and sorting on a plane. With more than six joints, as in the seven-axis Panda, the extra degree of freedom can be used to avoid obstacles and singularities; this is the subject of Chapter 17.

### 10.7.9 Everyday examples

A book lying flat on a desk can be moved forwards and backwards, left and right, and can be turned on the desk: exactly three degrees of freedom. It cannot leave the desk or tilt; these three constraints remove the other three degrees of freedom. A door can only turn about its hinges: one degree of freedom. A drawer can only be pulled out along its runners: also one degree of freedom. A mobile phone held in the hand can be moved anywhere and turned to face any direction: six degrees of freedom. The inertial measurement unit in the phone uses a three-axis accelerometer and a three-axis gyroscope to measure six quantities related to position and orientation respectively. The "three translations and three rotations" often spoken of in flying an aircraft, namely forwards and backwards, left and right, up and down, together with roll, pitch and yaw, are also these six degrees of freedom.

**Engineer's notes**

- Measuring the pose of an object by vision or motion capture needs at least three non-collinear markers; in practice four to six are often used, laid out in an asymmetric pattern, so that least squares can improve the accuracy and each marker can be identified automatically. The three points must not be close to collinear, or the denominator of $\hat{\boldsymbol y}$ in Eq. (10.7.2) becomes small and measurement errors are amplified.
- Finding the angular velocity from the velocities of a few points is equivalent to solving the least-squares problem of Eq. (10.7.6). Measuring the angular velocity directly with the gyroscope of an IMU is usually more accurate; marker velocities are suited to offline cross-checks.
- To decide how many joints a task needs, count the constraints the task places on the position and orientation of the end-effector. A task that only carries and turns things in a plane needs no more than four joints (SCARA); assembly at arbitrary orientations needs six; when obstacles or joint limits must be avoided, consider seven.
- The rigid-body assumption fails at high speed, under heavy loads or with long cantilevers: elastic deformation of the links makes the end-effector depart from the position computed for rigid bodies. "Six numbers" are then no longer enough to describe the end-effector, and flexibility must be taken into account (Chapters 31 and 43).
