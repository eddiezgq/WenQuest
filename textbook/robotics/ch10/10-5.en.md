### 10.5.1 The problem

A UR5e is deburring a round hole in a flange plate. The deburring tool is mounted on the flange, and the tool tip must move round the rim of the hole, a circle of radius R = 0.10 m. The process requires the tool tip to go once round at a constant speed of 0.25 m/s; for a smooth start, it first speeds up from rest to this speed with an acceleration of 0.5 m/s². The engineer has to check two things: how large the acceleration of the tool tip is during the start, and whether it exceeds what the tool and the robot allow; and whether the acceleration jumps when the speed-up ends and the motion turns into constant speed.

The sealant-dispensing problem of Section 10.2 left a doubt of the same kind: for an even bead of sealant the tool tip must move along the ellipse at constant speed; if the speed is constant, is the acceleration zero? Clearly not, because the direction of motion is still changing. How much acceleration does changing direction need, at which point of the ellipse is it largest, and what is the highest dispensing speed allowed?

These questions have something in common: the trajectory itself is given, and what we care about is how fast the point moves along the trajectory, and the acceleration that arises because the trajectory is curved. Neither fixed coordinate axes nor the radial and transverse components about some axis answer these questions directly. The most natural approach is to resolve the velocity and acceleration along the tangent and normal of the trajectory itself.

### 10.5.2 Arc length and the unit tangent vector

Given a trajectory, choose a point on it as the starting point and choose a forward direction. The length measured along the trajectory from the starting point to any point of the trajectory, positive in the forward direction and negative in the opposite direction, is called the **arc length** of that point; it is written s and measured in metres. When a moving point travels along the trajectory, its arc length changes with time, and the function s(t) describes how the point moves along the trajectory.

**Definition 10.5.1 (Natural coordinates)** When the trajectory of a moving point is known, the way of describing the motion in which the position of the point on the trajectory is fixed by the arc length s, and the quantities of motion are resolved along the tangent, the principal normal and the binormal of the trajectory at that point, is called **natural coordinates**, also called path coordinates.

The word "natural" means that the coordinates do not depend on axes imposed from outside but are determined by the geometry of the trajectory itself. As mentioned in Section 10.2, from Christiaan Huygens and Newton to the middle of the 18th century, this was the main method used by writers on mechanics to study curvilinear motion, until Leonhard Euler introduced fixed Cartesian axes. It remains important in robotics today: the parameters of trajectory planning, machining and sealant-dispensing processes are all given along the trajectory.

The relation between arc length and velocity can be found by comparing the displacement with the arc length. In a time dt the moving point travels an arc length ds along the trajectory, and its displacement is d**r**. When the curve is smooth enough, the ratio of the lengths of the arc and the chord tends to 1 as dt → 0, so |d**r**| = |ds|, that is,

$$
|\dot s| = |\boldsymbol v| , \qquad s(t) = s(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t}\dot s\,\mathrm d\tau . \tag{10.5.1}
$$

When the point moves in the forward direction, $\dot s$ = v > 0. Regarding the position vector as a function $\boldsymbol r(s)$ of the arc length, its derivative with respect to the arc length,

$$
\boldsymbol e_t = \frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol r}{\mathrm ds} \tag{10.5.2}
$$

is a unit vector along the tangent, pointing in the forward direction (its length is |d**r**|/|ds| = 1), called the unit tangent vector. By the chain rule,

$$
\boldsymbol v = \frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol r}{\mathrm ds}\,\frac{\mathrm ds}{\mathrm dt} = \dot s\,\boldsymbol e_t . \tag{10.5.3}
$$

The velocity has only a tangential component. This is the first advantage of natural coordinates: the velocity resolves into a single term, whose size is the speed along the trajectory.

### 10.5.3 Curvature and the principal normal

The direction of the unit tangent vector changes with the position of the moving point on the trajectory. On a straight line it is the same everywhere; on a circle it turns uniformly; the more sharply the trajectory bends, the faster it turns. Measuring how sharply the trajectory bends by how fast the direction of the tangent turns per unit arc length travelled gives the curvature.

**Definition 10.5.2 (Curvature, principal normal, binormal)** Let $\boldsymbol e_t(s)$ be differentiable with respect to the arc length. Then

$$
\kappa = \left|\frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol e_t}{\mathrm ds}\right| \tag{10.5.4}
$$

is called the **curvature** of the trajectory at that point, measured in 1/m; when κ ≠ 0, 1/κ is called the **radius of curvature**, measured in m. When κ ≠ 0, the unit vectors

$$
\boldsymbol e_n = \frac{1}{\kappa}\,\frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol e_t}{\mathrm ds} , \qquad \boldsymbol e_b = \boldsymbol e_t\times\boldsymbol e_n \tag{10.5.5}
$$

are called the **principal normal** direction and the **binormal** direction of the trajectory. $\boldsymbol e_t$, $\boldsymbol e_n$, $\boldsymbol e_b$ form a right-handed orthonormal basis, the local basis of natural coordinates.

$\boldsymbol e_t$ is a unit vector, so by the corollary of Section 10.2.3 its derivative with respect to arc length is perpendicular to it, and $\boldsymbol e_n$ is indeed perpendicular to the tangent. $\boldsymbol e_n$ points to the side towards which the tangent is turning, that is, to the inside of the bend of the trajectory. The point on the principal normal at a distance 1/κ from the given point is called the centre of curvature; the circle with the centre of curvature as its centre and 1/κ as its radius touches the trajectory at that point and has the same curvature there. It is called the **osculating circle** at that point, and is the circle that fits the trajectory best near that point. Measuring the bending of a curve by the osculating circle goes back to Huygens's *Horologium oscillatorium* (The Pendulum Clock) of 1673, in which he studied the evolutes of curves, that is, the loci of their centres of curvature, in order to design an isochronous pendulum.

The two simplest examples: on a straight line $\boldsymbol e_t$ does not change and κ = 0; on a circle of radius R, travelling an arc length ds turns the tangent through an angle ds/R, so κ = 1/R; the radius of curvature is the radius of the circle, and the principal normal points to the centre. A general curve has different curvatures at different points, and its osculating circle changes accordingly.

### 10.5.4 Tangential and normal acceleration

**Theorem 10.5.1** When a moving point travels along a trajectory of curvature κ, its acceleration is

$$
\boldsymbol a = \ddot s\,\boldsymbol e_t + \kappa\dot s^2\,\boldsymbol e_n , \tag{10.5.6}
$$

and its component in the binormal direction is zero.

*Proof*　Differentiate Eq. (10.5.3), using Theorem 10.2.1: $\boldsymbol a = \ddot s\,\boldsymbol e_t + \dot s\,\dot{\boldsymbol e}_t$. $\boldsymbol e_t$ depends on s and s depends on t, so by the chain rule and Eq. (10.5.5), $\dot{\boldsymbol e}_t = \frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol e_t}{\mathrm ds}\dot s = \kappa\dot s\,\boldsymbol e_n$. Substituting gives the result.　∎

**Definition 10.5.3 (Tangential and normal acceleration)** In Eq. (10.5.6), $a_t = \ddot s$ is called the **tangential acceleration** and $a_n = \kappa\dot s^2 = \kappa v^2$ the **normal acceleration**.

Theorem 10.5.1 splits the acceleration into two parts of quite different meaning. The tangential acceleration changes only the speed; it equals the rate of change of the speed and has nothing to do with the shape of the trajectory. The normal acceleration changes only the direction of motion; it always points to the inside of the bend of the trajectory, its size is set by the curvature and the square of the speed, and it has nothing to do with whether the speed is changing. The magnitude of the total acceleration is $|\boldsymbol a| = \sqrt{a_t^2 + a_n^2}$. For a circle of radius R, $a_n = v^2/R$, which is the centripetal acceleration of uniform circular motion, the same as Eq. (3.6.8); Huygens gave this result (without proof) in *Horologium oscillatorium* in 1673, and its derivation appeared in his *De vi centrifuga* (On Centrifugal Force), published after his death in 1703.

That the normal acceleration is proportional to the square of the speed is very important in engineering. Doubling the speed makes the acceleration needed to go round a bend four times as large. This is why robots, machine tools and vehicles must all slow down at sharp bends.

The local basis of natural coordinates also turns as the point moves. Jean Frédéric Frenet, in his doctoral thesis of 1847 (published in 1852), and Joseph Alfred Serret, in 1851, each gave the law by which it changes with arc length, called the **Frenet–Serret formulas**:

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol e_t}{\mathrm ds} = \kappa\,\boldsymbol e_n , \qquad
\frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol e_n}{\mathrm ds} = -\kappa\,\boldsymbol e_t + \tau\,\boldsymbol e_b , \qquad
\frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol e_b}{\mathrm ds} = -\tau\,\boldsymbol e_n , \tag{10.5.7}
$$

where τ is called the **torsion**; it measures how far the trajectory departs from a plane, and a plane curve has τ = 0. The first formula is just the definition of $\boldsymbol e_n$ in Eq. (10.5.5); the third follows by differentiating $\boldsymbol e_b = \boldsymbol e_t\times\boldsymbol e_n$ and using orthogonality, and the second then follows by differentiating $\boldsymbol e_n = \boldsymbol e_b\times\boldsymbol e_t$. As in Sections 10.3 and 10.4, Eq. (10.5.7) can be written in the form $\mathrm d\boldsymbol e/\mathrm ds = \boldsymbol d\times\boldsymbol e$ with $\boldsymbol d = \tau\boldsymbol e_t + \kappa\boldsymbol e_b$: the local basis of natural coordinates turns with angular velocity $\dot s\,(\tau\boldsymbol e_t + \kappa\boldsymbol e_b)$. The torsion does not appear in the formula (10.5.6) for the acceleration; it comes into play only in describing how the orientation of a welding torch or cutting tool changes as it moves along a space curve, and will be used again in Chapter 22.

### 10.5.5 Curvature from velocity and acceleration

In practice the trajectory is often not given with the arc length as parameter: the ellipse of Section 10.2, for example, has time t as its parameter, and a robot's trajectory is given in terms of joint angles or an interpolation parameter. In that case there is no need to find the arc length first; the curvature and the two components of the acceleration can be found directly from the velocity and acceleration.

**Theorem 10.5.2** Let a moving point have velocity **v** ≠ 0 and acceleration **a**. Then

$$
a_t = \frac{\boldsymbol v\cdot\boldsymbol a}{v} , \qquad a_n = \frac{|\boldsymbol v\times\boldsymbol a|}{v} , \qquad \kappa = \frac{|\boldsymbol v\times\boldsymbol a|}{v^3} . \tag{10.5.8}
$$

*Proof*　By Eqs. (10.5.3) and (10.5.6), $\boldsymbol v\cdot\boldsymbol a = \dot s\,\ddot s$, and $\dot s = v$, which gives the first formula (this is Theorem 10.2.3). Also $\boldsymbol v\times\boldsymbol a = \dot s\,\boldsymbol e_t\times(\ddot s\,\boldsymbol e_t + \kappa\dot s^2\,\boldsymbol e_n) = \kappa v^3\,\boldsymbol e_b$; taking lengths gives the third formula, and $a_n = \kappa v^2$ then gives the second.　∎

For a plane curve x(u), y(u) with parameter u (u may be time or any other parameter), writing $x' = \mathrm dx/\mathrm du$ and so on, the third formula of Eq. (10.5.8) becomes

$$
\kappa = \frac{|x'y'' - y'x''|}{\big(x'^2 + y'^2\big)^{3/2}} . \tag{10.5.9}
$$

This uses the fact that curvature is a geometric property of the trajectory itself and does not depend on how fast the trajectory is travelled, so any parameter may be used in place of time. For the ellipse of Section 10.2, x = a cos u, y = b sin u (leaving out the shift of the centre), Eq. (10.5.9) gives

$$
\kappa(u) = \frac{ab}{\big(a^2\sin^2u + b^2\cos^2u\big)^{3/2}} . \tag{10.5.10}
$$

At the ends of the major axis (sin u = 0), κ = a/b², the largest value; at the ends of the minor axis (cos u = 0), κ = b/a², the smallest value. The ellipse bends most sharply at the two ends of the major axis and most gently at the two ends of the minor axis (Figure 10.5.2).

### 10.5.6 Example: deburring with the UR5e

**Example 10.5.1** Answer the questions of Section 10.5.1. The tool tip moves along a horizontal circle of radius R = {{ex10_5.R:.2f}} m; starting from rest, it speeds up with tangential acceleration $a_t$ = {{ex10_5.at0:.1f}} m/s² until it reaches the speed {{ex10_5.vmax:.2f}} m/s, and then moves uniformly (at constant speed). Find the tangential, normal and total accelerations at the middle of the speed-up and at its end.

The speed-up lasts {{ex10_5.vmax:.2f}}/{{ex10_5.at0:.1f}} = {{ex10_5.t_acc:.1f}} s and covers an arc length $\frac12 a_t t^2$ = {{ex10_5.s_acc:.4f}} m, that is, {{ex10_5.s_acc_deg:.1f}}° of the circle. The curvature of the circle is κ = 1/R = 10 m⁻¹. By Eq. (10.5.6),

- at the middle of the speed-up (t = 0.25 s, v = 0.125 m/s): $a_t$ = {{ex10_5.mid_at:.1f}} m/s², $a_n = v^2/R$ = {{ex10_5.mid_an:.5f}} m/s², |**a**| = {{ex10_5.mid_a:.4f}} m/s², and the acceleration is turned {{ex10_5.mid_ang:.2f}}° away from the tangent;
- just before the speed-up ends (v = 0.25 m/s): $a_t$ = {{ex10_5.end_at:.1f}} m/s², $a_n$ = {{ex10_5.end_an:.3f}} m/s², |**a**| = {{ex10_5.end_a:.4f}} m/s², turned {{ex10_5.end_ang:.2f}}° away from the tangent;
- after the speed-up has ended: $a_t$ = 0, $a_n$ = {{ex10_5.end_an:.3f}} m/s², |**a**| = {{ex10_5.end_an:.3f}} m/s², pointing to the centre.

Figure 10.5.1(b) shows the three quantities against time. Two points deserve attention. First, at the end of the speed-up the total acceleration is much larger than the tangential acceleration: the normal acceleration grows as v² during the speed-up, and by the end it already exceeds the tangential acceleration. If the acceleration limits of the robot were checked against the tangential acceleration alone, the actual acceleration would be 1.6 times the value checked. Second, at t = 0.5 s the tangential acceleration jumps suddenly from 0.5 m/s² to 0, the total acceleration jumps from about 0.80 m/s² to 0.625 m/s², and the direction of the acceleration also changes suddenly from about 51° off the tangent to pointing at the centre. A jump in acceleration means an infinite jerk: the forces on the tool and the robot change suddenly and excite vibration. The S-curve velocity profile of Chapter 22 is designed precisely to remove such jumps.

The results agree with those above, and the curvature found differs from 1/R by no more than the error of numerical differentiation.

### 10.5.7 Example: the highest speed for dispensing sealant at constant speed

**Example 10.5.2** The sealant ellipse of Section 10.2 has semi-major axis a = 0.12 m and semi-minor axis b = 0.08 m. The tool tip is now to move along the ellipse at a constant speed $v_0$. If the acceleration must not exceed {{ex10_5.an_lim:.1f}} m/s², what is the highest $v_0$? How long does one loop take?

The speed is constant, so $a_t$ = 0 and the acceleration has only the normal component $a_n = \kappa v_0^2$, whose largest value occurs where the curvature is largest, that is, at the ends of the major axis. By Eq. (10.5.10), $\kappa_{\max}$ = a/b² = {{ex10_5.kmax:.2f}} m⁻¹, with radius of curvature {{ex10_5.rmin:.4f}} m; $\kappa_{\min}$ = b/a² = {{ex10_5.kmin:.4f}} m⁻¹, with radius of curvature {{ex10_5.rmax:.2f}} m. From $\kappa_{\max}v_0^2 \le 1.0$ m/s²,

$$
v_0 \le \sqrt{\frac{a_{\max}}{\kappa_{\max}}} = {{ex10_5.v0:.4f}}\ \mathrm{m/s} .
$$

The perimeter of an ellipse has no elementary expression. Program 10.5.1 integrates $\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{a^2\sin^2u + b^2\cos^2u}\,\mathrm du$ numerically by the trapezoidal rule, obtaining the perimeter L = {{ex10_5.L:.5f}} m, and checks it against the approximation formula of Srinivasa Ramanujan; the two differ by less than $10^{-9}$ m. Going once round at $v_0$ takes {{ex10_5.T_cycle:.3f}} s.

Compare this with the result of Section 10.2. There, with the parameter ωt increasing uniformly, one loop took 4 s, the speed varied between {{ex10_2.vmin:.4f}} m/s and {{ex10_2.vmax:.4f}} m/s, and the thickness of the bead varied by 50%. Here, moving at constant speed, the bead is even, one loop takes only about 2.75 s, and the largest acceleration is, if anything, under clear control. The price is that the controller must interpolate by arc length rather than by the parameter u, that is, for each time t it must find u from s = $v_0$t, which requires a numerical solution; Chapter 22 discusses methods of this kind of "reparametrization by arc length".

As a check, compute for the parametrization of Section 10.2 at t = 0.5 s by Eq. (10.5.8): the curvature is κ = {{ex10_5.k1:.4f}} m⁻¹, $a_t$ = {{ex10_5.at1}} m/s², $a_n$ = {{ex10_5.an1}} m/s², and $\sqrt{a_t^2 + a_n^2}$ = {{ex10_5.a1}} m/s², the same as |**a**| in Example 10.2.1; $a_t$ is also the same as the rate of change of the speed found there by Theorem 10.2.3.

Each of the four coordinate systems has its strengths. The basis of Cartesian coordinates is fixed, so differentiation is simplest; they are the common language of controllers, simulation and all numerical computation. Cylindrical coordinates suit mechanisms with one axis of rotation, such as SCARA robots, cylindrical robots and turntables; spherical coordinates suit devices that look outwards from a point, such as gimbals, lidars and tracking antennas; natural coordinates suit problems in which the trajectory is given and what matters is the speed along it and the turning, such as machining, sealant dispensing, welding and vehicles going round bends. Which to choose depends on which basis matches the geometry of the problem. Whichever is chosen, the formulas of curvilinear coordinates can all be obtained by the same method: write down the angular velocity of the local basis, and the derivative of a basis vector is the angular velocity crossed with the basis vector.

### 10.5.9 Everyday examples

A car taking a motorway bend of radius 500 m at 108 km/h (30 m/s) has a normal acceleration of 30²/500 = 1.8 m/s², about 18% of the acceleration due to gravity, and the passengers clearly feel pushed outwards. The smaller the radius of the bend, the lower the speed allowed; road design standards set the minimum radius of bends for each class of road from exactly the normal acceleration and the friction of the road surface.

More interesting is the junction between a straight and a bend. If a straight is joined directly to a circular arc, the curvature jumps from 0 to 1/R at the junction, and at constant speed the normal acceleration jumps with it. This is the same kind of problem as the jump in acceleration in Example 10.5.1: the driver has to wrench the steering wheel round, and the passengers feel a sideways jolt. So railways and roads insert a **transition curve** between a straight line and a circular curve, usually a clothoid, whose curvature is proportional to its arc length, so that the normal acceleration builds up gradually. The vertical loop of a roller coaster is not a true circle either, but a clothoid-shaped loop, narrow at the top and wide at the bottom, whose curvature changes gradually, so as to avoid an excessive normal acceleration at the bottom of the loop.

The local bases of the coordinate systems do turn, but they are only directions we choose for resolving vectors; the reference frame itself has not moved. The next section studies the case in which the reference frame itself turns.

**Engineer's notes**

- To check the acceleration limits of a robot's end-effector, use the total acceleration $\sqrt{a_t^2 + a_n^2}$, not just the acceleration along the path. On circular arcs of small radius and at corners, the normal acceleration is often the main part.
- The normal acceleration is proportional to the square of the speed. Raising the process speed by 20% raises the normal acceleration at corners by 44%. Before speeding up, recheck the acceleration, vibration and accuracy at places of small radius of curvature.
- A path whose curvature is discontinuous (a straight line joined directly to a circular arc) makes the normal acceleration jump. For processes sensitive to acceleration (such as high-speed sealant dispensing or laser cutting), insert a transition curve between the straight line and the arc, or slow down at the junction.
- When estimating the curvature from discrete path points, one can draw the circle through three neighbouring points; the curvature is the reciprocal of the radius of this circle. If the points are too close together, measurement errors make the estimated curvature fluctuate wildly; smooth first, then compute.
