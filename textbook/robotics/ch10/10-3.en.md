### 10.3.1 The problem

The SCARA robot in the parts library has two joints that turn about vertical axes: joint 1 on the base and joint 2 at the end of the upper arm, with horizontal arms of lengths $l_1$ = 0.35 m and $l_2$ = 0.25 m (Section 12.3). Seen from above, when joint 1 turns, the whole arm turns about the base and the direction in which the end-effector lies changes; when joint 2 turns, the forearm bends or straightens relative to the upper arm and the distance from the end-effector to the axis of the base changes. The two most natural numbers for describing the position of the end-effector are exactly "how far from the axis" and "in which direction", not the Cartesian coordinates x and y.

Now imagine the SCARA carrying a part: joint 1 turns at 1 rad/s while joint 2 straightens the arm, so that the end-effector reaches outwards. How hard must the gripper grip to hold the part? That depends on the acceleration of the part. The components $\ddot x$, $\ddot y$ of the acceleration in Cartesian coordinates are components along fixed directions of the workshop and have nothing to do with the configuration of the arm. From them we cannot see which part comes from the turning and which from the extending, nor in which direction the force acts on the finger faces of the gripper (which turn with the arm). We would like to resolve the acceleration along two directions that turn with the arm: "away from the axis of rotation" and "round the axis of rotation".

In this section we shall see that the acceleration resolved in this way contains a term $2\dot\rho\dot\varphi$: when the arm extends and turns at the same time, the end-effector has a transverse acceleration even if the speed of extension and the angular velocity of the turning are both constant. This term is the first appearance of the Coriolis acceleration of Section 10.6.

### 10.3.2 Cylindrical and polar coordinates

**Definition 10.3.1 (Cylindrical coordinates)** In a Cartesian frame {s}, the **cylindrical coordinates** (ρ, φ, z) of a point P are defined by

$$
x = \rho\cos\varphi , \qquad y = \rho\sin\varphi , \qquad z = z \tag{10.3.1}
$$

where ρ ≥ 0 is the distance from P to the z axis; φ is the angle between the x axis and the projection of P on the xy plane, called the azimuthal angle, positive from the x axis towards the y axis (anticlockwise seen from above) and measured in radians; and z is the same as in Cartesian coordinates. When only points in a plane are considered (z does not appear), (ρ, φ) are called **polar coordinates**.

Conversely, the cylindrical coordinates are found from the Cartesian coordinates by

$$
\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} , \qquad \varphi = \mathrm{atan2}(y,\ x) . \tag{10.3.2}
$$

The function atan2(y, x) gives the azimuthal angle of the point (x, y), with values in (−π, π]; it decides the quadrant from the signs of x and y, whereas arctan(y/x) can only give values in (−π/2, π/2) and is off by π in the second and third quadrants. In programs, always use atan2 to find an azimuthal angle.

The "coordinate lines" of cylindrical coordinates are as follows: if only ρ varies, the point moves along a horizontal ray starting from the z axis; if only φ varies, it moves along a horizontal circle with the z axis as its axis; if only z varies, it moves along a vertical line. At every point the three families of coordinate lines are mutually perpendicular, so cylindrical coordinates are orthogonal curvilinear coordinates. Figure 10.3.1 shows the cylindrical coordinates of a point P.

Describing the position of a point by cylindrical coordinates goes back to the surveying tradition of locating a point by a distance and a bearing; in his *Method of Fluxions*, written around 1671 and published in 1736, Isaac Newton already used polar coordinates as one way of representing curves. In robotics, cylindrical coordinates have a direct mechanical counterpart: in the early 1960s the American company AMF (American Machine and Foundry) brought out the Versatran industrial robot, made up of a column, a horizontal arm that could be raised, lowered and extended, and a rotating base. The position of the end of the arm is fixed by exactly three numbers, the swing angle, the extension and the height, and robots of this structure are therefore called cylindrical robots.

### 10.3.3 The local basis

In Cartesian coordinates, velocity and acceleration are resolved along three fixed coordinate axes. In cylindrical coordinates we resolve them instead along the directions of the three families of coordinate lines at P.

**Definition 10.3.2 (Local basis)** At a point P, the unit vectors in the directions of increasing ρ, increasing φ and increasing z are written $\boldsymbol e_\rho$, $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$, $\boldsymbol e_z$, and are called the **local basis** of cylindrical coordinates at P.

Their components in {s} follow from Eq. (10.3.1). The direction of increasing ρ is the direction of $\partial r_s/\partial\rho = (\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi, 0)^{\mathsf T}$; the direction of increasing φ is that of $\partial r_s/\partial\varphi = (-\rho\sin\varphi, \rho\cos\varphi, 0)^{\mathsf T}$, whose length is ρ; the direction of increasing z is $(0, 0, 1)^{\mathsf T}$. Dividing each by its length gives

$$
\boldsymbol e_\rho = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi \\ \sin\varphi \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad
\boldsymbol e_\varphi = \begin{pmatrix} -\sin\varphi \\ \cos\varphi \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} , \qquad
\boldsymbol e_z = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{10.3.3}
$$

(For brevity, a vector and its components in {s} are written with the same symbol here.) It can be checked directly that the three have unit length, are mutually orthogonal and satisfy $\boldsymbol e_\rho\times\boldsymbol e_\varphi = \boldsymbol e_z$, so they form a right-handed set. The position vector of P resolves particularly simply on the local basis:

$$
\boldsymbol r = \rho\,\boldsymbol e_\rho + z\,\boldsymbol e_z . \tag{10.3.4}
$$

There is no component along $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$. Beginners often write $\boldsymbol r = \rho\boldsymbol e_\rho + \varphi\boldsymbol e_\varphi + z\boldsymbol e_z$, which is wrong: φ is an angle, not a length along $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$; all the information about the azimuthal angle is hidden in the direction of $\boldsymbol e_\rho$.

Arranging the three basis vectors as the columns of a matrix and comparing with the elementary rotations of Chapter 4, we see at once that

$$
\big(\boldsymbol e_\rho\ \ \boldsymbol e_\varphi\ \ \boldsymbol e_z\big) = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat{\boldsymbol z},\ \varphi) . \tag{10.3.5}
$$

By the geometric meaning of the columns of a rotation matrix (Section 4.2), the local basis is the set of coordinate axes obtained by turning the three axes of {s} through φ about the z axis. What distinguishes it fundamentally from the Cartesian axes is that it changes with the azimuthal angle of P. When P moves, φ changes with time, and so the local basis turns with time. The three arrows in Figure 10.3.1 turn together with the azimuthal angle of P.

### 10.3.4 Derivatives of the local basis

**Theorem 10.3.1** When a point P moves, the time derivatives of the local basis of cylindrical coordinates are

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol e}_\rho = \dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi , \qquad
\dot{\boldsymbol e}_\varphi = -\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\rho , \qquad
\dot{\boldsymbol e}_z = 0 . \tag{10.3.6}
$$

*Proof*　By Eq. (10.3.3), the components of $\boldsymbol e_\rho$ depend only on φ, and φ depends on t. By the chain rule,

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol e}_\rho = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi \\ \sin\varphi \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \dot\varphi\begin{pmatrix} -\sin\varphi \\ \cos\varphi \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi ,
\qquad
\dot{\boldsymbol e}_\varphi = \dot\varphi\begin{pmatrix} -\cos\varphi \\ -\sin\varphi \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = -\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\rho .
$$

The components of $\boldsymbol e_z$ are constant, so its derivative is zero. Differentiating the components is legitimate here because they are read in the fixed frame {s} (Theorem 10.2.2).　∎

Theorem 10.3.1 has another, more illuminating proof, which reveals the geometric meaning of Eq. (10.3.6). By Eq. (10.3.5), the orientation of the three axes of the local basis relative to {s} is $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat{\boldsymbol z}, \varphi(t))$: the local basis as a whole turns about the z axis with angular velocity $\dot\varphi\,\hat{\boldsymbol z}$. By the result of Section 3.6, the rate of change of a unit vector that turns with a rigid body equals the angular velocity crossed with that vector (the derivation of Eq. (3.6.9)), so

$$
\dot{\boldsymbol e} = \dot\varphi\,\hat{\boldsymbol z}\times\boldsymbol e , \qquad \boldsymbol e = \boldsymbol e_\rho,\ \boldsymbol e_\varphi,\ \boldsymbol e_z . \tag{10.3.7}
$$

Then $\hat{\boldsymbol z}\times\boldsymbol e_\rho = \boldsymbol e_\varphi$, $\hat{\boldsymbol z}\times\boldsymbol e_\varphi = -\boldsymbol e_\rho$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol z}\times\boldsymbol e_z = 0$ give Eq. (10.3.6). The advantage of this view is that it generalizes: for any orthonormal basis that turns as a point moves, once its angular velocity is known, the derivative of every basis vector is "the angular velocity crossed with the basis vector". The spherical coordinates of Section 10.4, the natural coordinates of Section 10.5 and the rotating reference frames of Section 10.6 will all be treated by this method.

The directions of the two derivatives can also be understood intuitively. $\boldsymbol e_\rho$ is a unit vector whose length does not change, so by the corollary of Section 10.2.3 its derivative is perpendicular to it and can only lie along $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ (or $\boldsymbol e_z$); as the azimuthal angle of P increases, $\boldsymbol e_\rho$ turns towards the side of $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$, and the rate of turning is $\dot\varphi$. Likewise $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ turns towards the side of $-\boldsymbol e_\rho$.

### 10.3.5 Radial and transverse components of velocity and acceleration

**Theorem 10.3.2** When a point P moves with cylindrical coordinates (ρ(t), φ(t), z(t)), its velocity and acceleration are

$$
\boldsymbol v = \dot\rho\,\boldsymbol e_\rho + \rho\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi + \dot z\,\boldsymbol e_z , \tag{10.3.8}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol a = \big(\ddot\rho - \rho\dot\varphi^2\big)\,\boldsymbol e_\rho + \big(\rho\ddot\varphi + 2\dot\rho\dot\varphi\big)\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi + \ddot z\,\boldsymbol e_z . \tag{10.3.9}
$$

*Proof*　Differentiate Eq. (10.3.4), using Theorem 10.2.1 and Theorem 10.3.1:

$$
\boldsymbol v = \dot\rho\,\boldsymbol e_\rho + \rho\,\dot{\boldsymbol e}_\rho + \dot z\,\boldsymbol e_z + z\,\dot{\boldsymbol e}_z = \dot\rho\,\boldsymbol e_\rho + \rho\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi + \dot z\,\boldsymbol e_z .
$$

Differentiating Eq. (10.3.8) term by term again:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\dot\rho\,\boldsymbol e_\rho\big) &= \ddot\rho\,\boldsymbol e_\rho + \dot\rho\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi , \\
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\rho\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi\big) &= \dot\rho\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi + \rho\ddot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi - \rho\dot\varphi^2\,\boldsymbol e_\rho , \\
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\dot z\,\boldsymbol e_z\big) &= \ddot z\,\boldsymbol e_z .
\end{aligned}
$$

Adding the three and collecting terms along the local basis gives Eq. (10.3.9).　∎

**Definition 10.3.3 (Radial and transverse components)** The components of the velocity and acceleration along $\boldsymbol e_\rho$ are called the **radial components**, and those along $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ the **transverse components**; they are written $v_\rho$, $v_\varphi$, $a_\rho$, $a_\varphi$.

The components of the velocity are easy to understand: the radial component $\dot\rho$ is the rate of change of the distance from the axis; the transverse component $\rho\dot\varphi$ is the velocity due to turning about the axis, the same as $v = \rho\dot\theta$ of Section 3.6 (Eq. (3.6.1)). The four terms of the acceleration each have their own origin and deserve to be explained one by one.

- $\ddot\rho$: the second derivative of the distance from the axis, that is, the acceleration of the radial "extending" motion itself.
- $-\rho\dot\varphi^2$: the centripetal acceleration towards the axis, the same as in Eq. (3.6.8). Even with ρ constant and a steady turn, the direction of the velocity keeps changing.
- $\rho\ddot\varphi$: the transverse acceleration due to the turning speeding up or slowing down.
- $2\dot\rho\dot\varphi$: this is the least obvious term. The proof shows that it is made up of two equal parts. The first comes from the radial velocity $\dot\rho\,\boldsymbol e_\rho$: while the point is extending, $\boldsymbol e_\rho$ itself is turning, so the direction of the radial velocity changes, which produces $\dot\rho\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi$. The second comes from the transverse velocity $\rho\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi$: the point moves further and further from the axis, so the same angular velocity corresponds to an ever larger transverse speed $\rho\dot\varphi$, which produces another $\dot\rho\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi$. The two parts have the same direction and together give $2\dot\rho\dot\varphi$.

The last term is the same thing as the Coriolis acceleration $2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}}$ of Section 10.6: if the reference frame that turns with the arm is taken as the "moving frame", then $\dot\rho\,\boldsymbol e_\rho$ is the velocity of the point relative to the moving frame, $\dot\varphi\hat{\boldsymbol z}$ is the angular velocity of the moving frame, and $2\dot\varphi\hat{\boldsymbol z}\times\dot\rho\boldsymbol e_\rho = 2\dot\rho\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi$. The two sources of the factor 2 will also reappear there in a general form.

The two terms of the transverse component can also be combined into a compact form:

$$
a_\varphi = \rho\ddot\varphi + 2\dot\rho\dot\varphi = \frac{1}{\rho}\,\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\rho^2\dot\varphi\big) . \tag{10.3.10}
$$

This can be checked by expanding the right-hand side. The quantity $\rho^2\dot\varphi/2$ is the area swept out by the position vector per unit time. Equation (10.3.10) says: **the transverse acceleration is zero if and only if the position vector sweeps out area at a constant rate**. In Section 10.3.9 we shall see that this is precisely the kinematic content of Kepler's second law.

### 10.3.6 Example: cylindrical coordinates of the SCARA end-effector

The cylindrical coordinates of the SCARA end-effector are simply related to the joint angles. Take the SCARA base frame {s}, with its origin on the axis of joint 1 and its z axis vertically up; the joint angles θ₁ and θ₂ are both positive anticlockwise seen from above, θ₂ being the angle of the forearm relative to the upper arm (Section 12.3). The base, joint 2 and the end-effector form a triangle with sides $l_1$ and $l_2$ and included angle π − θ₂. By the law of cosines,

$$
\rho^2 = l_1^2 + l_2^2 + 2l_1l_2\cos\theta_2 . \tag{10.3.11}
$$

The azimuthal angle of the end-effector equals the azimuthal angle θ₁ of the upper arm plus the angle β by which the end-effector is turned away from the upper arm:

$$
\varphi = \theta_1 + \beta , \qquad \beta = \mathrm{atan2}\big(l_2\sin\theta_2,\ l_1 + l_2\cos\theta_2\big) . \tag{10.3.12}
$$

Equation (10.3.11) says that ρ is determined by joint 2 alone; Eq. (10.3.12) says that joint 1 changes only φ. Differentiating with respect to time:

$$
\dot\rho = -\frac{l_1l_2\sin\theta_2}{\rho}\,\dot\theta_2 , \qquad
\dot\varphi = \dot\theta_1 + \frac{l_2^2 + l_1l_2\cos\theta_2}{\rho^2}\,\dot\theta_2 , \tag{10.3.13}
$$

where the first follows by differentiating both sides of Eq. (10.3.11), and in the second the coefficient is dβ/dθ₂, found from the formula for the derivative of atan2. Differentiating once more gives $\ddot\rho$ and $\ddot\varphi$; Program 10.3.1 writes out these two expressions and checks them by numerical differentiation.

**Example 10.3.1** At some instant the SCARA has θ₁ = 30°, θ₂ = 90°, joint rates $\dot\theta_1$ = 1.0 rad/s and $\dot\theta_2$ = −1.5 rad/s, and zero angular acceleration at both joints; at the same time the ball screw descends at 0.1 m/s. Find the cylindrical coordinates of the end-effector and the radial, transverse and vertical components of its velocity and acceleration.

By Eqs. (10.3.11) to (10.3.13), ρ = {{ex10_3.rho:.5f}} m, β = {{ex10_3.beta_deg:.2f}}°, φ = {{ex10_3.phi_deg:.2f}}°, and

$$
\dot\rho = {{ex10_3.rhod}}\ \mathrm{m/s} , \quad \ddot\rho = {{ex10_3.rhodd}}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} , \quad
\dot\varphi = {{ex10_3.phid}}\ \mathrm{rad/s} , \quad \ddot\varphi = {{ex10_3.phidd}}\ \mathrm{rad/s^2} .
$$

$\dot\theta_2$ = −1.5 rad/s straightens the forearm and ρ increases, so $\dot\rho > 0$; the forearm turns clockwise relative to the upper arm and cancels part of the turning of joint 1, so $\dot\varphi$ is smaller than $\dot\theta_1$. Note that although both joints turn at constant rates, neither of the second derivatives of ρ and φ is zero: uniform motion in joint space is not uniform in cylindrical coordinates.

By Eq. (10.3.8), the three components of the velocity are

$$
v_\rho = \dot\rho = {{ex10_3.v_rho}} , \qquad v_\varphi = \rho\dot\varphi = {{ex10_3.v_phi}} , \qquad v_z = \dot z = {{ex10_3.v_z:.1f}} \qquad (\mathrm{m/s}) ,
$$

and the speed is {{ex10_3.speed}} m/s. By Eq. (10.3.9), the radial and transverse components of the acceleration are

$$
\begin{aligned}
a_\rho &= \ddot\rho - \rho\dot\varphi^2 = ({{ex10_3.rhodd}}) + ({{ex10_3.a_cent}}) = {{ex10_3.a_rho}}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} , \\
a_\varphi &= \rho\ddot\varphi + 2\dot\rho\dot\varphi = ({{ex10_3.a_ang}}) + {{ex10_3.a_cor}} = {{ex10_3.a_phi}}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} ,
\end{aligned}
$$

and the vertical component is zero (the ball screw moves at constant speed). The magnitude of the acceleration is {{ex10_3.a_mag}} m/s². We see that in the transverse acceleration the term $2\dot\rho\dot\varphi$ is the largest, twice as large as the term $\rho\ddot\varphi$ due to the turning slowing down. Going only by the intuition that "both joints are turning at constant rates", it is easy to think that the acceleration of the end-effector has only the centripetal term, and so to underestimate the sideways force on the gripper.

Program 10.3.1 checks these results in another way: it writes the Cartesian coordinates of the end-effector as functions of time from the forward kinematics of the SCARA, finds $\dot r_s$ and $\ddot r_s$ numerically by the central difference of Eq. (10.2.11), and takes their dot products with $\boldsymbol e_\rho$ and $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$; the radial and transverse components obtained agree with the results above. The acceleration in Cartesian coordinates is ({{ex10_3.ax:.5f}}, {{ex10_3.ay:.5f}}, 0) m/s²: it is the same arrow, only read along the fixed x and y axes. ### 10.3.7 Example: a cylindrical robot extending its arm

**Example 10.3.2** The arm of a cylindrical robot extends at a constant $\dot\rho$ = {{ex10_3.rd2:.1f}} m/s while swinging at a constant $\dot\varphi$ = {{ex10_3.pd2:.1f}} rad/s. What is the acceleration of the end-effector when the arm has reached ρ = {{ex10_3.r2:.1f}} m? If the gripper together with the part it holds has a mass of {{ex10_3.m:.0f}} kg, what transverse force must the arm exert on it?

With $\ddot\rho = \ddot\varphi = 0$, Eq. (10.3.9) gives

$$
a_\rho = -\rho\dot\varphi^2 = {{ex10_3.a2_rho:.1f}}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} , \qquad a_\varphi = 2\dot\rho\dot\varphi = {{ex10_3.a2_phi:.1f}}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} ,
$$

with magnitude {{ex10_3.a2_mag:.4f}} m/s². The transverse acceleration must be supplied by a sideways force from the arm, of magnitude $m\,a_\varphi$ = {{ex10_3.F_side:.1f}} N, pointing towards the side to which the arm is turning. This force is borne by the guides of the arm or by the telescopic mechanism, where it shows up as a side load inside the telescopic mechanism: when the arm extends without turning, or turns steadily without extending, there is no such side load; it appears only when the arm turns and extends at once. It must be taken into account when designing the guides of cylindrical robots and telescopic arms.

### 10.3.8 Singularity of the coordinates

Cylindrical coordinates break down on the z axis (ρ = 0): there the azimuthal angle φ is undefined, and so are $\boldsymbol e_\rho$ and $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$. This is not a difficulty of computation but a defect of the coordinates themselves: a point can leave the z axis in any direction, and all the "directions" labelled by φ come together in a single point on the axis.

The defect already shows itself near the z axis. By Eq. (10.3.8), $\dot\varphi = v_\varphi/\rho$: when a point sweeps past close to the z axis with a finite transverse velocity, ρ is small and $\dot\varphi$ is large. If a cylindrical robot is to move its end-effector at constant speed along a straight line passing close to its column, the angular velocity of the swing joint rises sharply at the nearest point. The SCARA in the parts library does not have this problem, because the distance from its end-effector to the axis of joint 1 is never less than $|l_1 - l_2|$ = 0.10 m, so the end-effector cannot reach the axis. But the same phenomenon will appear in a sharper form in the spherical coordinates of Section 10.4, and will be given an engineering name there.

Another practical problem is the jump in the azimuthal angle. atan2 takes values in (−π, π], so when a point goes once round the z axis, φ jumps from π to −π at ±π, and numerical differentiation gives a huge spurious $\dot\varphi$. The remedy is to "unwrap" the angle: if the difference between the φ of two neighbouring samples exceeds π, add or subtract 2π.

### 10.3.9 Everyday examples

When a figure skater spinning on the spot draws the outstretched arms in to the chest, the spin becomes noticeably faster. While the arms are drawn in, ρ of the hands decreases and $\dot\rho < 0$; the transverse acceleration $a_\varphi = \frac1\rho\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\rho^2\dot\varphi)$ has to be supplied by the force of the arm muscles. If this force is small, $\rho^2\dot\varphi$ of the hands stays nearly constant: when ρ falls to half, $\dot\varphi$ rises to four times. This is the kinematic expression of the conservation of angular momentum, which will be discussed again in Chapter 23.

A more famous example comes from astronomy. In his *Astronomia nova* (New Astronomy), published in 1609, Johannes Kepler concluded from the observations of Tycho Brahe that the line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times; this is Kepler's second law. By Eq. (10.3.10), a constant rate of sweeping out area, $\rho^2\dot\varphi/2$, is equivalent to zero transverse acceleration, that is, to the acceleration of the planet lying along the line joining it to the Sun. It was from exactly this point that Newton inferred that the force on a planet points towards the Sun, a centripetal force. Here a kinematic fact became one of the key clues to the discovery of universal gravitation.

**Engineer's notes**

- For SCARA robots, cylindrical robots and arms on a turntable, the motion of the end-effector is most conveniently described in cylindrical coordinates: the swing angle φ is set by joint 1, the distance ρ from the axis by the other horizontal joints, and the height z by the vertical axis.
- When all the joints turn at constant rates, the end-effector in general neither moves at constant speed nor is free of acceleration. To check the acceleration limits of the end-effector, compute by Eq. (10.3.9) or by differentiating the forward kinematics; looking only at the angular accelerations of the joints is not enough.
- The transverse acceleration $2\dot\rho\dot\varphi$ that appears when swinging and extending at once puts a side load on the telescopic mechanism, and can also make a thin sheet held by a suction cup slip sideways. When the cycle time allows, "extend first, then swing" is smoother than doing both at once.
- When finding an azimuthal angle with atan2 and differentiating it numerically, always unwrap the jumps at ±π first; otherwise, at the instant φ passes ±180°, a huge spurious angular velocity is computed, which can trip the overspeed protection of the controller.
