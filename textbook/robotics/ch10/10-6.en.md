### 10.6.1 The problem

The final-assembly area of the digital factory has a turntable 3 m in diameter, used to turn large fixtures to different workstations. To save time, AGVs often drive onto the turntable while it is turning and cross its centre to deliver material. A guide line is painted along a radius of the turntable; an AGV follows it in a straight line at a constant speed of 0.4 m/s, while the turntable turns anticlockwise (seen from above) with an angular velocity of 0.5 rad/s.

The AGV carries an inertial measurement unit whose accelerometer measures the acceleration of the body. When the AGV is 1.0 m from the centre of the turntable, the accelerometer reads 0.25 m/s² backwards and 0.40 m/s² to the left. Seen on the turntable, the AGV is plainly driving in a straight line at constant speed, so why is there an acceleration at all, and why is it mainly sideways? This sideways acceleration has to be supplied by the friction between the wheels and the turntable; if the friction is not enough, the AGV will slide off the guide line to the right. The engineer needs a formula that gives the true acceleration of the AGV from its motion relative to the turntable and the rotation of the turntable itself.

Problems of this kind are everywhere in robotics. A robot arm on a turntable, a robot on a ship or a vehicle, every piece of equipment on the Earth turning about its axis, and the motion of the next link as seen in the frame of one link of a robot arm, all amount to "describing motion in a rotating reference frame while needing to know its velocity and acceleration relative to a stationary reference frame". Equation (10.1.3) in Section 10.1 has already shown that when the reference frame moves, differentiation produces new terms. This section derives these terms in full.

### 10.6.2 Fixed frame, moving frame and the three motions

**Definition 10.6.1 (Fixed and moving reference frames)** When studying the motion of a point, two reference frames are chosen: one is agreed to be at rest and is called the **fixed reference frame**, or fixed frame for short; the other moves relative to the fixed frame and is called the **moving reference frame**, or moving frame for short.

In this section the fixed frame is written {s} and is usually the ground; the moving frame is written {p}, for example the turntable. The motion of the moving frame is described in two parts: the position $\boldsymbol p_{O'}(t)$ of its origin O′ in the fixed frame, and its orientation $R(t) = R_{sp}(t)$. By Section 6.2, the rate of change of the orientation gives the angular velocity **Ω** of the moving frame: $\dot RR^{\mathsf T} = [\Omega_s]$ (Eq. (6.2.3)); the rate of change of **Ω**, $\boldsymbol\alpha = \dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}$, is called the **angular acceleration** of the moving frame, in rad/s². By convention the angular velocity of a reference frame is written with a capital **Ω**, to distinguish it from the **ω** of a rigid body's own rotation.

The motion of one and the same moving point P can be looked at in three ways (Figure 10.6.1).

**Definition 10.6.2 (Absolute, relative and transport motion)** The motion of the moving point relative to the fixed frame is called its **absolute motion**, and the corresponding velocity and acceleration are called the absolute velocity $\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{abs}}$ and the absolute acceleration $\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{abs}}$. The motion of the moving point relative to the moving frame is called its **relative motion**, that is, the motion seen by an observer standing on the moving frame; the corresponding velocity and acceleration are called the **relative velocity** $\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}}$ and the **relative acceleration** $\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{rel}}$. The motion of the moving frame relative to the fixed frame is called the **transport motion**. At a given instant, the point of the moving frame that coincides with the moving point is called the **coincident point**; the velocity and acceleration of the coincident point, taken as a fixed point of the moving frame, are called the **transport velocity** $\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{tr}}$ and the **transport acceleration** $\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{tr}}$ of the moving point at that instant.

"Transport" means that the moving point is "carried along" by the moving frame. Take the turntable as an example: the AGV is the moving point and the turntable is the moving frame. The absolute motion is the motion of the AGV relative to the ground; the relative motion is the straight-line motion of the AGV along the guide line; the transport motion is the rotation of the turntable; the coincident point is the point of the turntable that the AGV is standing on at this instant, which moves in a circle with the turntable, and its velocity and acceleration are the transport velocity and the transport acceleration. Note that the coincident point is not one fixed point: an instant later the AGV has reached another place on the guide line, and the coincident point has become another point of the turntable.

A word about notation. Chinese textbooks of theoretical mechanics often write the subscripts of the absolute, relative and transport quantities as a, r and e (e from the French *entraînement*, "carrying along"), for example $\boldsymbol v_a = \boldsymbol v_e + \boldsymbol v_r$. In this book subscripts already denote frames ($p_a$ means components in {a}), so to avoid confusion we use the three abbreviations abs, rel and tr instead; the Coriolis acceleration is still written $\boldsymbol a_C$. The two notations correspond as $\boldsymbol a_a \leftrightarrow \boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{abs}}$, $\boldsymbol a_r \leftrightarrow \boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{rel}}$, $\boldsymbol a_e \leftrightarrow \boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{tr}}$.

### 10.6.3 The derivative of a vector in two reference frames

The starting point of everything is the relation between the rates of change of the same vector in two reference frames. Section 10.2.3 has already pointed out that the derivative of a vector function depends on the reference frame: an observer in the fixed frame and an observer in the moving frame, comparing the same arrow at two instants, obtain different differences. To tell them apart, we write the derivative in the fixed frame as $\mathrm d\boldsymbol q/\mathrm dt$ and the derivative in the moving frame as $\tilde{\mathrm d}\boldsymbol q/\mathrm dt$; the latter is called the **relative derivative**. Concretely, let $\hat{\boldsymbol e}_1$, $\hat{\boldsymbol e}_2$, $\hat{\boldsymbol e}_3$ be Cartesian axes fixed to the moving frame and $\boldsymbol q = q_1\hat{\boldsymbol e}_1 + q_2\hat{\boldsymbol e}_2 + q_3\hat{\boldsymbol e}_3$. To the observer in the moving frame the axes do not move, so

$$
\frac{\tilde{\mathrm d}\boldsymbol q}{\mathrm dt} = \dot q_1\hat{\boldsymbol e}_1 + \dot q_2\hat{\boldsymbol e}_2 + \dot q_3\hat{\boldsymbol e}_3 ,
$$

that is, only the components are differentiated. The reasoning is the same as for Theorem 10.2.2, only with Cartesian coordinates in the moving frame.

**Theorem 10.6.1 (Transformation of the derivative of a vector)** Let the moving frame turn relative to the fixed frame with angular velocity **Ω**, and let **q**(t) be any differentiable vector function. Then

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol q}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{\tilde{\mathrm d}\boldsymbol q}{\mathrm dt} + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol q . \tag{10.6.1}
$$

In components, writing $q_p$ for the components of **q** in {p} and $q_s = Rq_p$ for its components in {s},

$$
\dot q_s = R\,\dot q_p + [\Omega_s]\,q_s . \tag{10.6.2}
$$

*Proof*　Differentiate $\boldsymbol q = \sum_i q_i\hat{\boldsymbol e}_i$ in the fixed frame; by Theorem 10.2.1,

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol q}{\mathrm dt} = \sum_i \dot q_i\,\hat{\boldsymbol e}_i + \sum_i q_i\,\frac{\mathrm d\hat{\boldsymbol e}_i}{\mathrm dt} .
$$

The first term is exactly $\tilde{\mathrm d}\boldsymbol q/\mathrm dt$. The axes of the moving frame turn with **Ω** together with the moving frame, and seen in the fixed frame their rates of change are $\mathrm d\hat{\boldsymbol e}_i/\mathrm dt = \boldsymbol\Omega\times\hat{\boldsymbol e}_i$: this is the result of Section 3.6 for unit vectors fixed to a rotating rigid body, whose matrix form is $\dot R = [\Omega_s]R$ (Eqs. (3.6.9) and (6.2.3)), the i-th column of R being the components of $\hat{\boldsymbol e}_i$ in {s}. So the second term is $\sum_i q_i\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times\hat{\boldsymbol e}_i = \boldsymbol\Omega\times\sum_i q_i\hat{\boldsymbol e}_i = \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol q$.

Component form: differentiating $q_s = Rq_p$ gives $\dot q_s = \dot Rq_p + R\dot q_p = [\Omega_s]Rq_p + R\dot q_p = [\Omega_s]q_s + R\dot q_p$. Here $R\dot q_p$ is the components in {s} of the relative derivative, and $[\Omega_s]q_s$ is the components in {s} of **Ω** × **q** (Eq. (3.2.10)).　∎

Theorem 10.6.1 involves only the rotation of the moving frame and does not depend on whether the origin of the moving frame moves: a translation does not change the direction of an arrow and does not affect the rate of change of a vector. It is the source of every other result in this section. Two immediate corollaries are worth remembering. First, if **q** is fixed in the moving frame ($\tilde{\mathrm d}\boldsymbol q/\mathrm dt = 0$), then $\mathrm d\boldsymbol q/\mathrm dt = \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol q$; this is the **v** = **ω** × **r** of Section 3.6. Second, taking **q** = **Ω** itself, $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol\Omega = 0$, so $\mathrm d\boldsymbol\Omega/\mathrm dt = \tilde{\mathrm d}\boldsymbol\Omega/\mathrm dt$: the angular acceleration **α** is the same in both reference frames and need not be distinguished.

### 10.6.4 The theorem of composition of velocities

The position of the moving point P can be written in two pieces: from the origin O of the fixed frame to the origin O′ of the moving frame, and then from O′ to P (Figure 10.6.1):

$$
\boldsymbol r = \boldsymbol p_{O'} + \boldsymbol r' , \tag{10.6.3}
$$

where $\boldsymbol r' = \overrightarrow{O'P}$ is the position vector of P relative to the origin of the moving frame; its components $r'_p$ in {p} are the coordinates of P in the moving frame. By definition, the relative velocity and the relative acceleration are the first and second relative derivatives of **r**′ in the moving frame: $\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}} = \tilde{\mathrm d}\boldsymbol r'/\mathrm dt$, $\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{rel}} = \tilde{\mathrm d}\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}}/\mathrm dt$; their components in {s} are $R\dot r'_p$ and $R\ddot r'_p$ respectively.

**Theorem 10.6.2 (Theorem of composition of velocities)** The absolute velocity of the moving point equals the sum of the transport velocity and the relative velocity:

$$
\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{abs}} = \boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{tr}} + \boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}} , \tag{10.6.4}
$$

where the transport velocity is

$$
\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{tr}} = \boldsymbol v_{O'} + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r' , \tag{10.6.5}
$$

and $\boldsymbol v_{O'} = \dot{\boldsymbol p}_{O'}$ is the velocity of the origin of the moving frame. In components,

$$
\dot r_s = \dot p_{O'} + [\Omega_s]\,R\,r'_p + R\,\dot r'_p . \tag{10.6.6}
$$

*Proof*　Differentiate Eq. (10.6.3) in the fixed frame and apply Theorem 10.6.1 to **r**′:

$$
\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{abs}} = \frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol r}{\mathrm dt} = \boldsymbol v_{O'} + \frac{\tilde{\mathrm d}\boldsymbol r'}{\mathrm dt} + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r' = \big(\boldsymbol v_{O'} + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r'\big) + \boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}} .
$$

It remains to show that the quantity in brackets is the transport velocity. The coincident point is the point of the moving frame that coincides with P at this instant; it is fixed in the moving frame and its relative velocity is zero. Writing the same equation for it, the relative-velocity term vanishes and its velocity is $\boldsymbol v_{O'} + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r'$, where **r**′ is its position vector relative to O′ (which is also that of P at this instant). The component form follows from Eq. (10.6.2).　∎

Equation (10.6.5) has exactly the same form as the velocity $\dot x = \dot p + \omega_s\times(x - p)$ of a point of a rigid body in Section 6.3 (Eq. (6.3.1)). This is no surprise: the moving frame is a rigid body, and the transport velocity is the velocity of a point of this rigid body. The theorem of composition of velocities says something intuitive: the velocity of the AGV relative to the ground equals the velocity of the turntable at the place where the AGV is, plus the velocity of the AGV relative to the turntable. The velocity of the part as seen from the AGV in the example of Section 10.1 was obtained from this theorem in reverse: the absolute velocity of the part is zero, so its relative velocity equals minus the transport velocity.

### 10.6.5 The theorem of composition of accelerations

Velocities can simply be added; accelerations cannot. One naturally guesses that the absolute acceleration equals the transport acceleration plus the relative acceleration, but when the moving frame rotates this guess is wrong: a term is missing.

**Theorem 10.6.3 (Theorem of composition of accelerations, Coriolis' theorem)** The absolute acceleration of the moving point is

$$
\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{abs}} = \boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{tr}} + \boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{rel}} + \boldsymbol a_C , \tag{10.6.7}
$$

where the transport acceleration is

$$
\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{tr}} = \boldsymbol a_{O'} + \boldsymbol\alpha\times\boldsymbol r' + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\big(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r'\big) , \tag{10.6.8}
$$

$\boldsymbol a_{O'} = \ddot{\boldsymbol p}_{O'}$ is the acceleration of the origin of the moving frame, and

$$
\boldsymbol a_C = 2\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}} \tag{10.6.9}
$$

is called the **Coriolis acceleration**. In components,

$$
\ddot r_s = \ddot p_{O'} + \big([\dot\Omega_s] + [\Omega_s]^2\big)R\,r'_p + 2\,[\Omega_s]\,R\,\dot r'_p + R\,\ddot r'_p . \tag{10.6.10}
$$

*Proof*　Differentiate in the fixed frame the result of the theorem of composition of velocities, $\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{abs}} = \boldsymbol v_{O'} + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r' + \boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}}$, treating the three terms one by one.

First term: $\mathrm d\boldsymbol v_{O'}/\mathrm dt = \boldsymbol a_{O'}$.

Second term: by the rule for differentiating a cross product in Theorem 10.2.1, and then Theorem 10.6.1 applied to **r**′,

$$
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r'\big) = \dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\boldsymbol r' + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol r'}{\mathrm dt}
= \boldsymbol\alpha\times\boldsymbol r' + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\big(\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}} + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r'\big)
= \boldsymbol\alpha\times\boldsymbol r' + \boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r') + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}} .
$$

Third term: applying Theorem 10.6.1 to $\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}}$,

$$
\frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}}}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{\tilde{\mathrm d}\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}}}{\mathrm dt} + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}} = \boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{rel}} + \boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}} .
$$

Adding the three terms:

$$
\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{abs}} = \big[\boldsymbol a_{O'} + \boldsymbol\alpha\times\boldsymbol r' + \boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r')\big] + \boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{rel}} + 2\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}} .
$$

Finally we show that the quantity in square brackets is the transport acceleration. The coincident point is fixed in the moving frame; if at every instant we take the same point of the moving frame, its relative velocity and relative acceleration are always zero. Writing the equation above for it, the last two terms vanish and its acceleration is the quantity in square brackets.

Component form: differentiate Eq. (10.6.6). $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big([\Omega_s]R\big) = [\dot\Omega_s]R + [\Omega_s]\dot R = \big([\dot\Omega_s] + [\Omega_s]^2\big)R$; $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(R\dot r'_p\big) = [\Omega_s]R\dot r'_p + R\ddot r'_p$. Hence

$$
\ddot r_s = \ddot p_{O'} + \big([\dot\Omega_s] + [\Omega_s]^2\big)Rr'_p + [\Omega_s]R\dot r'_p + [\Omega_s]R\dot r'_p + R\ddot r'_p ,
$$

and combining the two middle terms gives Eq. (10.6.10).　∎

**Where the factor 2 comes from.** The proof shows clearly that the two halves of the Coriolis acceleration come from two different places. One half comes from the third term: the arrow of the relative velocity is "carried round" by the rotating moving frame, so even if it looks unchanged in the moving frame, its direction is changing as seen in the fixed frame, at the rate $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}}$. The other half comes from the second term: as the moving point moves relative to the moving frame, its coincident point keeps changing; each new coincident point lies at a different place relative to the axis, so the transport velocity $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r'$ changes with it, at the rate $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}}$. This agrees completely with the analysis of $2\dot\rho\dot\varphi$ in Section 10.3.5: there, the "directions that turn with the arm" are a moving frame turning at $\dot\varphi$, $\dot\rho\,\boldsymbol e_\rho$ is the relative velocity, and $2\dot\varphi\hat{\boldsymbol z}\times\dot\rho\,\boldsymbol e_\rho = 2\dot\rho\dot\varphi\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ is exactly the Coriolis acceleration. The intuition "transport acceleration plus relative acceleration" goes wrong because it treats the coincident point as one fixed point.

**Names and properties of the terms.** Of the three terms of the transport acceleration (10.6.8), $\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r')$ is the **centripetal acceleration** caused by the rotation of the moving frame. By Eq. (3.6.8) it equals $-|\boldsymbol\Omega|^2\boldsymbol r'_\perp$, where $\boldsymbol r'_\perp$ is the part of **r**′ perpendicular to **Ω**, that is, the vector from the axis of rotation perpendicular to it out to the moving point; so the centripetal acceleration points perpendicularly towards the axis, with magnitude $\Omega^2$ times the distance from the axis. $\boldsymbol\alpha\times\boldsymbol r'$ comes from the speeding up or slowing down of the rotation of the moving frame and is called the **Euler acceleration**; it is zero when the turntable turns at a constant rate. The Coriolis acceleration (10.6.9) has three properties: it is perpendicular to **Ω** and also to the relative velocity, so it does not change the speed of the relative motion, only its direction; its magnitude is $2\Omega v_{\mathrm{rel}}\sin\gamma$, where γ is the angle between the two, and it is zero when the relative velocity is parallel to the axis of rotation; and when the moving frame only translates without rotating (**Ω** = 0), the Coriolis acceleration vanishes together with the centripetal and Euler accelerations, and Eq. (10.6.7) reduces to $\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{abs}} = \boldsymbol a_{O'} + \boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{rel}}$, which is the case of Galilean relativity.

**Background and history.** The French engineer Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis, studying rotating machines such as water wheels and turbines, needed to compute work and energy in a reference frame turning with the machine. In a paper published in 1832 he discussed the principle of kinetic energy in relative motion; in the paper "Mémoire sur les équations du mouvement relatif des systèmes de corps" (Memoir on the equations of relative motion of systems of bodies), published in 1835 in cahier 24 of the *Journal de l'École Polytechnique*, he proved that in a rotating reference frame, besides the usual centrifugal force, a further force must be added, which he called the "compound centrifugal force" (*force centrifuge composée*), whose magnitude is the mass times twice the product of the angular velocity and the relative velocity. This is what is today called the Coriolis force. Equations containing this term had already appeared in Pierre-Simon Laplace's studies of the tides around 1775, but stating it explicitly as a general kinematic theorem was the contribution of Coriolis. In 1851 Léon Foucault demonstrated the rotation of the Earth visibly in Paris with a long pendulum: the plane of swing of the pendulum turns slowly, which is precisely an effect of the Coriolis acceleration.

### 10.6.6 Writing the laws of motion in the moving frame: inertial forces

Multiplying both sides of Eq. (10.6.7) by the mass m and using Newton's second law in the fixed frame (an inertial reference frame), $m\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{abs}} = \boldsymbol F$, we obtain the equation satisfied by the relative motion in the moving frame:

$$
m\,\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{rel}} = \boldsymbol F - m\,\boldsymbol a_{O'} - m\,\boldsymbol\alpha\times\boldsymbol r' - m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r') - 2m\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}} . \tag{10.6.11}
$$

If an observer standing in the moving frame insists on using the form "mass times acceleration equals force", the last four terms on the right must be added to the real force **F**. These four terms are not actions exerted by any body; they appear because the moving frame is not an inertial reference frame, and together they are called **inertial forces**: $-m\boldsymbol a_{O'}$ is the translational inertial force; $-m\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r')$ points away from the axis of rotation and is called the **centrifugal force**; $-2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is called the **Coriolis force**; and $-m\boldsymbol\alpha\times\boldsymbol r'$ is called the Euler force. Being "thrown" towards the outside in a car going round a bend, or being "pushed" to one side when walking along a radius on a turntable, is exactly the centrifugal force and the Coriolis force at work.

This chapter is about kinematics and does not discuss forces. Equation (10.6.11) is written out here because its idea is needed in all the later chapters on dynamics. Chapters 24 and 25 will show that the dynamic equations of a robot arm contain a "Coriolis and centrifugal force" term, and that it comes from precisely the centripetal and Coriolis accelerations here: a link moves in a reference frame turning with the link before it, so its acceleration contains these two terms.

### 10.6.7 Example: an AGV on a turntable

**Example 10.6.1** Answer the question of Section 10.6.1. The turntable turns steadily anticlockwise about a vertical axis at Ω = {{ex10_6.Om:.1f}} rad/s; an AGV starts at the centre of the turntable and drives outwards along a radius of the turntable at a constant $v_{\mathrm{rel}}$ = {{ex10_6.vr:.1f}} m/s. Find the absolute velocity and absolute acceleration of the AGV at t = {{ex10_6.t1:.1f}} s (at ρ = {{ex10_6.rho1:.1f}} m from the centre), and the reading of the accelerometer on the vehicle. The x axis of the accelerometer points towards the front of the vehicle and the y axis to the left.

Take the ground frame {s} with its origin at the centre of the turntable and the z axis upwards; the turntable frame {p} has the same origin, with its x axis along the guide line. The origin of the moving frame does not move, so $\boldsymbol v_{O'} = \boldsymbol a_{O'} = 0$; **Ω** = Ω $\hat{\boldsymbol z}$ and **α** = 0. Express all quantities with the radial and transverse unit vectors $\boldsymbol e_\rho$, $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ that turn with the radius on which the AGV is ($\boldsymbol e_\rho$ points outwards along the guide line, which is the direction the vehicle faces; $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ points to the vehicle's left). **r**′ = ρ $\boldsymbol e_\rho$, $\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}} = v_{\mathrm{rel}}\boldsymbol e_\rho$, $\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{rel}} = 0$.

Velocity: the transport velocity is $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r' = \Omega\rho\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi$, of magnitude {{ex10_6.v_tr:.1f}} m/s; by Theorem 10.6.2 the absolute velocity is {{ex10_6.vr:.1f}} $\boldsymbol e_\rho$ + {{ex10_6.v_tr:.1f}} $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ m/s, of magnitude {{ex10_6.v_abs:.4f}} m/s.

Acceleration: by Eqs. (10.6.8) and (10.6.9),

$$
\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{tr}} = \boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r') = -\Omega^2\rho\,\boldsymbol e_\rho = -{{ex10_6.a_tr:.2f}}\,\boldsymbol e_\rho , \qquad
\boldsymbol a_C = 2\Omega\hat{\boldsymbol z}\times v_{\mathrm{rel}}\boldsymbol e_\rho = 2\Omega v_{\mathrm{rel}}\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi = {{ex10_6.a_C:.1f}}\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi ,
$$

in m/s². By Theorem 10.6.3,

$$
\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{abs}} = -{{ex10_6.a_tr:.2f}}\,\boldsymbol e_\rho + {{ex10_6.a_C:.1f}}\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi\ \ \mathrm{m/s^2} , \qquad |\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{abs}}| = {{ex10_6.a_abs:.5f}}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} .
$$

The accelerometer is fixed to the vehicle, and at this instant its x and y axes point exactly along $\boldsymbol e_\rho$ and $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$. An accelerometer measures specific force, that is, acceleration minus the acceleration due to gravity; in the horizontal plane gravity has no component, so the horizontal components of the specific force are the horizontal components of the acceleration. The readings are therefore x: {{ex10_6.imu_x:.2f}} m/s², y: {{ex10_6.imu_y:.2f}} m/s², that is, 0.25 backwards and 0.40 to the left, in agreement with Section 10.6.1 (Figure 10.6.2).

The 0.25 m/s² backwards is the centripetal acceleration: the AGV goes round with the turntable and needs an acceleration towards the centre. The 0.40 m/s² to the left is the Coriolis acceleration: the further out the AGV drives, the greater the linear speed of the turntable there, so to "keep up" with the turntable the AGV must keep gaining sideways velocity; at the same time its direction of travel turns with the turntable; each of the two contributes one half. This sideways acceleration is supplied by the sideways friction force between the wheels and the turntable. If the AGV and its load have a mass of 200 kg, the sideways force needed is about 80 N; if the surface of the turntable is oily, or if the AGV runs on Mecanum wheels with very little lateral stiffness, it may slide to the right. Reducing either the speed of the turntable or the speed of the AGV reduces this force, since the Coriolis acceleration is proportional to their product.

(10.3.9). The three give the same result, and the program also checks it term by term in the matrix form (10.6.10). Seen from the ground, the AGV traces an Archimedean spiral ρ = ($v_{\mathrm{rel}}$/Ω)φ, the dashed line in Figure 10.6.2(a).

**Example 10.6.2** At the same instant, if the turntable is speeding up at α = {{ex10_6.Omd:.1f}} rad/s² and the AGV is also accelerating along the guide line at {{ex10_6.ar:.1f}} m/s², what does the accelerometer read?

Two terms are added: the relative acceleration {{ex10_6.ar:.1f}} $\boldsymbol e_\rho$, and the Euler acceleration $\boldsymbol\alpha\times\boldsymbol r' = \alpha\rho\,\boldsymbol e_\varphi$ = {{ex10_6.a_euler:.1f}} $\boldsymbol e_\varphi$. The readings become x: {{ex10_6.imu2_x:.2f}} m/s², y: {{ex10_6.imu2_y:.2f}} m/s², with magnitude {{ex10_6.a_abs2:.4f}} m/s². These are exactly the transverse component $\rho\ddot\varphi + 2\dot\rho\dot\varphi$ and the radial component $\ddot\rho - \rho\dot\varphi^2$ of Eq. (10.3.9). Writing the accelerometer readings as formulas in body coordinates, for an AGV driving along a radius of the turntable we have

$$
a_x = \dot v_{\mathrm{rel}} - \Omega^2\rho , \qquad a_y = 2\Omega v_{\mathrm{rel}} + \alpha\rho . \tag{10.6.12}
$$

If the controller of the AGV knows only its own motion relative to the turntable (its speed and acceleration), it can predict the accelerometer readings from Eq. (10.6.12); if the readings do not match the prediction, the wheels are slipping. This is one way for a mobile robot on a moving platform to detect slip.

**Example 10.6.3** The Earth rotates at $\Omega_E = {{ex10_6.w_E:sci4}}$ rad/s (one turn relative to the stars takes about 86,164 s, one sidereal day). Shanghai lies at a latitude of about λ = 31.2° N. An AGV drives due north at 1.0 m/s. How large is the horizontal component of its Coriolis acceleration?

Take the local east, north and up directions as coordinate axes. In local coordinates the angular velocity of the Earth is $\Omega_E(0,\ \cos\lambda,\ \sin\lambda)$, and the relative velocity of the AGV is (0, 1, 0) m/s. By Eq. (10.6.9) the Coriolis acceleration is $2\Omega_E(-\sin\lambda,\ 0,\ 0)$ m/s², pointing horizontally west, with magnitude

$$
a_C = 2\,\Omega_E\,v\sin\lambda = {{ex10_6.aC_h:sci3}}\ \mathrm{m/s^2} , \tag{10.6.13}
$$

about ${{ex10_6.aC_h_g:sci1}}$ times the acceleration due to gravity. For the AGV to keep heading due north, the ground must exert on it a sideways force of this size towards the west; without this force it would drift east, that is, to the right of its direction of travel. But this quantity is far smaller than any error in the localization and control of an AGV: deflected at this acceleration for 10 s, the sideways offset is only about 4 mm, while a single small slip of a wheel easily exceeds that. This is the quantitative basis for the statement in Section 10.1.3 that for robots in a workshop the ground can be treated as an inertial reference frame. The centripetal acceleration due to the Earth's rotation (about 0.034 m/s² at the equator), on the other hand, is included in the local acceleration due to gravity; this is part of the reason why the value of g differs from place to place.

### 10.6.8 Coriolis deflection: a ball on a turntable

The most intuitive manifestation of the Coriolis acceleration is the "deflection" seen in a rotating reference frame. Imagine a turntable turning anticlockwise, and let a ball roll out from its centre at 0.6 m/s in a fixed direction on the ground, neglecting friction. The ball feels no horizontal force, so seen from the ground it moves in a straight line at constant speed (Figure 10.6.3(a)).

What does it look like on the turntable? The absolute acceleration of the ball is zero, so by Eq. (10.6.7) its relative acceleration is

$$
\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{rel}} = -\boldsymbol a_{\mathrm{tr}} - \boldsymbol a_C = -\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol r') - 2\,\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}} .
$$

The first term points away from the centre, and the second is perpendicular to the relative velocity. With **Ω** pointing up, $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\boldsymbol v_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is the relative velocity turned anticlockwise through 90°, that is, pointing to the left; with the minus sign it points to the right. So seen on the turntable, the path of the ball keeps bending to the right (Figure 10.6.3(b)). For every angle through which the turntable turns, the path of the ball on the turntable "falls behind" by the same angle; the ball takes 2.5 s to reach the rim, during which the turntable turns through 1.25 rad, about 72°.

### 10.6.9 Everyday examples

On a turning merry-go-round, throw a ball straight to a friend on the opposite side and it always lands off to one side; experienced children aim to the "left" and let the Coriolis deflection bring the ball back. This is the same phenomenon as the ball on the turntable.

On the Earth, the angular velocity of the Earth's rotation has an upward (local vertical) component in the northern hemisphere, and objects moving horizontally are deflected to the right by the Coriolis effect. For this reason large-scale air flows in the northern hemisphere circulate anticlockwise around centres of low pressure, as the cloud pictures of typhoons show; in the southern hemisphere it is the other way round. In 1851 Foucault hung a pendulum 67 m long and weighing 28 kg beneath the dome of the Panthéon in Paris; its plane of swing turned clockwise by about 11° per hour, a full turn in about 32 h, and the spectators present "saw" the rotation of the Earth for the first time. The often-heard claim that "in the northern hemisphere water always swirls anticlockwise down a washbasin drain", however, is not true: the speed and scale of the water flow in a washbasin are far too small, and the Coriolis acceleration is far smaller than the effects of the shape of the basin, the way the water is let out and so on; the order of magnitude found in Example 10.6.3 already shows this.

**Engineer's notes**

- An accelerometer measures specific force, not "acceleration relative to the ground", nor "acceleration relative to the platform carrying it". An accelerometer mounted on a turntable, a rotating robot arm or a ship has centripetal and Coriolis terms in its reading, which must be removed using Eq. (10.6.7) before the reading is used.
- For a robot or AGV on a turntable or rotary table, the sideways grip must be checked against the Coriolis acceleration $2\Omega v_{\mathrm{rel}}$ and the centripetal acceleration $\Omega^2\rho$; the most dangerous position is usually at the edge of the turntable (largest centripetal term) while driving radially (largest Coriolis term).
- Forgetting the Coriolis term is a common error when simulating or controlling in a moving frame. A check: keep the moving point at rest in the moving frame and turn the moving frame at a constant rate; the computed absolute acceleration should be exactly the centripetal acceleration. Then let the moving point move along a radius at constant speed in the moving frame; the sideways acceleration should be $2\Omega v_{\mathrm{rel}}$.
- The Coriolis acceleration due to the Earth's rotation can be neglected on the scale of a workshop, but the gyroscopes of a navigation-grade inertial measurement unit can measure the Earth's rotation rate (about 15°/h), and long periods of pure inertial navigation must take the Earth's rotation into account.
