### 11.5.1 The problem: why one active arm cannot be turned on its own

In Lab 1.3 the joints of every robot in the parts library can be dragged, except those of the Delta robot: if one active arm is dragged while the other two stay put, the lower end of its parallelogram "comes apart" from the moving platform and the model cannot stay closed. A real Delta of course does not come apart. Its three active arms can only turn together in some coordinated way, or, when one of them turns, carry the moving platform to a definite new position. Example 11.2.2 has already counted 3 degrees of freedom for the Delta, yet the mechanism has 39 joint variables. How are these 39 numbers related? Given the angles of the three motors, where is the moving platform? Conversely, how far must each of the three motors turn for the suction cup to reach a given point on the conveyor?

On the same assembly line, the differential-drive AGV that delivers the parts is also constrained: its wheels can only roll in the direction the vehicle is heading and cannot slip sideways (Section 1.3). This constraint looks similar to that of the Delta, since both say "some motions are not allowed", but the result is completely different. The Delta's constraints reduce it from 39 joint variables to 3 degrees of freedom; the AGV's constraint does not reduce the poses it can reach at all. The (x, y, φ) of the vehicle body on the floor can still take any value; only the way of getting there is restricted, and parallel parking takes several back-and-forth moves (Section 6.7).

This section studies these two kinds of constraint. The first kind appears in closed chains and is written as equations among the joint variables; it is called a holonomic constraint, and it "cuts" the configuration space down to a lower-dimensional surface. The second kind is written as a relation among velocities; it is called a nonholonomic constraint, and it leaves the configuration space unchanged, restricting only the directions in which the system can move at each instant.

### 11.5.2 The loop-closure equation: the four-bar linkage

Let us first look at the simplest closed chain, the planar four-bar linkage (Figure 11.5.1a). The ground AD has length $l_0$, the crank AB length $l_1$, the coupler BC length $l_2$ and the rocker DC length $l_3$. Take A as the origin and AD as the x-axis; the three angles $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$, $\theta_3$ are the angles that AB, BC and DC make with the positive x-axis, positive anticlockwise, measured in radians (marked in degrees in the figure).

Going from point A along the crank and the coupler to C, and going from A along the ground and the rocker to C, the two routes must arrive at the same point. Writing $e(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ for the unit vector with direction angle θ, this condition reads

$$
l_1 e(\theta_1) + l_2 e(\theta_2) - l_3 e(\theta_3) - l_0 e(0) = 0 . \tag{11.5.1}
$$

This is one planar vector equation, that is, two scalar equations, in three unknown angles. Equation (11.5.1) is called the **loop-closure equation** of the mechanism. It expresses "going once round the loop brings you back to where you started", and says the same thing as the velocity loop-closure equation (11.2.5) of Section 11.2.5, except that here the positions are constrained and there the velocities. Differentiating Eq. (11.5.1) with respect to time gives the planar form of Eq. (11.2.5).

Three unknowns and two equations leave, in general, one degree of freedom, in agreement with the result of Example 11.2.1. In engineering the crank angle $\theta_1$ is usually the input and the rocker angle $\theta_3$ is wanted. Eliminate $\theta_2$ from Eq. (11.5.1): move $l_2 e(\theta_2)$ to one side and take the squared magnitude of both sides, $l_2^2 = |l_0 e(0) + l_3 e(\theta_3) - l_1 e(\theta_1)|^2$; expanding gives

$$
l_2^2 = l_0^2 + l_1^2 + l_3^2 + 2l_0l_3\cos\theta_3 - 2l_0l_1\cos\theta_1 - 2l_1l_3\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_3) .
$$

Dividing both sides by $2l_1l_3$ and rearranging,

$$
K_1\cos\theta_3 - K_2\cos\theta_1 + K_3 = \cos(\theta_1 - \theta_3), \quad K_1 = \frac{l_0}{l_1},\ K_2 = \frac{l_0}{l_3},\ K_3 = \frac{l_0^2 + l_1^2 - l_2^2 + l_3^2}{2l_1l_3} . \tag{11.5.2}
$$

This equation, which contains only the input and output angles, is called **Freudenstein's equation** after the American kinematician Ferdinand Freudenstein. In 1954 he used it for the design of four-bar linkages: when the input and output angles are required to satisfy a given functional relation, substituting several pairs of angles into Eq. (11.5.2) gives linear equations in $K_1$, $K_2$, $K_3$, and solving them gives the ratios of the link lengths. This marked the beginning of the move in mechanism synthesis from graphical construction to computation.

To find $\theta_3$ from a given $\theta_1$, expand the right-hand side of Eq. (11.5.2) as $\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_3 + \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_3$ and rearrange into the form $A\cos\theta_3 + B\sin\theta_3 = C$, where $A = K_1 - \cos\theta_1$, $B = -\sin\theta_1$, $C = K_2\cos\theta_1 - K_3$. Put $A = \rho\cos\psi$, $B = \rho\sin\psi$, $\rho = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}$; the equation becomes $\cos(\theta_3 - \psi) = C/\rho$, and therefore

$$
\theta_3 = \operatorname{atan2}(B, A) \pm \arccos\frac{C}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}} . \tag{11.5.3}
$$

Here atan2(B, A) is the direction angle of the point (A, B), taking values in (−π, π]; it picks the correct quadrant automatically from the signs of A and B. There is a solution when $|C| \le \rho$: two solutions when $|C| < \rho$, which merge into one when $|C| = \rho$; when $|C| > \rho$ there is no solution, meaning that the mechanism cannot be assembled at this input angle.

The two solutions have a very intuitive geometric meaning. Point B is already fixed by $\theta_1$; point C must lie both on the circle of radius $l_2$ about B and on the circle of radius $l_3$ about D, and two circles in general meet in two points (C and C′ in Figure 11.5.1a).

**Definition 11.5.1 (Assembly mode)** The different configurations of a closed-chain mechanism for the same set of inputs are called its **assembly modes**.

A four-bar linkage has two assembly modes. The one with C on one side of the line BD is called the open mode; the one with C on the other side, where the coupler crosses the ground, is called the crossed mode. Once the mechanism has been assembled, it stays in its original assembly mode as long as it does not pass through a position where the two solutions merge; to change to the other mode, the mechanism must be taken apart and reassembled.

**Example 11.5.1** A four-bar linkage has $l_0 = 0.40$ m, $l_1 = 0.15$ m, $l_2 = 0.35$ m, $l_3 = 0.30$ m. Find the two solutions for the rocker angle at $\theta_1 = 60^\circ$.

From Eq. (11.5.2), $K_1 = {{ex11_5.K1:.3f}}$, $K_2 = {{ex11_5.K2:.3f}}$, $K_3 = {{ex11_5.K3:.3f}}$. Substituting into Eq. ### 11.5.3 The configuration space of a closed chain

Each of the three joint angles of the four-bar linkage lies on a circle, so without constraints their configuration space is the three-dimensional torus $T^3$ (Section 11.3). The loop-closure equation (11.5.1) consists of two equations, which cut away two dimensions; what remains is a curve in $T^3$. Projecting this curve onto the $(\theta_1, \theta_3)$ square gives Figure 11.5.1b. The left and right edges of the square, and its top and bottom edges, are still glued together.

In general, the joint variables $\theta = (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_n)$ of a closed chain must satisfy k loop-closure equations, written

$$
g(\theta) = 0, \qquad g: T^n \to \mathbb R^k . \tag{11.5.4}
$$

**Definition 11.5.2 (Holonomic constraint)** A constraint that can be written as an equation g(q) = 0 in the configuration variables is called a **holonomic constraint**.

Loop-closure equations are holonomic constraints; so is the gearing that links the two hands of a clock in Section 11.3.7, $\theta_m = 12\theta_h$. Holonomic constraints make the configuration space the set of points of $T^n$ (or, in general, of the space of configuration variables) at which g = 0, that is, the implicit representation of Section 11.3.4.

**Theorem 11.5.1** Let $\theta^*$ satisfy $g(\theta^*) = 0$, let g be continuously differentiable, and let the Jacobian matrix $\partial g / \partial\theta$ (k×n) have constant rank r near $\theta^*$. Then near $\theta^*$ the configuration space is a smooth surface of dimension n − r; when r = k the dimension is n − k.

*Proof* (outline)　The case r = k: choose k linearly independent columns of $\partial g/\partial\theta$, call the corresponding joint variables $\theta_{\mathrm d}$ (dependent), and the remaining n − k variables $\theta_{\mathrm i}$ (independent). $\partial g/\partial\theta_{\mathrm d}$ is an invertible k×k matrix, so by the implicit function theorem (used in Section 8.2) there is a continuously differentiable function $\theta_{\mathrm d} = h(\theta_{\mathrm i})$ near $\theta^*$ such that $g(\theta_{\mathrm i}, h(\theta_{\mathrm i})) = 0$, and every point with g = 0 is of this form. The n − k independent variables can therefore serve as local coordinates on the configuration space. When r < k, k − r of the equations are consequences of the others in this neighbourhood; drop them and apply the argument above.　∎

This theorem is the basis of "counting by rank" in Section 11.2.5: differentiating Eq. (11.5.4) gives $(\partial g/\partial\theta)\dot\theta = 0$, so the coefficient matrix of the velocity loop-closure equation is exactly the Jacobian here, and the instantaneous mobility n − r is the dimension of the configuration space at that point.

**The Grashof condition.** In Figure 11.5.1b the blue and orange curves each go once round the torus: the crank can turn through a full revolution, each assembly mode is a closed curve, and the two curves never meet. Not every four-bar linkage has this property. The German engineer Franz Grashof gave the criterion in the second volume of his *Theoretische Maschinenlehre* (Theoretical Mechanical Engineering) in 1883. Denote the length of the shortest of the four links by s, that of the longest by l, and those of the other two by p and q.

**Theorem 11.5.2 (Grashof)** Let the shortest link of a four-bar linkage be adjacent to the ground and serve as the crank. Then the crank can turn through a full revolution if and only if

$$
s + l \le p + q . \tag{11.5.5}
$$

*Proof*　Let d be the distance from the end B of the crank to the other end D of the ground. The mechanism can be assembled at a given $\theta_1$ if and only if the circles of radii $l_2$ and $l_3$ about B and D intersect, that is, $|l_2 - l_3| \le d \le l_2 + l_3$. As the crank turns once round, d takes every value in $[l_0 - l_1,\ l_0 + l_1]$ (the crank is the shortest link, $l_1 < l_0$). Hence the crank can turn fully if and only if

$$
l_0 + l_1 \le l_2 + l_3 \quad \text{and} \quad l_0 - l_1 \ge |l_2 - l_3| .
$$

The crank has $l_1 = s$. If the longest link is the ground, $l_0 = l$, the first inequality is exactly $s + l \le p + q$; the second is equivalent to $l_0 + \min(l_2, l_3) \ge l_1 + \max(l_2, l_3)$, which holds automatically because $l_0$ is the longest and $l_1$ the shortest. If the longest link is the coupler, $l_2 = l$, the first inequality holds automatically because $l_2 \ge l_0$ and $l_3 \ge l_1$; the second becomes $l_1 + l_2 \le l_0 + l_3$, that is, $s + l \le p + q$. The case where the longest link is the rocker is symmetric to that of the coupler.　∎

A four-bar linkage satisfying Eq. (11.5.5) is said to satisfy the **Grashof condition**. When the shortest link is the crank and is adjacent to the ground, the other side link can only swing back and forth; such a mechanism is called a **crank-rocker mechanism**. Windscreen wipers, sewing-machine treadles and jaw crushers all use it to convert rotation into oscillation.

Theorem 11.5.2 deals only with the case in which the shortest link is adjacent to the ground. The same argument of intersecting circles gives the complete classification of four-bar linkages. When s + l < p + q: if the shortest link is a side link, we get the crank-rocker mechanism above; if the shortest link is the ground, both side links can turn fully, and the mechanism is called a double-crank mechanism; if the shortest link is the coupler, both side links can only swing, while the coupler can turn fully relative to them, which is also a kind of double-rocker mechanism. When s + l > p + q, whichever link is taken as the ground, no link can turn fully relative to its neighbours, and both side links can only swing: this is a double-rocker mechanism (the second half of Example 11.5.2). When s + l = p + q, the mechanism passes through positions in which all four pivots are collinear, where it may "switch branches"; it is called a change-point mechanism, and the parallelogram linkage is an example (Section 11.2.5).

The limit position is also an example of Theorem 11.5.1. There the Jacobian determinant of the loop-closure equation with respect to $(\theta_2, \theta_3)$ is zero (Program 11.5.1 obtains ${{ex11_5.det23_lim:sci2}}$, compared with about {{ex11_5.det23_gen:.2f}} at a general position), while the Jacobian with respect to all three angles still has rank {{ex11_5.rank_lim}}. In other words, the configuration space is still a smooth curve here; only $\theta_1$ can no longer serve as a local coordinate on it, because the curve "turns back" in the $\theta_1$ direction. In the theory of mechanisms such a position is called a dead point of the input. If the rocker is the input and the crank the output, a crank-rocker mechanism also has such dead points when the crank and the coupler are collinear: when a sewing-machine treadle sometimes will not move, it has stopped at a dead point, and the wheel has to be nudged by hand to carry it through. Chapters 16 and 18 will discuss such singular configurations systematically from the point of view of the Jacobian.

### 11.5.4 Parallel mechanisms: easy inverse kinematics, hard forward kinematics

Connecting a moving platform to the ground through several legs at once gives a **parallel mechanism**. The Delta and the Stewart platform are both parallel mechanisms; by contrast, a mechanism such as the UR5e, with a single chain from the base to the end-effector, is called a serial mechanism. The loop-closure equations determine a basic feature of parallel mechanisms: exactly the opposite of a serial arm, their inverse kinematics is easy and their forward kinematics is hard.

**Inverse kinematics of the Delta.** Take the dimensions of the parts-library model B-PAR-DELTA: the distance from the centre of the base to each of the three active-arm pivots is $R_b = 0.15$ m, the distance from the centre of the moving platform to its spherical joints is $R_p = 0.04$ m, the active arms are $L_a = 0.22$ m long and the passive rods $L_b = 0.5$ m. The origin of the coordinate frame is at the centre of the base, with the z-axis pointing up. Leg k (k = 1, 2, 3) lies in the vertical plane at azimuth $\phi_k = 0^\circ, 120^\circ, 240^\circ$, whose horizontal unit vector is $e_k = (\cos\phi_k, \sin\phi_k, 0)$. The angle $\theta_k$ of the active arm is measured from the horizontal, positive downwards, about a horizontal axis in the tangential direction, so the elbow point is

$$
E_k = R_b e_k + L_a(\cos\theta_k\, e_k - \sin\theta_k\, \hat z) .
$$

The two rods of the parallelogram are equally long, so it acts like a single rod of length $L_b$ with a spherical joint at each end, connecting the elbow point $E_k$ to the point $P'_k = p + R_p e_k$ on the moving platform (Section 11.5.5 explains why the platform does not rotate), where p is the centre of the moving platform. The loop-closure condition of leg k is

$$
|p + R_p e_k - E_k(\theta_k)| = L_b . \tag{11.5.6}
$$

For a given p, each equation contains only one unknown $\theta_k$, and the three legs do not interact. Write $q_k = p + (R_p - R_b)e_k$, with components $u_k$, $v_k$, $h_k$ in the $e_k$ direction, the tangential direction and the vertical direction. Squaring both sides of Eq. (11.5.6) and expanding gives $A_k\cos\theta_k + B_k\sin\theta_k = C_k$, where $A_k = -2L_au_k$, $B_k = 2L_ah_k$, $C_k = L_b^2 - L_a^2 - |q_k|^2$. This has the same form as Eq. (11.5.3), and the solution is

$$
\theta_k = \operatorname{atan2}(B_k, A_k) \pm \arccos\frac{C_k}{\sqrt{A_k^2 + B_k^2}} . \tag{11.5.7}
$$

Geometrically, in the vertical plane of leg k, the elbow point lies both on the circle of radius $L_a$ about the pivot and on the intersection of this plane with the sphere about $P'_k$ (which is a circle); the two intersections of the two circles are the two solutions of Eq. (11.5.7) (Figure 11.5.2a). Each of the three legs has two solutions, so the inverse kinematics has $2^3 = 8$ sets of solutions in all; the one used in practice is the set with the elbows pointing outwards (the + sign).

**Example 11.5.3** Find the angles of the three active arms of the Delta when the centre of the moving platform is at (0, 0, −0.42) m and at (0.1, 0, −0.45) m, and check them with the forward kinematics.

The first position is the rest position of the parts-library model. From Eq. (11.5.7) all three angles equal {{ex11_5.th0_deg:.2f}}°, the same as the rest position of {{ex11_5.th0:.6f}} rad recorded in the model file. The second position is shifted towards leg 1, and gives $\theta_1 = {{ex11_5.th1a:.2f}}^\circ$, $\theta_2 = \theta_3 = {{ex11_5.th1b:.2f}}^\circ$: the active arm of leg 1 is almost horizontal, while the other two have turned somewhat further down. The other set of solutions of Eq. (11.5.7), with the − sign, is $\theta_1 = {{ex11_5.th1_in_a:.1f}}^\circ$, $\theta_2 = \theta_3 = {{ex11_5.th1_in_b:.1f}}^\circ$: the active arms are flipped up inwards, far outside the model's angle range of −40° to 90°, and are of no use in practice.

**Forward kinematics of the Delta.** Conversely, given the three angles, the three elbow points are fixed. By Eq. (11.5.6), the distances from $p$ to the points $c_k = E_k - R_pe_k$ all equal $L_b$:

$$
|p - c_k(\theta_k)| = L_b, \qquad k = 1, 2, 3 . \tag{11.5.8}
$$

p is an intersection point of three spheres. Three spheres in general meet in two points, one below the plane of the $c_k$ and one above it. Program 11.5.1 finds the two intersection points by the formula for "intersecting three spheres": for the second position, the lower intersection is exactly (0.1, 0, −0.45) m, and the upper one has z = {{ex11_5.fk_high_z:.3f}} m, above the base, where the mechanism cannot be assembled. The program also takes {{ex11_5.nd}} random points in the workspace, finds the angles by Eq. (11.5.7) and then the platform position back again by Eq. (11.5.8); all agree with the original points to within $10^{-9}$ m.

The forward kinematics of the Delta is still fairly simple, because the three equations happen to reduce to intersecting three spheres. For a general parallel mechanism, the forward kinematics requires solving a system of simultaneous nonlinear equations, and multiple solutions are the rule.

**The Stewart platform.** The 6-UPS Stewart platform (Figure 11.2.2b) is like this too. Let the centre of the universal joint of leg i on the ground be $a_i$, and the centre of its spherical joint on the moving platform be $b_i$ in the platform frame; if the pose of the moving platform is (R, p), the leg length is

$$
l_i = |p + Rb_i - a_i|, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, 6 . \tag{11.5.9}
$$

Given the pose, the six leg lengths are computed in one step; this is its inverse kinematics. The forward kinematics, finding (R, p) from the six leg lengths, is a system of nonlinear equations in six unknowns. In 1993 Madhusudan Raghavan used the polynomial continuation method to find numerically that the forward kinematics of a Stewart platform of general geometry has 40 complex solutions, and in the same period several other researchers proved this number rigorously by algebraic methods; in 1998 Peter Dietmaier constructed an example in which all 40 solutions are real.

This platform is named after the British engineer D. Stewart, who in a 1965 paper proposed using a six-degree-of-freedom parallel platform for flight simulators. However, Eric Gough of the British Dunlop company had already built, in the mid-1950s, a platform supported by six extensible legs to apply loads to tyres in all directions at once, and published it in 1956–1957; the mechanism drawn in Stewart's paper was in fact a different structure with three legs. The six-legged platform is therefore also often called the Gough–Stewart platform in the literature. In practice the forward kinematics is usually solved iteratively by Newton's method, starting from the current pose (Chapters 15 and 18).

For a parallel mechanism it is actually more convenient to use the inverse kinematics: take points in space; a point at which all three legs have solutions, with the solutions within the joint limits, belongs to the workspace. ### 11.5.5 Why the parallelograms let the moving platform only translate

We now supply an explanation left over from Section 11.2. The passive part of each leg of the Delta is a parallelogram: two rods of equal length, connected at their upper ends by spherical joints to a short crossbar at the end of the active arm, and at their lower ends to the edge of the moving platform. Since the two rods are equally long and the distances between their two ends are equal, this quadrilateral is always a parallelogram, and its upper and lower sides are always parallel. The upper side is fixed to the active arm, parallel to the axis of the active arm, and the direction of this axis, $t_k = \hat z \times e_k$, is fixed on the base. So the corresponding side on the moving platform always has direction $t_k$.

Let the orientation of the moving platform be R, and let the direction of this side of the platform be $t_k$ in the platform frame (the same as on the base at the zero position). The conclusion above then reads $Rt_k = t_k$, k = 1, 2, 3. The three $t_k$ are three horizontal vectors, no two of them parallel. A rotation that leaves two non-parallel vectors unchanged also leaves their cross product unchanged, and hence leaves three linearly independent vectors unchanged; it can only be the identity. So R = I, and the moving platform can only translate. This is exactly the conclusion that Program 11.2.1 reached by the rank method: the angular velocity components of the twist of the moving platform are all zero.

The Delta was invented by Reymond Clavel of the École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne (EPFL) in Switzerland. In the early 1980s his team designed the mechanism to place chocolates into packaging quickly; it was published at the International Symposium on Industrial Robots in 1988 and received a US patent in 1990. Today most high-speed picking robots in the food, pharmaceutical and electronics industries have this structure.

### 11.5.6 Velocity constraints: the Pfaffian form

Now back to the AGV. Let the position of the centre of the AGV body be (x, y) and the angle between its heading and the x-axis be φ, positive anticlockwise (Figure 11.5.3a). That the wheels do not slip sideways means that the velocity of the centre of the body has no component perpendicular to the heading. The unit vector perpendicular to the heading is $(-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)$, and so

$$
-\dot x\sin\varphi + \dot y\cos\varphi = 0, \quad \text{that is,} \quad \dot x\sin\varphi - \dot y\cos\varphi = 0 . \tag{11.5.10}
$$

This constraint has a different form from a loop-closure equation: it is not an equation among (x, y, φ) but a linear relation among velocities, with coefficients that depend on the configuration.

**Definition 11.5.3 (Pfaffian constraint)** Let the configuration variables be $q \in \mathbb R^n$. A constraint of the form

$$
A(q)\,\dot q = 0 \tag{11.5.11}
$$

is called a **Pfaffian constraint**, where A(q) is a k×n matrix with linearly independent rows.

The name comes from the German mathematician Johann Friedrich Pfaff, who around 1815 studied differential equations of the form $a_1dq_1 + \cdots + a_ndq_n = 0$, known today as Pfaffian equations. Equation (11.5.10) is a Pfaffian constraint with n = 3 and k = 1, $A = (\sin\varphi,\ -\cos\varphi,\ 0)$.

Every holonomic constraint can be put in Pfaffian form: differentiate g(q) = 0 with respect to time, $(\partial g/\partial q)\dot q = 0$, and take $A = \partial g/\partial q$. Conversely, does every Pfaffian constraint come from some holonomic constraint?

**Definition 11.5.4 (Integrable and nonholonomic constraints)** If there exist a function g(q) and a function λ(q) that is nowhere zero such that $\lambda(q)a(q) = \nabla g(q)$, the Pfaffian constraint $a(q)\cdot\dot q = 0$ is said to be **integrable**. When it is integrable, the constraint is equivalent to g(q) = constant, which is a holonomic constraint. A Pfaffian constraint that is not integrable (or a set of Pfaffian constraints that cannot be reduced to holonomic constraints) is called a **nonholonomic constraint**.

λ is called an integrating factor: sometimes a itself is not a gradient, but becomes one after multiplication by a function. The two terms were introduced by the German physicist Heinrich Hertz in *Die Prinzipien der Mechanik* (*The Principles of Mechanics Presented in a New Form*), published in 1894; "holonomic" means that the constraint can be written wholly as a relation among the coordinates.

How can we tell whether a constraint is integrable? For one constraint in three variables there is a simple test.

**Theorem 11.5.3** Let $q \in \mathbb R^3$, and let a(q) be continuously differentiable and nowhere zero. If the Pfaffian constraint $a(q)\cdot\dot q = 0$ is integrable, then

$$
a \cdot (\nabla \times a) = 0 , \tag{11.5.12}
$$

where ∇ × a is the curl of a taken with respect to the three components of q.

*Proof*　By definition, $\lambda a = \nabla g$. The curl of a gradient is zero, so $\nabla \times (\lambda a) = 0$. By the product rule, $\nabla \times (\lambda a) = \nabla\lambda \times a + \lambda\,\nabla \times a$, and therefore $\nabla\lambda \times a + \lambda\,\nabla \times a = 0$. Take the dot product of both sides with a: $a \cdot (\nabla\lambda \times a)$ is a scalar triple product with two equal vectors and is zero (Section 3.2), so $\lambda\, a \cdot (\nabla \times a) = 0$. Since λ is nowhere zero, Eq. (11.5.12) follows.　∎

Conversely, if Eq. (11.5.12) holds everywhere in a region, the constraint is integrable in that region. This is a special case of a more general theorem of 1877 by the German mathematician Ferdinand Georg Frobenius, and is not proved here.

Now substitute Eq. (11.5.10). Take $q = (x, y, \varphi)$, $a = (\sin\varphi, -\cos\varphi, 0)$. The three components of the curl are

$$
\nabla \times a = \left(\frac{\partial a_3}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial a_2}{\partial\varphi},\ \frac{\partial a_1}{\partial\varphi} - \frac{\partial a_3}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial a_2}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial a_1}{\partial y}\right) = (-\sin\varphi,\ \cos\varphi,\ 0) ,
$$

and $a \cdot (\nabla \times a) = -\sin^2\varphi - \cos^2\varphi = -1$, which is nowhere zero. Program 11.5.1 computes this quantity by numerical differences at 20 random configurations, and the result is always −1; as a control, it takes an integrable constraint (obtained by differentiating x + 2y − φ = 0), for which the result is 0. Rolling without side slip is therefore a nonholonomic constraint: no equation among (x, y, φ) can replace it. The configuration space of the vehicle body is still the three-dimensional $\mathbb R^2 \times S^1$ (Table 11.3.1); only, at each configuration, the allowed velocities form just a two-dimensional set: driving forwards along the heading, and turning about the vertical axis.

This is precisely the fundamental difference between holonomic and nonholonomic constraints. k independent holonomic constraints reduce the dimension of the configuration space by k, and also reduce the allowed velocities by k dimensions; k nonholonomic constraints reduce only the allowed velocities by k dimensions, and leave the dimension of the configuration space unchanged. Section 6.7 has already explained the latter point in terms of the Lie bracket: alternating the two allowed motions, driving forwards and turning, has a net effect of moving sideways, so the vehicle body can reach any (x, y, φ).

### 11.5.7 The differential-drive AGV: wheel angles do not determine the position

If the angles of the two wheels are included in the configuration as well, it becomes clearer what "non-integrable" means. The differential-drive AGV of the parts library (B-EDU-DIFF) has wheel radius r = 0.05 m and track b = 0.26 m. Take the configuration as $q = (x, y, \varphi, \theta_R, \theta_L)$, where $\theta_R$ and $\theta_L$ are the angles of the right and left wheels, positive when rolling forwards.

Each wheel rolls on the floor without slipping and without side slip. Let v be the velocity of the centre of the body along the heading. The right wheel is at distance b/2 on the right of the body, and the velocity of its centre along the heading is $v + \tfrac{b}{2}\dot\varphi$ (when the body turns anticlockwise the right side moves faster); pure rolling requires this to equal $r\dot\theta_R$. Likewise, for the left wheel, $v - \tfrac{b}{2}\dot\varphi = r\dot\theta_L$. Adding and subtracting these two equations, and adding the no-side-slip condition, gives three Pfaffian constraints:

$$
\dot x\cos\varphi + \dot y\sin\varphi = \frac{r}{2}(\dot\theta_R + \dot\theta_L), \qquad
\dot x\sin\varphi - \dot y\cos\varphi = 0, \qquad
\dot\varphi = \frac{r}{b}(\dot\theta_R - \dot\theta_L) . \tag{11.5.13}
$$

With five configuration variables and three constraints, the allowed velocities are two-dimensional: the two wheel speeds can be chosen freely, and the velocity of the body then follows. This is the precise meaning of "two driven wheels, three degrees of freedom" in Section 1.6.7.

Of the three constraints, the third is integrable: integrating both sides over time,

$$
\varphi - \frac{r}{b}(\theta_R - \theta_L) = \text{constant} . \tag{11.5.14}
$$

The heading is determined completely by the difference between the two wheel angles, regardless of how the wheels turned in between; this is a holonomic constraint. The other two constraints are not integrable. Hence Eq. (11.5.14) cuts one dimension from the configuration space, which becomes four-dimensional; in this four-dimensional space the allowed velocities at each point are only two-dimensional.

**Example 11.5.5** Let the AGV drive for 4 s under each of two wheel-speed programmes. Programme A: right wheel $\omega_R = 10 + 6\sin 1.3t$, left wheel $\omega_L = 10 - 4\cos 0.7t$ (rad/s, t in s). Programme B: only the right wheel turns for the first 2 s and only the left wheel for the last 2 s, each at constant speed, so that the total angles of the two wheels are the same as in programme A. Compare the end points.

Program 11.5.1 integrates Eq. (11.5.13) with a step of 1 ms (each step integrated exactly along a circular arc). At the end of programme A the right wheel has turned through {{ex11_5.TR:.2f}} rad and the left wheel through {{ex11_5.TL:.2f}} rad, the heading has turned through {{ex11_5.phi_end:.2f}}°, and the centre of the body is at ({{ex11_5.xa:.3f}}, {{ex11_5.ya:.3f}}) m. In programme B the two wheel angles are the same, and so is the heading, as a consequence of Eq. (11.5.14); but the centre of the body stops at ({{ex11_5.xb:.3f}}, {{ex11_5.yb:.3f}}) m, {{ex11_5.gap_xy:.2f}} m away from programme A (Figure 11.5.3b). Throughout, the left-hand side of Eq. (11.5.14) stays zero, deviating by no more than ${{ex11_5.inv_max:sci2}}$ rad, which is mere rounding error.

This example shows the practical consequence of "non-integrable": knowing only how far the wheels have turned does not tell where the vehicle is; one must also know how they turned. This is quite unlike a robot arm. By the product of exponentials formula, the pose of the end-effector of an arm is determined entirely by the joint angles, regardless of how the joints were turned to reach them, because there are only holonomic constraints among the joint angles and between the end-effector pose and the joint angles (Section 6.7). An AGV must integrate the whole history of its wheel speeds step by step to work out its position; this is odometry (Chapter 19). The errors of the integration keep accumulating, so lidar or vision is also needed for localization.

### 11.5.8 Everyday examples

Folding chairs and window stays are four-bar linkages: as they are opened and closed, the positions of all the links are determined by a single angle, which is the result of holonomic constraints. The treadle of a sewing machine is a crank-rocker mechanism: the foot pushes the rocker, and the output is the full rotation of the crank; when it occasionally will not move, it has stopped at a dead point.

Nonholonomic constraints are just as common in daily life. A car cannot drive sideways into a parking space, because its tyres cannot slip sideways; yet by going back and forth a few times it can park in any space slightly longer than itself, which shows that the constraint does not restrict where the car can get to, only how it gets there. An ice skate can only glide along its blade, yet a skater can glide to any spot on the rink and stop facing any direction. When you push a shopping trolley, the rear wheels have fixed directions and the midpoint of the rear wheels can only move along the trolley, exactly like the no-side-slip constraint of the AGV; the front wheels are freely swivelling castors and impose no such constraint.

This concludes the chapter. The next chapter uses the product of exponentials formula to write the forward kinematics of open-chain arms; the kinematics of closed chains will be developed in Chapter 18, and that of wheeled mobile robots in Chapter 19.

**Engineer's notes**

- When designing a four-bar linkage, check the Grashof condition first: if the crank must turn fully, the shortest link must be adjacent to the ground and s + l ≤ p + q. When s + l is very close to p + q, the mechanism passes through nearly collinear positions where force transmission is poor, so leave a margin.
- When a closed-chain mechanism is assembled, the same set of inputs may correspond to several assembly modes. If the controller assumes the wrong assembly mode after power-up (for example, takes the wrong sign in the inverse solution of a Delta), the motor angles it commands will jam the mechanism or even damage it. At initialization, first confirm which assembly mode the mechanism is in.
- The forward kinematics of a parallel mechanism has multiple solutions. In numerical iteration, use the pose of the previous control cycle as the initial guess and check that the iteration converges to the same assembly mode; do not start from a fixed initial guess every time.
- An AGV's odometry obtains the position only by integrating wheel speeds, and the error accumulates with the distance travelled. A wheel-diameter error of 0.5% means an error of about 5 cm for every 10 m driven, and unequal diameters of the two wheels also make the heading drift gradually. Regular correction by lidar, QR-code landmarks or vision is needed.
- To decide whether a velocity constraint is integrable, use Eq. (11.5.12) or the more general Frobenius condition. An integrable constraint should be written directly as a relation among positions, reducing the number of state variables; a non-integrable constraint can only be kept at the velocity level and handled by planning and control (Chapters 19 and 53).
