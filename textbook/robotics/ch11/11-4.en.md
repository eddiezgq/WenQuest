### 11.4.1 The problem: what does 850 mm mean?

The digital factory is to install a UR5e beside a CNC lathe to load and unload it: the robot takes a blank from a rack, feeds it into the chuck, then takes out the finished part and places it, moving vertically downwards, on a conveyor. The manufacturer's datasheet says "reach 850 mm". Example 1.6.1 has already explained that this number is the horizontal distance from the flange centre to the base axis at the zero position; Section 1.3 roughly treated the workspace of the UR5e as a ball of radius 0.85 m.

But when laying out the workstation, the engineer has to answer more specific questions: can the robot reach the farthest slot of the rack? If so, can the gripper point vertically downwards there? Can the space directly above the base be used? If a SCARA were used instead, how large an area could it cover? The answers to all these questions depend on the exact shape of the workspace, and on which orientations the end-effector can take at different places in the workspace. This section first makes the concepts clear on planar arms, then computes the workspaces of the UR5e and of the SCARA by program.

### 11.4.2 Reachable and dexterous workspaces

Section 1.3 called the set of all positions that the end-effector can reach the workspace. In the language of Section 11.3, forward kinematics maps the configuration space into the task space, and the workspace is the set of end-effector positions obtained as the joints vary within their limits, that is, the "image" of forward kinematics.

The end-effector is not just a point; it also has an orientation. At one and the same position, some orientations can be achieved and others cannot. This leads to two kinds of workspace.

**Definition 11.4.1 (Reachable and dexterous workspaces)** The set of positions that the reference point of the end-effector can reach in at least one orientation is called the **reachable workspace**; the set of positions that it can reach in every orientation is called the **dexterous workspace**.

Clearly the dexterous workspace is part of the reachable workspace. "Every orientation" is relative to the task space: for a planar arm the orientation is an angle in the plane; for a general spatial arm it is any rotation in SO(3); for a SCARA it is any heading about the vertical axis. These two concepts were set out systematically by A. Kumar and Kenneth J. Waldron in 1981, for comparing and designing the geometric dimensions of manipulators.

### 11.4.3 Workspaces of planar arms

First consider the planar 2R arm with link lengths $L_1$ and $L_2$, both joints being able to turn through a full circle.

**Theorem 11.4.1** The workspace of the planar 2R arm is the annulus centred at the base

$$
|L_1 - L_2| \le r \le L_1 + L_2 , \tag{11.4.1}
$$

where r is the distance from the end-effector to the base.

*Proof*　Let the end-effector be at p, |p| = r. The elbow joint is at distance $L_1$ from the base and at distance $L_2$ from the end-effector, so the base, the elbow and the end-effector form a triangle with sides $L_1$, $L_2$ and r (which may degenerate into a line segment). By the law of cosines,

$$
r^2 = L_1^2 + L_2^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos\theta_2 . \tag{11.4.2}
$$

As $\cos\theta_2$ runs through [−1, 1], r runs through $[|L_1 - L_2|, L_1 + L_2]$; for each such r, turning joint 1 makes the end-effector sweep the whole circle of radius r. Conversely, if r is outside this range, the triangle inequality fails and the three points cannot form a triangle.　∎

The outer boundary of the annulus corresponds to $\theta_2 = 0$ (arm stretched out), the inner boundary to $\theta_2 = \pi$ (arm folded back). The two positions have something in common: by Eq. (11.4.2), the derivative of r with respect to $\theta_2$, which is $\propto \sin\theta_2$, is zero, and the end-effector "cannot move" in the radial direction. Chapter 16 will show that the determinant of the Jacobian matrix, $L_1L_2\sin\theta_2$, is then zero, and the arm is in a singular configuration. The boundary of the workspace is always made up of singular configurations; this is a general rule: at a non-singular configuration the end-effector can move a little in every direction, so it cannot be on the boundary.

Now add a tool of length $L_3$ to the end-effector, making a planar 3R arm. The tool direction φ is the angle between the tool and the x-axis. For the end-effector to reach p with direction φ, the wrist joint (joint 3) must be at $w = p - L_3(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$, and w must lie in the annulus (11.4.1) formed by $L_1$ and $L_2$.

**Theorem 11.4.2** Let the three links of a planar 3R arm have lengths $L_1$, $L_2$ and $L_3$, with all joints able to turn through a full circle, and let r be the distance from the end-effector to the base. Then:

1. the reachable workspace is $\max(0,\ |L_1 - L_2| - L_3) \le r \le L_1 + L_2 + L_3$;
2. the dexterous workspace consists of the points that satisfy both of the following conditions:

$$
r \le L_1 + L_2 - L_3 , \qquad |r - L_3| \ge |L_1 - L_2| . \tag{11.4.3}
$$

*Proof*　As the direction φ runs through a full turn, the wrist joint w goes once around the circle of centre p and radius $L_3$. The distance of points on this circle from the base is at least $|r - L_3|$ and at most $r + L_3$.

(1) p is reachable if and only if this small circle has a point in common with the annulus (11.4.1), that is, the distance interval $[|r - L_3|,\ r + L_3]$ meets $[|L_1 - L_2|,\ L_1 + L_2]$: $r + L_3 \ge |L_1 - L_2|$ and $|r - L_3| \le L_1 + L_2$. When $L_3 \le L_1 + L_2$ (as for every real arm), the latter becomes $r \le L_1 + L_2 + L_3$ and the former $r \ge |L_1 - L_2| - L_3$.

(2) p is dexterous if and only if the whole small circle lies within the annulus, that is, $r + L_3 \le L_1 + L_2$ and $|r - L_3| \ge |L_1 - L_2|$.　∎

**Example 11.4.1** A planar 3R arm has $L_1 = 0.425$ m, $L_2 = 0.392$ m and $L_3 = 0.1$ m (the UR5e's upper arm and forearm plus a 0.1 m tool). Find the reachable workspace and the dexterous workspace.

By Theorem 11.4.2, $|L_1 - L_2| - L_3 < 0$, so the reachable workspace is the whole disc of radius {{ex11_4.r_out:.3f}} m, with area {{ex11_4.A_reach:.4f}} m². The dexterous workspace consists of two parts: on the outside an annulus {{ex11_4.gap1:.3f}} m ≤ r ≤ {{ex11_4.dex_out:.3f}} m, and in the middle a small disc of radius {{ex11_4.gap0:.3f}} m; the narrow ring 0.066 m wide between the two is not dexterous. The area of the dexterous workspace is {{ex11_4.A_dex:.4f}} m², only {{ex11_4.dex_ratio:.1f}}% of the reachable workspace (Figure 11.4.1).

The small disc in the middle is not very intuitive: when the end-effector is very close to the base, the wrist joint goes around a small circle of radius 0.1 m, and its distance from the base varies between 0.033 m and 0.167 m, all of which happens to fall within the annulus (11.4.1). Program 11.4.1 checks 1,900 distances, each with 720 orientations, one by one, and the result agrees completely with Theorem 11.4.2 (points of disagreement: {{ex11_4.mism_reach}} for reachability, {{ex11_4.mism_dex}} for dexterity).

### 11.4.4 The workspace of the UR5e

The workspace of a spatial arm seldom has an analytic expression and is generally computed numerically. The most direct way is the one of Section 1.3: draw random joint angles and compute the end-effector positions; the sample points outline the reachable workspace (Lab 11.4 does exactly this). But this approach cannot answer the question "in which orientations can a given point be reached?" For that we must work the other way round: take points in space and, for each point and each orientation, solve the inverse kinematics and see whether a solution exists.

The UR5e has an analytic inverse kinematics (Chapter 14), which can quickly decide whether an end-effector pose has a solution. Program 11.4.1 uses two simplifications. First, joint 1 can turn through a full circle, so the workspace is symmetric about the vertical axis of the base, and it suffices to take points on one vertical half-plane: the abscissa ρ is the horizontal distance from the axis and the ordinate is the height z; a 2 cm grid gives {{ex11_4.ncell:,}} grid points in all. Second, rotation of the flange about its own normal is done by joint 6, which changes neither the position of the flange centre nor the direction of the normal; so whether a point can be reached in a given orientation depends only on where the flange normal points. The normal directions are points on the sphere, and the program takes 100 directions evenly spread over the sphere.

For each grid point and each direction the inverse kinematics is solved: if at least one direction has a solution, the grid point belongs to the reachable workspace; if all 100 directions have solutions, it belongs to the dexterous workspace. Each grid point stands for a ring going once around the axis, whose volume is 2πρ times the area of the cell; summing gives the volume:

$$
V = \sum 2\pi\rho\,\Delta\rho\,\Delta z . \tag{11.4.4}
$$

To make sure the decisions are reliable, the program draws 300 grid points at random from those with solutions, takes 5 more directions at each, and substitutes all {{ex11_4.nchk:,}} inverse solutions so obtained back into forward kinematics; they all differ from the target poses by less than $10^{-8}$.

**Example 11.4.2** The workspace of the UR5e flange centre. Ignoring collisions of the arm with itself and with the floor, Program 11.4.1 finds (Figure 11.4.2a):

- The reachable workspace has a volume of about {{ex11_4.V_reach:.2f}} m³. Its outer boundary is about {{ex11_4.d_reach_max:.2f}} m from the shoulder centre (at the height where the axis of joint 2 meets the base axis), farther than 0.85 m, because the two 0.1 m segments of the wrist can also stretch outwards. The estimate of {{ex11_4.V_ball:.2f}} m³ in Section 1.3, from a ball of radius 0.85 m, is too small.
- The dexterous workspace has a volume of about {{ex11_4.V_dex:.2f}} m³, only {{ex11_4.dex_pct:.0f}}% of the reachable workspace; it lies within about {{ex11_4.d_dex_max:.2f}} m of the shoulder centre.
- The region that can be reached with the flange facing vertically downwards (gripper grasping downwards) is about {{ex11_4.V_down:.2f}} m³, {{ex11_4.down_pct:.0f}}% of the reachable workspace.
- With the number of normal directions raised to 400, the reachable and dexterous volumes become {{ex11_4.V_reach4:.2f}} m³ and {{ex11_4.V_dex4:.2f}} m³, differing from those for 100 directions by less than 1.5%, which shows that 100 directions are enough.

Figure 11.4.2a also shows two boundaries fixed directly by the geometric dimensions. The axes of joints 2, 3 and 4 of the UR5e are parallel to one another, and the three links move in one vertical plane; the distance of this plane from the base axis is set by the sideways offsets between the joints, written $d_4$ in the DH parameters; with the dimensions of the parts-library model, $d_4 = W_1 - W_2 + W_3 = 0.134$ m (Section 12.3). The step of the analytic inverse solution that finds joint 1 requires the wrist point, obtained by going back $d_6 = 0.1$ m from the flange centre along the normal, to be at least $d_4$ from the base axis. The flange centre is 0.1 m from the wrist point, so the flange centre is at least $d_4 - d_6 = {{ex11_4.rho_in:.3f}}$ m from the axis: the thin cylinder of radius 3.4 cm around the base axis cannot be reached by the arm in any way. To be reached in every direction, the wrist point must satisfy this condition for every direction, and the flange centre must be at least $d_4 + d_6 = {{ex11_4.rho_dex:.3f}}$ m from the axis. The grid results of Program 11.4.1 agree with these two numbers. The UR user manual also warns users to avoid moving the tool centre close to the cylindrical region directly above and below the base; this is the reason.

Back to the workstation at the beginning of this section. If the farthest slot of the rack is about 0.8 m from the base axis, it lies in the reachable workspace but already outside the dexterous workspace, and the gripper can take only a limited range of directions there; if the gripper must also point vertically downwards, check against the orange line in Figure 11.4.2a. Moving the robot 10 cm closer to the rack, or raising the rack, often brings the key positions into the dexterous region.

### 11.4.5 The workspace of the SCARA

The SCARA is much simpler. Joints 1 and 2 fix the position of the tool in the horizontal plane, joint 3 the height, and joint 4 the heading about the vertical axis. The reachable region in the horizontal plane is the one computed in Example 1.3.1 of Section 1.3: the outer radius is $L_1 + L_2 = 0.6$ m; the inner radius would be $L_1 - L_2 = 0.1$ m, but joint 2 can turn only ±{{ex11_4.sc_t2:.0f}}°, and by Eq. (11.4.2) the inner radius grows to

$$
r_{\min} = \sqrt{L_1^2 + L_2^2 + 2L_1L_2\cos 145^\circ} \approx {{ex11_4.r_sc_min:.3f}}\ \mathrm{m} ;
$$

joint 1 can turn only ±{{ex11_4.sc_t1:.0f}}°, so a piece is missing at the back. With a 2.5 mm grid, Program 11.4.1 finds the area to be {{ex11_4.A_sc:.3f}} m² (in agreement with Example 1.3.1); multiplied by the screw stroke of 0.15 m, this gives a workspace volume of about {{ex11_4.V_sc:.3f}} m³, see Figure 11.4.2b. The reachable workspace of the UR5e is about {{ex11_4.ratio_ur_sc:.0f}} times as large.

The dexterity of the SCARA depends on which task space it is judged against. Its task space is $\mathbb R^3 \times S^1$ (Section 11.3.6), and its orientation is only the heading about the vertical axis; joint 4 can turn ±360°, so at every point of the reachable workspace it can take any heading, and for its own task space the dexterous workspace is the whole reachable workspace. If a general spatial orientation is required, for example tilting the tool by 10°, the SCARA cannot achieve this at any point: its dexterous workspace relative to SO(3) is empty.

This shows clearly how the two designs divide the work. The UR5e has a large workspace with a sizeable dexterous region, suited to loading and unloading, assembly and grinding, which need changes of orientation; the SCARA has a small workspace, but it is dexterous everywhere in its own task space and stiff in the vertical direction, making it fast and accurate in tasks such as planar pick-and-place and insertion. When choosing a robot, first settle the task space, then check whether the shape of the workspace and its dexterous region can cover all the working positions.

### 11.4.6 An everyday example

The region a person's hand can reach while standing still and moving only the arm is called the reach envelope in ergonomics, and it is used in designing workbenches, shelves and vehicle cabs. The human arm, like the UR5e, has reachable and dexterous regions: a book on the top shelf can just be touched with the fingertips with the arm stretched out, which is reachable; but taking the book down supported on an upturned palm is impossible, because that position lies outside the dexterous workspace. Everyday experience also tells us that within an arm's length in front of the chest the hand can take almost any orientation; this is the "dexterous region" of the human arm, which is why fine work is always done in front of the body.

The workspace answers "where can it reach?" The next section turns to a question that never arises in open chains: when the links are joined into closed loops, the joint variables are constrained, and what do the configuration space and the workspace then look like?

**Engineer's notes**

- The manufacturer's "reach" is only a single number. When laying out a workstation, look at the workspace plot and distinguish between reachable and dexterous. Key working positions (grasping and assembly points that need a particular orientation) should lie in the dexterous region, with a margin.
- A computed workspace usually ignores collisions. The part actually usable must exclude collisions of the arm with itself, its base and the mounting surface, as well as interference from cables and the gripper. The cylindrical regions directly above and below the UR5e's base should be avoided.
- The boundary of the workspace is made up of singular configurations. Near the boundary the joint speeds become large and controllability is poor (Chapter 16), so actual work should keep some distance from the boundary.
- A longer tool enlarges the reachable workspace but shrinks the dexterous workspace: by Theorem 11.4.2 the outer radius of the dexterous region is $L_1 + L_2 - L_3$, which gets smaller as the tool gets longer.
- The gap at the back of a SCARA's workspace and the hole on its inner side are often just the places for the cables and the base. When laying out, put the worktable in the sector in front.
