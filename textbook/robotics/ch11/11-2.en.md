### 11.2.1 The problem: how many degrees of freedom does a Delta have?

On the packaging line of the digital factory, a Delta robot is picking parts from a conveyor. It has three motors, all mounted on the fixed base at the top; each of its three active arms is connected to a parallelogram made of two slender rods, and the lower ends of the three parallelograms are all connected to a small moving platform that carries the suction cup (Figure 11.2.2a).

How many degrees of freedom does this robot have? Section 1.4 gave the answer for open chains: the number of degrees of freedom equals the sum of the degrees of freedom of the joints, Eq. (1.4.3). If we applied this rule unchanged, the Delta would have 3 revolute joints and, since both rods of each parallelogram have a spherical joint at each end, 12 spherical joints, giving a sum of 3 + 12 × 3 = 39. This is plainly wrong: three motors are enough to control the moving platform, so it cannot have 39 degrees of freedom.

The trouble is that the Delta is a closed chain. In an open chain every joint variable can be chosen independently; in a closed chain, several legs must "close up" at the moving platform, so there are constraints between the joint variables and they are no longer independent. Section 1.4 pointed out that the degrees of freedom of a closed chain must be computed with Grübler's formula. This section derives the formula, uses it to compute the degrees of freedom of a series of mechanisms, and explains in which cases it gives a wrong answer and how the rank gives the correct one.

### 11.2.2 From counting constraints to Grübler's formula

The idea is first to set everything free, then to constrain. Suppose a mechanism consists of N links, one of which is the ground (the fixed link, for example the base of the Delta or the base of a robot arm), N including the ground; the links are connected by J joints, and the i-th joint has $f_i$ degrees of freedom.

First imagine that all the joints are taken apart, so that the N − 1 moving links move freely in space. By Theorem 1.4.1, each rigid body has m degrees of freedom: m = 6 in space and m = 3 in the plane. After taking the joints apart there are therefore m(N − 1) degrees of freedom in all.

Now put the joints back one by one. By Theorem 11.1.1, the i-th joint imposes $c_i = m - f_i$ constraints (in the plane $c_i = 3 - f_i$; for example, a planar revolute joint imposes 2 constraints: the x and y coordinates of the two links at the pin are the same). If these constraints are independent of one another, each constraint removes one degree of freedom, so that

$$
M = m(N - 1) - \sum_{i=1}^{J} c_i = m(N - 1) - \sum_{i=1}^{J}(m - f_i) . \tag{11.2.1}
$$

Rearranging gives

$$
M = m(N - 1 - J) + \sum_{i=1}^{J} f_i . \tag{11.2.2}
$$

**Theorem 11.2.1 (Grübler's formula)** Suppose a mechanism consists of N links (including the ground) and J joints, the i-th joint having $f_i$ degrees of freedom; take m = 6 for a spatial mechanism and m = 3 for a planar one. If the constraints imposed by all the joints are independent of one another, the number of degrees of freedom M of the mechanism is given by Eq. (11.2.2).

The proof is just the derivation above. The hypothesis of the theorem, "the constraints are independent of one another", is the crux; in Section 11.2.5 below we shall see that it does not always hold.

A note on notation: Eq. (1.4.3) wrote the number of degrees of freedom of an open chain as m; in this section m is reserved for the degrees of freedom of a single rigid body (3 or 6), and the number of degrees of freedom of the mechanism is written M instead.

First check the formula on an open chain. The n joints of an open chain connect n + 1 links in sequence, so N = n + 1 and J = n, and Eq. (11.2.2) gives $M = m(n + 1 - 1 - n) + \sum f_i = \sum f_i$, which is exactly Eq. (1.4.3). In an open chain each joint attaches exactly one new link to the existing chain, so the constraints are always independent; the result of Section 1.4 is thus a special case of Grübler's formula.

The planar formula was first used by the Russian mathematician Pafnuty Chebyshev in 1869, in his study of approximate straight-line linkages (then called "parallelograms"), in the form "3 times the number of moving links minus 2 times the number of hinges". The German mechanism theorist Martin Grübler, in a paper published in 1883 in the journal *Der Civilingenieur* (The Civil Engineer), studied systematically the conditions for a planar kinematic chain to have "constrained motion" and gave this counting formula; in 1917 he summed it up in his monograph *Getriebelehre* (Theory of Mechanisms). In 1929 the German engineer Karl Kutzbach extended it to general spatial mechanisms, that is, to the form with m = 6. The formula is therefore also often called the Chebyshev–Grübler–Kutzbach formula, or the Grübler–Kutzbach formula; this book calls it Grübler's formula for short.

### 11.2.3 Examples

**Example 11.2.1** Find the degrees of freedom of the five planar mechanisms in Figure 11.2.1.

(a) Four-bar linkage: the ground, the crank, the coupler and the rocker, 4 links in all, and 4 revolute joints. In the plane m = 3, and by Eq. (11.2.2), M = 3(4 − 1 − 4) + 4 = 1. So a four-bar linkage needs only one input (for example turning the crank), and the positions of all the other links are then determined. It is one of the most widely used mechanisms in machinery.

(b) Slider-crank mechanism: the ground, the crank, the connecting rod and the slider, with 3 revolute joints and 1 prismatic joint, M = 3(4 − 1 − 4) + 4 = 1. The piston, connecting rod and crankshaft of an engine form this mechanism, only with the slider (the piston) as the input.

(c) Five-bar linkage: 5 links and 5 revolute joints, M = 3(5 − 1 − 5) + 5 = 2. When the two links attached to the ground are each driven by a motor, the end-effector (the pin joining the two couplers) can be placed anywhere within a region of the plane. The planar five-bar robot is the simplest parallel robot.

(d) Parallelogram linkage with an extra crank: 5 links and 6 revolute joints, M = 3(5 − 1 − 6) + 6 = 0. According to the formula, it is a structure that cannot move. But in reality it does move; this is discussed in Section 11.2.5.

(e) Single-stage scissor lift: the two crossed bars, the platform, the upper and lower sliders and the ground, 6 links in all; 5 revolute joints and 2 prismatic joints, M = 3(6 − 1 − 7) + 7 = 1. Push the slider at the bottom, and the platform rises or falls.

**Example 11.2.2** Find the degrees of freedom of the Delta robot (Figure 11.2.2a).

Links: 1 ground, 3 active arms, 6 passive rods (2 per parallelogram) and 1 moving platform, N = 11 in all. Joints: 3 revolute joints between the active arms and the ground; one spherical joint at each end of each passive rod, 12 in all. By Eq. (11.2.2),

$$
M = 6(11 - 1 - 15) + (3 \times 1 + 12 \times 3) = -30 + 39 = {{ex11_2.gd}} .
$$

9 degrees of freedom? Three motors plainly cannot control 9 independent motions. A closer look shows that, since each passive rod has spherical joints at both ends, the rod can spin idly about its own axis, and this rotation does not affect the position of any other link. The 6 rods give 6 such idle spins. A degree of freedom of this kind, which does not affect the rest of the mechanism, is called a **passive degree of freedom** (also called an idle degree of freedom). Removing the 6 passive degrees of freedom leaves 3, exactly the number controlled by the three motors. If the spherical joint at one end of each rod is replaced by a universal joint (f = 2), the rod can no longer spin, and the formula gives directly 6(11 − 1 − 15) + (3 + 6 × 3 + 6 × 2) = {{ex11_2.gd2}}.

What kind of motion are these 3 degrees of freedom? Section 11.5 will show that the three parallelograms keep the moving platform always parallel to the base, so that the moving platform can only translate along x, y and z and cannot rotate. Program 11.2.1 also verifies this with the rank method of Section 11.2.5: at the rest position of the model (the moving platform 0.42 m below the centre of the base, all three active arms turned downwards by {{ex11_2.delta_th_deg:.2f}}°), among all the possible velocities of the mechanism, the twists of the moving platform form a 3-dimensional subspace in which all the angular-velocity components are zero.

**Example 11.2.3** Find the degrees of freedom of the Stewart platform 6-UPS (Figure 11.2.2b).

Each leg consists of two links, a lower and an upper part; the lower part is connected to the ground by a universal joint, the upper part to the moving platform by a spherical joint, and between the two parts is a prismatic joint. Links: the ground, the moving platform and the 12 leg parts, N = 14; joints: 6 universal joints, 6 prismatic joints and 6 spherical joints, J = 18. Hence M = 6(14 − 1 − 18) + (6 × 2 + 6 × 1 + 6 × 3) = {{ex11_2.gst}}. The six prismatic joints are driven by motors; changing the lengths of the six legs controls both the position and the orientation of the moving platform in space. The motion platforms of flight simulators are mechanisms of this kind (Section 11.5, Chapter 18).

### 11.2.4 The loop formula

Equation (11.2.2) can also be written in an equivalent form that helps to show where the constraints of a closed chain come from. Regard the links as "points" and the joints as "lines" connecting two points; the mechanism then becomes a graph. An open chain is a tree, in which N links are connected by N − 1 joints; each additional joint creates one more closed loop. The number of independent loops is therefore

$$
L = J - N + 1 . \tag{11.2.3}
$$

In graph theory this relation is called Euler's formula. L is called the **number of independent loops**. By Eq. (11.2.3), N − 1 − J = −L, and substituting into Eq. (11.2.2),

$$
M = \sum_{i=1}^{J} f_i - mL . \tag{11.2.4}
$$

The meaning of this form is intuitive: first add up the degrees of freedom of all the joints, as though they were all independent; then each closed loop requires that "going once round brings you back to where you started", which in space is 6 equations (3 for position, 3 for orientation) and in the plane 3, so mL is subtracted. A four-bar linkage has L = 1 loop, M = 4 − 3 = 1; the Stewart platform has L = 18 − 14 + 1 = 5 loops, M = 36 − 30 = 6; the Delta has L = 5 loops, M = 39 − 30 = 9.

### 11.2.5 When the formula is wrong: counting with the rank

In Table 11.2.1 there are two places where the formula disagrees with reality: for the parallelogram with an extra crank the formula gives 0 but the truth is 1; for the Bennett linkage the formula gives −2 but the truth is 1. There is also a third kind of disagreement, which does not appear in the table: when a mechanism passes through certain special positions, its number of degrees of freedom suddenly increases. All three problems have the same root: the hypothesis of Theorem 11.2.1, that the constraints are independent of one another, does not hold. To get the correct answer we must actually compute how many of the constraints are independent, that is, the rank of the constraint equations.

**The velocity equation of a single loop.** Consider first a mechanism with only one loop. Suppose the loop contains n single-degree-of-freedom joints in sequence, starting from the ground and passing through the links back to the ground. At a given instant, write the screw axis of the i-th joint at that instant in {s} as $\mathcal S_i$ (note: the axis at that instant, not the axis at the zero position). Going round the loop, the twist of the 1st link relative to the ground is $\mathcal S_1\dot\theta_1$; the twist of the 2nd link relative to the ground is the twist of the 1st link plus the relative motion of the 2nd joint, that is, $\mathcal S_1\dot\theta_1 + \mathcal S_2\dot\theta_2$. Here we use the fact that relative motions, as twists expressed in the same frame, can simply be added, just as in the composition of the velocities of the points of a rigid body (Section 6.3). Continuing in this way, after going once round we are back at the ground, and the twist of the ground relative to itself is of course zero:

$$
\mathcal S_1\dot\theta_1 + \mathcal S_2\dot\theta_2 + \cdots + \mathcal S_n\dot\theta_n = 0 . \tag{11.2.5}
$$

These are 6 linear equations in the n joint rates, called the velocity loop-closure equations of the loop. Arrange the screw axes as a 6×n matrix $J_L = (\mathcal S_1\ \cdots\ \mathcal S_n)$; Eq. (11.2.5) is then $J_L\dot\theta = 0$. By the rank–nullity theorem (Theorem 2.6.2), the joint rates that satisfy it form a subspace of dimension $n - \mathrm{rank}\,J_L$. This dimension is the instantaneous mobility of the mechanism at this position:

$$
M_{\mathrm{inst}} = n - \mathrm{rank}\,J_L . \tag{11.2.6}
$$

For a mechanism with several loops, Eq. (11.2.5) can be written for every loop and the equations stacked together before taking the rank; alternatively, as Program 11.2.1 does, the twist of every moving link and the rate of every joint can all be taken as unknowns, with each joint giving 6 equations saying that "the difference between the twists of the links on its two sides equals a twist the joint allows". Both ways give the same instantaneous mobility.

**Theorem 11.2.2** For any mechanism that can be assembled, at any position, the instantaneous mobility is not less than the value given by Grübler's formula: $M_{\mathrm{inst}} \ge M$.

*Proof*　In the second formulation, the unknowns are the 6(N − 1) twist components of the links and the $\sum f_i$ joint rates, and there are 6J equations. The rank of the coefficient matrix does not exceed the number of equations 6J, so the dimension of its null space is at least $6(N - 1) + \sum f_i - 6J$, which is exactly Eq. (11.2.2). For planar mechanisms the argument is the same with 6 replaced by 3.　∎

Equality holds if and only if the 6J equations are independent of one another, that is, the coefficient matrix has full row rank. Grübler's formula therefore gives a lower bound on the number of degrees of freedom; if the actual number is larger, some constraints are "repeated". We look at the three cases below.

**First case: passive degrees of freedom.** For the Delta of Example 11.2.2 the formula and the rank both give 9 and agree; it is just that 6 of them are idle spins of the rods. Passive degrees of freedom do not change the motion of the other links, and must be removed when we ask how many controllable degrees of freedom a robot has.

**Second case: overconstrained mechanisms.** Because of special geometric conditions, some constraints are automatically repeated and the mechanism has more degrees of freedom than the formula predicts; such a mechanism is called an **overconstrained mechanism**.

The commonest overconstrained mechanisms are in fact the planar mechanisms themselves. If a four-bar linkage is counted as a spatial mechanism, with m = 6, Eq. (11.2.2) gives 6(4 − 1 − 4) + 4 = {{ex11_2.g4s}}: the four-bar linkage cannot move and indeed has two constraints too many. The reason is that its four revolute axes are all perpendicular to the same plane: the screw axis of an axis along z through the point (x, y, 0) is (0, 0, 1, y, −x, 0), whose 1st, 2nd and 6th components are always zero, so these screws all lie in a 3-dimensional subspace and the rank of $J_L$ is at most 3. By Eq. (11.2.6), $M_{\mathrm{inst}} = 4 - 3 = 1$. In other words, the geometric condition "the axes are all parallel" makes 3 of the 6 loop-closure equations satisfied automatically. This is why planar mechanisms are counted with m = 3. In engineering terms, it means that the axes of a planar mechanism must be machined sufficiently parallel; when they are not, a four-bar linkage binds or even jams, and this can only be "absorbed" by clearance in the joints and elastic deformation of the links.

The parallelogram with an extra crank is another example (Figure 11.2.1d). When the three cranks are of equal length and parallel to one another, the coupler translates, and the end of the third crank moves exactly on the circle centred at its pivot; the constraint brought by the third crank is repeated, and the actual number of degrees of freedom is 1. This geometric condition is very demanding: Program 11.2.1 lengthens the third crank by 1 cm, from 0.25 m to 0.26 m, and moves its pin on the coupler outwards by 1 cm accordingly, so that the mechanism can still be assembled in its original position. At this position the instantaneous mobility found from the rank is still 1, yet as soon as crank 1 has turned through 0.5 rad, the third crank would have to lengthen or shorten by {{ex11_2.jam_err_mm:.2f}} mm to keep up. A rigid bar cannot do this, so the mechanism jams. The example also shows that the instantaneous mobility reflects only whether the mechanism "can move a little at this instant" (first order); finite motion depends on second and higher orders as well. At a general position the two are the same; at special positions they can differ.

The most famous spatial overconstrained mechanism is the Bennett linkage, discovered by the British mathematician Geoffrey Thomas Bennett in 1903. By the formula, a spatial single loop of four revolute joints has M = 6(4 − 1 − 4) + 4 = −2, that is, in general a spatial quadrilateral of four links is an "excessively" rigid structure. Program 11.2.1 confirms this: taking four arbitrary lines in space as joint axes, the rank of $J_L$ is {{ex11_2.rank_r}} and the instantaneous mobility is 0. But Bennett found that if the two links of one pair of opposite sides both have length a and twist angle α, the two links of the other pair both have length b and twist angle β (the length of a link is the length of the common perpendicular between the two joint axes at its ends, and the twist angle is the angle between these two axes), and

$$
\frac{a}{\sin\alpha} = \frac{b}{\sin\beta} , \tag{11.2.7}
$$

then the quadrilateral can move continuously, with 1 degree of freedom. Program 11.2.1 takes a = 0.3 m, α = 30° and β = 60°, obtains b ≈ {{ex11_2.bB:.4f}} m from Eq. (11.2.7), verifies that the loop-closure equations hold at a series of positions, and finds that the rank of $J_L$ is {{ex11_2.rank_B}} and the instantaneous mobility 1. The earlier mechanism of Pierre Frédéric Sarrus (1853) is a spatial single loop of six revolute joints for which the formula gives 0, yet it actually has 1 degree of freedom, and it converts rotation into exact straight-line motion. Overconstrained mechanisms are stiff and move precisely, and have many applications in deployable structures and origami mechanisms, but they also demand high accuracy in manufacture and assembly.

**Third case: singular positions.** For some mechanisms the formula is correct at general positions, but when the mechanism passes through certain special positions the constraints temporarily become dependent and the instantaneous mobility increases. Take a parallelogram linkage (ground 0.4 m, crank 0.2 m, coupler 0.4 m, rocker 0.2 m) as an example: at a general position the rank of $J_L$ is 3 and the instantaneous mobility 1. When the crank turns into line with the ground, the four pins all lie on one straight line, the v parts of the four screw axes have only y components, and Program 11.2.1 finds that the rank drops to {{ex11_2.rank_col}} and the instantaneous mobility becomes {{ex11_2.m_col}}. At this position the mechanism can "switch branches": it can carry on moving as a parallelogram, or it can become an antiparallelogram, with the coupler crossing the ground. In mechanism design such positions are avoided, or auxiliary links are added to "carry the mechanism through" them; for example, the coupling rods on the two sides of a locomotive are set 90° out of phase. Problems of this kind are the same thing as the singular configurations of Chapter 16, and Chapter 18 discusses parallel mechanisms systematically.

### 11.2.6 Everyday examples

Folding chairs, ironing boards, car windscreen wipers and window stays are mostly four-bar linkages, which unfold or fold up in a single movement. The scissor lifts common in supermarkets and warehouses are made of several scissor stages in series: in the plane each stage is a structure like that of Example 11.2.1(e), and however many stages there are, the number of degrees of freedom is still 1, so a single hydraulic cylinder can drive the whole platform up and down.

The human arm can also be looked at with Grübler's formula. Take the trunk as the ground, and the upper arm, the forearm and the hand as three links; the shoulder is approximately a spherical joint (f = 3), the elbow a revolute joint (f = 1), and the wrist together with the rotation of the forearm about its own axis approximately a spherical joint (f = 3), 7 degrees of freedom in all, in agreement with the count in Section 1.6.11. Press the palm flat on a table, and between the hand and the table (that is, the ground) there is, in effect, an additional rigid connection with f = 0, forming a loop. By Eq. (11.2.4), M = 7 − 6 × 1 = 1: with the hand still, the arm has 1 degree of freedom left, and this is exactly the motion in Section 1.6.11 in which the elbow can be raised and lowered.

The next section looks at degrees of freedom from another angle: all the possible positions of a mechanism form a "space", the number of degrees of freedom is the dimension of this space, and the shape of the space holds still more information.

**Engineer's notes**

- When designing a mechanism, first count it with Grübler's formula: M should equal the number of motors you plan to use. If M is less than the number of motors, the motors will "fight" one another (unless redundant actuation is intended); if M is greater, there will be uncontrolled motion.
- The formula gives a lower bound. If the actual number of degrees of freedom is larger, look first for passive degrees of freedom (rods with spherical joints at both ends, shafts that can spin freely about their own axes), then for overconstraint (parallel axes, links of equal length).
- Overconstrained mechanisms are sensitive to errors in manufacture and assembly. Three or more parallel cranks, or two or more coaxial bearing housings, all call for tight geometric tolerances; otherwise clearance must be left or compliant elements used to "remove" the redundant constraints.
- Singular positions, such as the collinear position of a parallelogram linkage, a crank in line with the coupler, or the dead point of a slider-crank, must, during motion, either be avoided or be carried through by a flywheel, out-of-phase arrangement or an additional link.
- The idle spin of the passive rods of a Delta is harmless, but it makes the wear of the spherical joints uneven; some designs add springs between the two rods to pull them together, which also takes up the clearance in the spherical joints.
