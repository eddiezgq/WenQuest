---

Before writing down any kinematic equations, this chapter answered the question "how can a robot move?". One view runs through the whole chapter: **Motion is determined by constraints.** Joints are constraints, loops are constraints, and wheels that do not slip sideways are constraints too; degrees of freedom, the configuration space and the workspace are all consequences of constraints.

The constraints of a joint are described most cleanly with screws. The twists a joint allows form an f-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb R^6$, where f is the number of degrees of freedom of the joint; the constraint forces of an ideal joint do no work, so the constraint wrenches are reciprocal to every allowed twist and form a subspace of dimension 6 − f. The three single-degree-of-freedom lower pairs, revolute, prismatic and helical, are each a single screw axis, differing only in pitch. In engineering terms, the former tells us in which direction the motor must drive, and the latter which loads the bearings must carry.

When joints are connected into a mechanism, the degrees of freedom can be counted by first releasing and then constraining: this is Grübler's formula $M = m(N - 1 - J) + \sum f_i$. It assumes that the constraints of the joints are independent of one another, so it gives a lower bound on the number of degrees of freedom. Passive degrees of freedom, overconstraint caused by parallel or intersecting axes (planar mechanisms, the Bennett linkage), and singular positions such as collinear ones all make the actual number of degrees of freedom larger. The truly reliable method is to write the velocity loop-closure equations and find the rank of their coefficient matrix.

All the configurations of a robot form its configuration space, whose dimension is the number of degrees of freedom; its shape carries more information than its dimension. The variable of a revolute joint lies on a circle, and the configuration space of two revolute joints is a torus rather than a plane, so joint interpolation must take wrap-around into account; SO(3) is not homeomorphic to $\mathbb R^3$, so every three-parameter representation of orientation has singularities. In the configuration space the robot shrinks to a point, obstacles become forbidden regions, and motion planning becomes finding a path for a point. Tasks are described in the task space; comparing the dimensions of the configuration space and the task space tells whether a robot is sufficient for a task and whether it is redundant.

The workspace is the image of the forward kinematics. The reachable workspace says where the end-effector can get to, and the dexterous workspace where the end-effector can take any orientation; the latter is much smaller, and it shrinks as the tool gets longer. The workspace of a planar arm has an analytical expression; that of a spatial arm is generally computed by a program: for the UR5e point by point with the analytical inverse solution, for the SCARA directly from the joint limits. The boundary of the workspace consists of singular configurations.

Closed chains give rise to loop-closure equations among the joint variables. Loop-closure equations are holonomic constraints, which cut the configuration space down to lower-dimensional curves or surfaces: the configuration space of a four-bar linkage is a closed curve on a torus, one input corresponds to several assembly modes, and the Grashof condition decides whether the crank can turn through a full revolution. The inverse kinematics of a parallel mechanism is solved leg by leg, while its forward kinematics requires solving simultaneous equations and has multiple solutions, exactly the opposite of a serial arm. Another kind of constraint can only be written as a relation among velocities, the Pfaffian constraint; a non-integrable Pfaffian constraint is a nonholonomic constraint, which does not reduce the dimension of the configuration space and restricts only the direction of motion at each instant. The heading of a differential-drive AGV is determined by its two wheel angles, but its position is not; it must be obtained by integrating the whole history of the wheel speeds.

Chapter 12 will use the joint screws of this chapter to write the forward kinematics of open-chain arms.

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| Kinematic pair (joint) | A movable connection between two links that allows a certain relative motion; surface contact gives a lower pair, point or line contact a higher pair |
| Degrees of freedom and number of constraints of a joint | The dimension f of the subspace of allowed twists; the dimension c = 6 − f of the subspace of constraint wrenches |
| Constraint wrench | A wrench exerted by an ideal joint that is reciprocal to every allowed motion; it determines the loads on the bearings |
| Passive degree of freedom | A degree of freedom that does not affect the rest of the mechanism, such as the rotation of a rod with spherical joints at both ends about its own axis |
| Overconstrained mechanism | Because of special geometry, some constraints automatically repeat others, and the actual number of degrees of freedom exceeds the value given by Grübler's formula |
| Configuration space | The space of all possible configurations of a robot; its dimension equals the number of degrees of freedom |
| Task space | The space of end-effector positions and/or orientations used to describe a task |
| Configuration-space obstacle | The region of the configuration space occupied by configurations that collide with an obstacle |
| Reachable / dexterous workspace | The positions the end-effector can reach with at least one / with any orientation |
| Loop-closure equation | The condition that going once round a loop returns to the starting point; a holonomic constraint among the joint variables of a closed chain |
| Assembly mode | The different configurations of a closed chain for the same set of inputs; they cannot change into one another without passing through a position where two solutions merge |
| Limit position | A position at which the input of a closed chain reaches the end of its range of motion and the two assembly modes meet |
| Holonomic constraint | A constraint that can be written as an equation g(q) = 0 in the configuration variables; it reduces the dimension of the configuration space |
| Pfaffian constraint | A linear constraint on the velocities, A(q)q̇ = 0 |
| Nonholonomic constraint | A non-integrable Pfaffian constraint; it restricts only the direction of motion at each instant and does not reduce the dimension of the configuration space |

### Historical notes

| Year | Person | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| 1676 | Robert Hooke | Described the universal joint in print; today the universal joint is named after Gerolamo Cardano and Hooke |
| 1788 | Joseph-Louis Lagrange | *Méchanique analitique* (*Analytical Mechanics*): describing the position of a whole mechanical system by a set of generalized coordinates, the origin of the idea of the configuration space |
| About 1815 | Johann Friedrich Pfaff | Studied differential equations of the form $\sum a_i dq_i = 0$ (Pfaffian equations) |
| 1853 | Pierre Frédéric Sarrus | Proposed a spatial single loop of six revolute joints that produces exact straight-line motion |
| 1869 | Pafnuty Chebyshev | Used a degree-of-freedom count for planar mechanisms in studying parallel motions (approximate straight-line linkages) |
| 1875 | Franz Reuleaux | *Theoretische Kinematik*: analysed machines into links and kinematic pairs and classified the lower pairs, laying the foundations of the theory of mechanisms |
| 1877 | Ferdinand Georg Frobenius | Gave the general condition for a system of Pfaffian equations to be integrable |
| 1883 | Martin Grübler | Studied systematically the conditions for constrained motion of planar kinematic chains and gave the formula for counting degrees of freedom; summed up in *Getriebelehre* in 1917 |
| 1883 | Franz Grashof | Gave, in the second volume of *Theoretische Maschinenlehre*, the criterion for whether a link of a four-bar linkage can turn through a full revolution |
| 1894 | Heinrich Hertz | Introduced the terms holonomic and nonholonomic constraint in *Die Prinzipien der Mechanik* |
| 1903 | Geoffrey Thomas Bennett | Discovered a movable spatial single loop of four revolute joints (the Bennett linkage) |
| 1929 | Karl Kutzbach | Extended the formula for counting degrees of freedom to spatial mechanisms |
| 1954 | Ferdinand Freudenstein | Proposed the equation between the input and output angles of a four-bar linkage, for the analytical synthesis of mechanisms |
| 1956–1957 | Eric Gough | Published a parallel platform with six extensible legs, used for tyre testing |
| 1965 | D. Stewart | Proposed using a six-degree-of-freedom parallel platform for flight simulation; such platforms were later named after him (also called Gough–Stewart platforms) |
| 1979–1983 | Tomás Lozano-Pérez | Studied robot motion planning with the configuration space as the basic tool |
| 1981 | A. Kumar, Kenneth J. Waldron | Set out the reachable workspace and the dexterous workspace systematically, for use in manipulator design |
| 1988 | Reymond Clavel | Published the Delta parallel robot; US patent granted in 1990 |
| 1993 | Madhusudan Raghavan | Found by polynomial continuation that the forward kinematics of a general Stewart platform has 40 complex solutions |
