title: Chapter Overview
---

Chapter 10 studied the motion of a point and explained that it takes six numbers to describe a rigid body. A robot is made of many rigid bodies, connected to one another by joints. A joint allows some relative motions and forbids the rest. How a whole robot can move depends on the types of its joints and on how its bodies are connected. This chapter answers the basic questions that arise from this: what motion a joint allows and what forces it must carry; how many degrees of freedom a robot or a mechanism has; what kind of space all its possible configurations make up; which places its end-effector can reach; and, when links are connected into a closed loop, what constraints hold between the joint variables. All these questions come before the kinematic equations are written; they decide whether a robot can do a given task, and how many variables, and of what kind, are needed to describe it.

The chapter unfolds as follows:

- Section 11.1 starts from the coupling observed in the ball-screw spline shaft of a SCARA and places the six lower pairs and the universal joint in the framework of the screw theory of Chapter 6: the motion a joint allows is a set of twists, the constraint forces it must carry are the set of wrenches reciprocal to them, and the three single-degree-of-freedom joints, revolute, prismatic and helical, are one and the same kind of screw with different pitches;
- Section 11.2 derives Grübler's formula from the idea "first set free, then constrain", uses it to compute the degrees of freedom of planar mechanisms, the Delta and the Stewart platform, and explains why the formula goes wrong for passive degrees of freedom, for overconstrained mechanisms (the Bennett linkage) and at singular positions, and how the rank of the constraints gives the correct answer;
- Section 11.3 regards all the configurations of a robot as a space, explains that its dimension is the number of degrees of freedom and that its shape (torus, sphere, SO(3)) decides how joint angles "wrap around" and why Euler angles must have singularities, and introduces configuration-space obstacles and the task space;
- Section 11.4 distinguishes the reachable workspace from the dexterous workspace, gives analytical results for planar arms, computes the workspaces of the UR5e and the SCARA by program, and compares how the two structures divide the work;
- Section 11.5 studies closed chains: the loop-closure equations are holonomic constraints that cut the configuration space down to a lower-dimensional curve or surface; the four-bar linkage, the Delta and the Stewart platform illustrate assembly modes, the Grashof condition, and the characteristic of parallel mechanisms that "the inverse solution is easy and the forward solution hard"; finally, a differential-drive AGV shows that a Pfaffian constraint written as a relation between velocities may be non-integrable, and that such a nonholonomic constraint does not reduce the dimension of the configuration space, so that the wheel angles do not determine the position of the vehicle.

The chapter uses the first introduction to robot structure and degrees of freedom in Chapter 1 (Sections 1.4 and 1.6), the rank and null space of Chapter 2, the rotation matrices and Euler angles of Chapter 4, and the twists, wrenches and reciprocal product of Chapter 6; for the workspaces it uses the product-of-exponentials forward kinematics and the dimensions of the UR5e from Chapter 12, and the analytical inverse kinematics that Chapter 14 will treat systematically (here only its results are used). The results of this chapter are used again and again later: the forward kinematics of Chapters 12 and 13 starts from the joint screws of this chapter; the singular configurations of Chapter 16 are exactly the boundaries of the workspaces and the dead points of the closed chains of this chapter; the redundancy of Chapter 17 rests on comparing the dimensions of the configuration space and the task space introduced here; Chapter 18 develops parallel mechanisms, Chapter 19 the nonholonomic constraints of wheeled robots, and the motion planning of Chapter 53 takes place in the configuration space.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. state the degrees of freedom and the number of constraints of the six lower pairs and the universal joint, write down their screw axes and constraint wrenches, and from these decide which loads a joint bearing must carry;
2. compute the degrees of freedom of planar and spatial mechanisms with Grübler's formula, recognize passive degrees of freedom, overconstraint and singular positions, and when necessary find the true degrees of freedom from the rank of the constraints;
3. write down the configuration spaces of common systems, explain why joint angles wrap around and why every three-parameter representation of orientation must have singularities, and distinguish the configuration space from the task space;
4. distinguish the reachable workspace from the dexterous workspace, compute the workspace of a robot arm analytically or by program, and use it for workstation layout and robot selection;
5. write down the loop-closure equations of a closed chain, determine the type and the assembly modes of a four-bar linkage, and solve the inverse kinematics of the Delta and the Stewart platform and the forward kinematics of the Delta;
6. write velocity constraints in Pfaffian form, decide whether they are integrable, and explain the different effects of holonomic and nonholonomic constraints on the configuration space and on motion.
