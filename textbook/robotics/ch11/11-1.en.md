### 11.1.1 The problem: only the tool is turned, so why does it move up or down?

On the assembly line of the WenQuest digital factory there is a SCARA whose screw axes have already been written down in Chapter 12 (Table 12.3.2): joint 3 is a vertical prismatic joint and joint 4 is a revolute joint about a vertical axis. In the parts-library model the two joints have nothing to do with each other. Yet the commissioning engineers on site found that on the real machine, if only the motor of joint 4 is turned, the tool slowly moves up or down while it turns; a compensation term has to be added in the controller, making the motor of joint 3 turn along with it, for the tool to stay at its original height.

The reason lies in how these two joints are built. On many SCARAs the end-effector is carried by a **ball-screw spline** shaft: the outer surface of the shaft carries both a helical screw raceway and straight spline grooves, and two nuts sit on the shaft. The spline nut drives the shaft round through the straight grooves but lets the shaft slide along its own axis; the screw nut engages the shaft through the helical raceway, and when the nut turns it pushes the shaft up or down. Each nut is driven by its own motor. If only the spline nut is turned while the screw nut is held still, the shaft both turns and slides relative to the screw nut, just as a screw turning in a nut advances along its axis as it turns.

This phenomenon raises two questions. First, what kind of joint is "advancing along the axis while turning", and how is it related to the revolute joint and the prismatic joint? Second, which forces must the structure of a joint (bearings, guides, nuts) carry, and which forces are passed on to the motor? This section answers both. We shall see that the screw theory of Chapter 6 gives a single answer to both questions: **the motion a joint allows is a set of twists, and the forces it must carry are the set of wrenches reciprocal to them.**

### 11.1.2 Links, kinematic pairs and kinematic chains

Section 1.4 has already introduced links, joints, kinematic chains, open chains and closed chains. Here we place these ideas in the history of the theory of mechanisms and add the basis for classifying them.

A machine consists of a number of rigid bodies that transmit motion and force to one another through contact. The German engineer Franz Reuleaux, in *Theoretische Kinematik* (*The Kinematics of Machinery*), published in 1875, was the first to make such a "movable connection between two bodies" an object of study in its own right; he called it a "pair of elements" (*Elementenpaar*), and it later became known generally as a **kinematic pair**. His idea was that what makes a machine move in a definite way is not the shape of its parts themselves, but the surfaces through which the parts touch one another and the relative motions that this contact restricts. Taking a machine apart into two kinds of elements, "links" and "kinematic pairs", and then studying how they are joined into chains: this is the starting point of today's theory of mechanisms.

**Definition 11.1.1 (Kinematic pair)** A movable connection in which two links are in direct contact and which allows a certain relative motion between them is called a **kinematic pair**. In robotics, kinematic pairs are usually called joints. By the form of contact, a kinematic pair with surface contact is called a **lower pair**, and one with point or line contact a **higher pair**.

Lower pairs transmit force through surface contact, so the pressure per unit area is low, wear is small and lubrication is easy; the great majority of robot joints are lower pairs. Examples of higher pairs are the meshing of gears (the tooth flanks touch along a line), a cam and its follower, and a wheel rolling on the ground (Section 11.5).

Surface contact also has a geometric meaning: while the two links move relative to each other, the contact surfaces must stay in contact all the time. Imagine the surface of one link "sliding over" the surface of the other in the relative motion without coming away from it; this surface must remain unchanged under such motion. In space, the only surfaces that remain unchanged under a continuous family of rigid-body motions are of six kinds: surfaces of revolution (unchanged under rotation about an axis), prismatic surfaces (unchanged under translation along the generators), helicoidal surfaces (unchanged under a screw motion), circular cylinders (unchanged both under rotation and under translation along the axis), spheres (unchanged under any rotation about the centre) and planes (unchanged under translation and rotation within the plane). Correspondingly, there are exactly six lower pairs: the revolute pair, the prismatic pair, the helical pair, the cylindrical pair, the spherical pair and the planar pair. Reuleaux had already reduced the lower pairs to these six in his book. In robotics, the revolute pair is usually called a **revolute joint**, the prismatic pair a **prismatic joint** and the spherical pair a **spherical joint**.

Robots also make wide use of the **universal joint**. It consists of two pairs of mutually perpendicular hinges and a cross-shaped spider, and is equivalent to two revolute joints in series whose axes intersect and are perpendicular to each other; it is not a lower pair, but it is usually treated as a single joint. The universal joint is named after the Italian scholar Gerolamo Cardano and the English scholar Robert Hooke; Hooke described it in a work of 1676. Today it is found in the steering columns and drive shafts of cars.

### 11.1.3 The motion a joint allows is a set of twists

How can "the relative motion a joint allows" be described precisely in mathematics? Chapter 6 provides a ready-made tool. Let link A be fixed and link B move relative to A. The motion of B at a given instant is described completely by its twist $\mathcal V = (\omega, v)$ (Definition 6.3.1), where ω is the angular velocity and v is the velocity of the point of the body that is at the origin at this instant. What a joint does is to allow certain twists and forbid the rest.

Consider first the single-degree-of-freedom joints. A revolute joint allows B only to turn about a fixed axis; by Eq. (12.1.3), the twists it allows are $\mathcal V = \mathcal S\dot\theta$, $\mathcal S = (\hat\omega, -\hat\omega \times q)$, where $\hat\omega$ is the unit direction vector of the axis, q is any point on the axis and $\dot\theta$ is the joint rate (rad/s). A prismatic joint allows only translation along one direction $\hat v$, $\mathcal S = (0, \hat v)$, Eq. (12.1.4).

A helical joint allows B to turn about the axis and, at the same time, to translate along the axis in a fixed ratio: for every 1 rad turned it advances h along the axis. As in Section 6.1, h is called the pitch, in m/rad; the distance a thread advances in one full turn is called the **lead**, and equals 2πh. By Definition 6.3.3, the screw axis of pitch h is

$$
\mathcal S_{\mathrm H} = \begin{pmatrix} \hat\omega \\ -\hat\omega \times q + h\,\hat\omega \end{pmatrix} . \tag{11.1.1}
$$

The twists it allows are $\mathcal V = \mathcal S_{\mathrm H}\dot\theta$. When the joint turns through θ, the displacement of B is given by the matrix exponential. By Eqs. (6.4.2) and (6.4.6),

$$
e^{[\mathcal S_{\mathrm H}]\theta} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{[\hat\omega]\theta} & (I - e^{[\hat\omega]\theta})\,q + h\theta\,\hat\omega \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{11.1.2}
$$

The first term in the upper right corner is the translation caused by "turning through θ about the axis through q", the same as in Eq. (12.1.2); the second term, $h\theta\,\hat\omega$, is the distance advanced along the axis. This is exactly the screw motion of Theorem 6.4.2.

Now compare the three single-degree-of-freedom joints side by side. A revolute joint is a helical joint with pitch h = 0; the larger h is, the further the joint advances for a given angle; in the limit h → ∞, dividing $\mathcal S_{\mathrm H}$ by h and taking the limit gives $(0, \hat\omega)$, which is exactly a prismatic joint. So the three single-degree-of-freedom lower pairs are really one and the same thing: **a single-degree-of-freedom joint is simply a screw axis.** They differ only in the pitch. This is what "joints as screws" in the title of this section means. Chasles' theorem (Theorem 6.1.2) says that every rigid-body displacement is a screw motion; here we say that the motion allowed by any single-degree-of-freedom lower pair is a screw motion about a fixed screw axis.

Now consider the joints with several degrees of freedom. A cylindrical joint allows both rotation about the axis and translation along it, and the two motions can take place independently or at the same time. The twists it allows are all linear combinations of two screw axes:

$$
\mathcal V = \mathcal S_1\dot\theta_1 + \mathcal S_2\dot\theta_2 + \cdots + \mathcal S_k\dot\theta_k , \tag{11.1.3}
$$

where $\mathcal S_1 = (\hat\omega, -\hat\omega \times q)$ is the rotation and $\mathcal S_2 = (0, \hat\omega)$ the translation along the same axis, k = 2. A universal joint is two revolute screw axes through the same point q, with mutually perpendicular directions; a spherical joint is three revolute screw axes through the centre q, along the three coordinate directions; a planar joint is one rotation perpendicular to the plane and two translations within the plane. These screw axes are listed in Table 11.1.1, with every joint placed in a standard position: the axis along the z direction and through the origin, the centre of the sphere at the origin, and the plane of the planar joint the xy-plane.

::: Table 11.1.1

|---|---|---|---|

Equation (11.1.3) shows that all the twists a joint allows form a subspace of $\mathbb R^6$, namely the subspace spanned by $\mathcal S_1, \ldots, \mathcal S_k$ (Definition 2.6.2). In matrix form, arrange these screw axes as a 6×k matrix $S = (\mathcal S_1\ \cdots\ \mathcal S_k)$; the allowed twists are then the range R(S) of S (Definition 2.6.5).

**Definition 11.1.2 (Degrees of freedom of a joint)** The dimension of the subspace formed by the twists a joint allows is called the number of degrees of freedom of the joint, written f. It equals the rank of the matrix S.

This agrees with the statement in Section 1.4, "the number of independent variables needed to fix the relative position": as many dimensions as the subspace has, so many independent joint rates $\dot\theta_i$ are needed to describe the twists in it, and after integration so many independent joint variables. The degrees of freedom are defined by the rank rather than by "how many screws are listed" in order to guard against repetition: if the axis of a revolute joint is written down twice, there are 2 columns but the rank is still 1. This point is essential when we decide the degrees of freedom of mechanisms in Section 11.2.

### 11.1.4 Constraint wrenches: which forces a joint must carry

While allowing some motions, a joint must prevent the others, and it does so by applying forces and moments to the links, namely constraint forces. Let the joint be ideal, that is, frictionless. Then the constraint forces do no work in any allowed motion: the supporting forces of a bearing neither speed the shaft up nor slow it down. By Eq. (6.6.3), the power of a wrench $\mathcal F = (m, f)$ on a twist $\mathcal V$ is $\mathcal F^{\mathsf T}\mathcal V = m\cdot\omega + f\cdot v$. The constraint forces of an ideal joint therefore satisfy

$$
\mathcal F^{\mathsf T}\mathcal V = 0 \quad \text{for every allowed}\ \mathcal V . \tag{11.1.4}
$$

A wrench that satisfies Eq. (11.1.4) is called a **constraint wrench** of the joint. Equation (11.1.4) is exactly the "reciprocity" of Section 6.6: a constraint wrench is reciprocal to every twist the joint allows (Definition 6.6.2).

**Theorem 11.1.1** If a joint has f degrees of freedom, its constraint wrenches form a subspace of $\mathbb R^6$ of dimension 6 − f.

*Proof*　By Eq. (11.1.3), the allowed twists are $S\dot\theta$ with $\dot\theta$ arbitrary. Equation (11.1.4) holding for every $\dot\theta$ is equivalent to $\mathcal F^{\mathsf T}S = 0$, that is, $S^{\mathsf T}\mathcal F = 0$, which means that $\mathcal F$ belongs to the null space of $S^{\mathsf T}$. $S^{\mathsf T}$ is a k×6 matrix with the same rank as S, namely f. By the rank–nullity theorem (Theorem 2.6.2), the dimension of its null space is 6 − f.　∎

We write c = 6 − f and call it the number of constraints of the joint. The f degrees of freedom and the c constraints together make exactly 6: in any direction, a joint either allows motion or supplies a constraint force, one or the other. The last column of Table 11.1.1 is c.

This theorem has direct engineering significance. Take the revolute joint, with c = 5. With the axis along z and through the origin, the five constraint wrenches can be taken as the forces along the x, y and z directions with lines of action through the origin, and the two couples about the x- and y-axes. In engineering language: the bearing of a revolute joint must carry radial forces (in two directions), an axial force, and the two bending moments that tend to tilt the shaft; the only thing it does not carry is the torque about the joint axis, which can be supplied only by the motor and the speed reducer. So bearings must be checked against all five of these components, while a motor is selected by looking only at the one component about the axis. The crossed roller bearings commonly used in the joints of robot arms are designed precisely to carry all five components at once in a very small space.

The case of the helical joint is more interesting. With the axis along $\hat\omega$ and through the origin, substitute into Eqs. (11.1.1) and (11.1.4): for a force F along the axis and a moment m along the axis, the power is $m\cdot\hat\omega + F\,\hat\omega\cdot(h\hat\omega) = m_{\parallel} + hF$, and for it to vanish we need $m_{\parallel} = -hF$. In other words, an ideal helical joint cannot carry an axial force on its own: an axial force F must be accompanied by a torque of magnitude hF. Conversely, if this torque is not applied, the axial load will set the helical joint turning. This is exactly why a ball screw can be "back-driven": a ball screw has very little friction and comes close to an ideal helical joint, so a vertically mounted screw, when the power is off, turns and slides down under the weight of the load; vertical axes must therefore be fitted with brakes. An ordinary trapezoidal lead screw has large friction and can be self-locking.

(11.1.4) and the vanishing of the reciprocal product one by one.

### 11.1.5 Example: the ball-screw spline shaft

We now return to the question at the start of this section.

**Example 11.1.1** The ball-screw spline shaft at the end of a SCARA arm is mounted vertically at (0.6, 0, z) m, and the lead of the screw is 20 mm. (1) With the screw nut held still, the spline nut turns the shaft through 90° (clockwise seen from above, that is, a positive turn about the −z-axis). Where does the point (0.62, 0, 0.2) m on the shaft, 0.02 m from the axis and 0.2 m high, move to? (2) For the tool only to turn, without moving up or down, how should the screw nut turn? (3) The lower end of the shaft carries an axial load of 50 N. How large a torque is needed to keep it still?

(1) The shaft makes a screw motion relative to the screw nut. Take $\hat\omega = (0, 0, -1)$ (pointing down, in agreement with the positive direction of joint 3 of the SCARA) and q = (0.6, 0, 0) m. The pitch is

$$
h = \frac{20\ \mathrm{mm}}{2\pi\ \mathrm{rad}} \approx {{ex11_1.h_mm:.4f}}\ \mathrm{mm/rad} .
$$

By Eq. (11.1.2), after turning through θ = π/2 the shaft advances $h\theta = {{ex11_1.dz_mm:.1f}}$ mm along $\hat\omega$, that is, it moves down by 5 mm; at the same time the point turns through 90° about the axis. Program 11.1.1 computes this in three ways, by the matrix exponential of Eq. (11.1.2), by the series definition of the matrix exponential, and by the geometric procedure "first turn about the axis, then move along it"; all three give (0.6, −0.02, {{ex11_1.z_end:.3f}}) m.

(2) The screw motion of the shaft relative to the screw nut is determined by the difference between the angles turned by the two nuts. If the screw nut turns through the same 90° in the same direction as the spline nut, there is no relative rotation between the shaft and the screw nut, hence no relative screw motion, and the shaft only turns, without moving up or down. So when the controller issues the command "turn joint 4 through 90°", it must at the same time make the motor of the screw nut turn through {{ex11_1.comp_deg:.0f}}°. In the joint space of the controller this appears as a coupling term between the motor angles of joints 3 and 4; the parts-library model treats joints 3 and 4 as an independent prismatic joint and revolute joint, which amounts to assuming that this compensation has already been made by the controller.

(3) By Section 11.1.4, the axial force F of an ideal helical joint is accompanied by a torque hF. For F = 50 N,

$$
\tau = hF = \frac{0.02\ \mathrm{m}}{2\pi} \times 50\ \mathrm{N} \approx {{ex11_1.tau:.4f}}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} .
$$

Without this torque, the shaft would turn and move along its axis under the load. The torque is very small, which is why a ball screw can produce a very large thrust with a fairly small motor; but for the same reason it cannot hold the load when the power is off.

### 11.1.6 Joints in robots

Comparing Table 11.1.1 with the robots of this book shows what each kind of joint is used for in robots.

Serial robot arms use revolute joints almost exclusively. The six joints of the UR5e and the seven joints of the Panda are all revolute joints, each driven by a motor through a speed reducer. Revolute joints are compact, a single bearing can carry all five constraint components, and the range of rotation is large. The Panda's gripper has two prismatic joints, driven by one actuator and opening and closing symmetrically (Section 1.4).

Prismatic joints are usually not realized "directly". A linear motor can produce linear motion directly, but it is expensive; more common is a rotary motor with a ball screw, that is, a helical joint that turns rotation into translation. Joint 3 of the SCARA is built this way: kinematically it is a prismatic joint, structurally it is a helical joint plus a constraint (the spline) that stops the shaft from turning, and Example 11.1.1 is precisely a consequence of this structure.

Parallel mechanisms make heavy use of joints with several degrees of freedom. Each **leg** of a Delta robot consists of an active arm and a parallelogram: the active arm is connected to the fixed base by a revolute joint, and the two rods of the parallelogram each have a spherical joint at both ends, connecting them to the end of the active arm and to the **moving platform** respectively (Section 11.5). The six legs of a Stewart platform commonly have the structure "universal joint – prismatic joint – spherical joint". For brevity, the theory of mechanisms describes a leg by a string of joint letters; for example, the Stewart platform is written 6-UPS, meaning six identical UPS legs. The joints driven by motors (called active joints) are underlined, as in 6-U$\underline{\mathrm P}$S, which says that the prismatic joints are active. In the same notation the UR5e is a 6R arm and the SCARA an RRPR arm.

The hip joints of legged and humanoid robots need large three-dimensional rotations. The human hip is a spherical joint, but it is hard to build a spherical joint for a robot that can be both driven and loaded heavily, so it is usually replaced by three revolute joints in series whose axes intersect (almost) at one point; for example, the hip of the humanoid robot G1 has three revolute joints, pitch, roll and yaw. When the three axes meet at one point, by Table 11.1.1 the subspace spanned by three revolute screws through the same point is exactly the same as for a spherical joint, so kinematically it is a spherical joint driven by three motors.

Higher pairs are also common in robots, although they usually do not appear in the kinematic model: the meshing gears in a speed reducer (Chapter 32), the rolling contact between the wheels of an AGV and the ground (Section 11.5, Chapter 19), and the contact between fingers and objects (Chapter 33). The contact between wheels and ground is especially important; it brings a special kind of constraint, discussed in Section 11.5.

### 11.1.7 Everyday examples

In daily life the seven joints are everywhere. A door hinge is a revolute joint, and a drawer runner a prismatic joint. A bottle cap is a helical joint: each turn of the cap moves it along the bottle neck by one lead; if the lead is 3 mm, unscrewing the cap two turns raises it by 6 mm (the everyday scene of Lab 11.1). The column of an office chair is a cylindrical joint: sitting on the chair you can turn, and also move up and down on the gas spring, and the two motions do not affect each other. The universal joint in a car's steering column lets the rotation of the steering wheel pass through a bend to reach the steering gear. The human shoulder and hip are spherical joints, whereas the "spherical joints" of robots are mostly three revolute joints. A mouse sliding on a desk is a planar joint: it can translate freely on the desk and turn about a vertical axis, but it cannot leave the desk or tilt, f = 3, c = 3.

A joint is a set of screws, and its number of degrees of freedom is the dimension of the subspace these screws span. The next section connects many joints into a mechanism and answers the question: how many degrees of freedom does the whole mechanism have?

**Engineer's notes**

- Select joint bearings against all the components of the constraint wrenches: a revolute joint has 2 radial forces, 1 axial force and 2 tilting moments, 5 components in all. Selecting the motor by torque alone without checking the bearing's tilting moments is a common cause of early failure in robot-arm joints.
- The efficiency of a ball screw can exceed 90%, close to an ideal helical joint, so after power-off it will be back-driven by the load. Vertically mounted screw axes (the Z axis of a SCARA, the Z axis of a gantry robot) must be fitted with a holding brake, and the distance the axis falls at power-off must be considered in the design.
- On a ball-screw spline shaft the Z axis and the rotary axis are coupled: when only the rotary axis turns, the displacement in Z equals lead × (angle / 360°). The controller parameters usually include a setting for this coupling compensation; after the screw is replaced (with a different lead) it must be changed accordingly, or the tool will strike the workpiece or lift too high when it turns.
- The speeds on the two sides of a single universal joint are not equal: when the input shaft turns at constant speed and the two shafts are at an angle, the speed of the output shaft fluctuates periodically, and the larger the angle the larger the fluctuation. Where constant-velocity transmission is needed, use two universal joints arranged symmetrically (a double universal joint) or a constant-velocity joint.
- The range of rotation of a spherical joint is limited by the opening of the socket, typically only a cone angle of ±20° to ±45°. When designing a parallel mechanism, check that the angles of all the spherical joints stay within their limits throughout the workspace.
