### 11.3.1 The problem: turn 270° or turn 90°?

The planar 3R arm of Chapter 12 takes the lengths of the UR5e's upper arm and forearm, 0.425 m and 0.392 m (Section 12.1). Removing the last link gives a planar two-link arm, called a planar 2R arm for short. Imagine such a 2R arm moving above a horizontal worktable, and suppose it has to move from configuration A to configuration B: the joint angles of A are $\theta_A = (150^\circ, -60^\circ)$, those of B are $\theta_B = (-120^\circ, 60^\circ)$. Angles are positive anticlockwise, and $\theta_2$ is the angle of the forearm relative to the upper arm. On the worktable stands a vertical post, which seen from above is a circle of centre (0.45, 0.35) m and radius 0.10 m; the origin of coordinates lies on the axis of joint 1 (Figure 11.3.2a).

The simplest approach is to let both joint angles change uniformly from their start values to their goal values, that is, to follow a straight line in the $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ plane. Joint 1 has to go from 150° to −120°, turning through −270°. Program 11.3.1 checks for collision point by point along this path and finds that the upper arm hits the post when it has turned to $\theta_1 \approx {{ex11_3.th1_hit:.0f}}^\circ$.

Yet if joint 1 turns in the positive direction from 150° to 180°, a further 60° brings it to the position −120°: −120° and 240° are the same angle. In this direction joint 1 needs to turn only {{ex11_3.d1_torus:.0f}}°, and it does not touch the post on the way. The two paths end in the same configuration, and the second is both shorter and safe.

The first approach went wrong by treating the joint angles as coordinates on an ordinary plane. The joint angles are indeed two numbers, but "the space in which the two numbers live" is not a plane: 180° and −180° are the same point. This section studies the space made up of all possible configurations of a robot. Its dimension is the number of degrees of freedom, and its "shape" decides how distances are measured and paths are planned in it; it also explains the root of the Euler-angle singularity of Chapter 4.

### 11.3.2 Configurations and configuration space

Section 1.4 called the positions of all the parts of a mechanism, taken together, its configuration. The number of independent variables needed to fix the configuration is the number of degrees of freedom. We now look at all the possible configurations together.

**Definition 11.3.1 (Configuration space)** The set of all possible configurations of a robot (or mechanism) is called its **configuration space**, or C-space for short. A point of the configuration space represents one configuration of the robot; a motion of the robot is a continuous curve in the configuration space.

The dimension of the configuration space is the number of degrees of freedom. Describing motion in the language of configuration space goes back to the *Méchanique analitique* (*Analytical Mechanics*) of Joseph-Louis Lagrange of 1788 (2nd ed. *Mécanique analytique*, 1811): he described the position of a whole mechanical system by a set of generalized coordinates, and regarded the motion of the system as the change of these coordinates with time. In robotics, Tomás Lozano-Pérez, in a series of works from 1979 to 1983, made "configuration space" the basic tool of motion planning: the robot, which has volume, becomes a point in the configuration space, the obstacles become forbidden regions of the configuration space, and the planning problem becomes that of finding a path for a point in the configuration space (Section 11.3.5, Chapter 53).

For an open-chain robot the configuration is completely determined by the joint variables, and the configuration space is the "product" of the spaces in which the joint variables lie. The variable of a prismatic joint is the displacement along its guide, and takes values in an interval. The variable of a revolute joint is an angle, and since the angles θ and θ + 2π represent the same position, the space of its values is a circle, written $S^1$. The configuration space of two revolute joints is the product of two circles, $S^1 \times S^1$, called the two-dimensional **torus** and written $T^2$. The configuration space of n revolute joints is the n-dimensional torus $T^n$.

Why is the product of two circles a torus? Restricting both $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ to [−180°, 180°) gives a square (Figure 11.3.1a). The left edge of the square, $\theta_1 = -180^\circ$, and the right edge, $\theta_1 = 180^\circ$, are the same set of configurations and should be glued together; the same goes for the top and bottom edges. First glue the top and bottom edges together, and the square rolls up into a tube; then glue the two open ends of the tube (the left and right edges) together, and we obtain a surface shaped like a doughnut, that is, a torus (Figure 11.3.1b). A path that seems to "leave through the right edge" of the square simply carries on around the tube on the torus. The second path of Section 11.3.1 is exactly such a path: it crosses the right edge of the square at $\theta_1 = 180^\circ$ and comes back in through the left edge.

### 11.3.3 Topology: same dimension, different shape

The line and the circle are both one-dimensional, the plane and the torus both two-dimensional; what distinguishes them is not the dimension but the overall shape. The branch of mathematics that studies this kind of "shape" is called **topology**: it is concerned with properties that are unchanged under continuous deformation, where stretching and bending are allowed but neither tearing apart nor gluing different points together. From this point of view a coffee cup and a doughnut "have the same shape" (each has exactly one hole), whereas a doughnut and a ball do not.

**Definition 11.3.2 (Homeomorphism)** If there exists a one-to-one correspondence f: X → Y between two spaces X and Y such that f and its inverse are both continuous, then f is called a **homeomorphism**, and X and Y are said to be homeomorphic.

Put simply, homeomorphic means "can be continuously deformed into one another without tearing or gluing". Two homeomorphic spaces are regarded as the same space in topology.

**Theorem 11.3.1** The circle $S^1$ is not homeomorphic to the line $\mathbb R$, nor to any interval.

*Proof*　Suppose f: $S^1$ → $\mathbb R$ is a homeomorphism. Take any point P on $S^1$ and remove it; what remains is still connected (one can go continuously along the circle from any point to any other without passing through P). A homeomorphism maps connected sets to connected sets, so $\mathbb R$ with f(P) removed should also be connected. But a line with one point removed falls into two pieces, and a point on the left cannot be reached continuously from the right without passing through f(P). This is a contradiction. For an interval, removing an interior point likewise splits it into two pieces, and the argument is the same.　∎

This seemingly simple theorem has an immediate consequence: it is impossible to label the positions on the whole of a circle by one real number continuously and one-to-one. Any "angle" coordinate must jump somewhere, for example from just under 180° to −180°. This is the root of the "wrap-around" problem of joint angles in programs.

The same reasoning extends to higher dimensions. The torus $T^2$ is not homeomorphic to the plane, so the coordinates formed by two joint angles must jump at the edges of the square. The unit vectors pointing in the directions of space (for example the direction of a camera's optical axis) form the sphere $S^2$, which is not homeomorphic to the plane either: every map is torn open or squashed somewhere, and the failure of latitude and longitude at the North and South Poles is one example.

The rotations in three dimensions, which form SO(3), are more complicated. SO(3) is three-dimensional, but it is homeomorphic neither to $\mathbb R^3$ nor to the three-dimensional sphere. By Section 4.6, every rotation corresponds to two unit quaternions that are negatives of each other, and the unit quaternions form the three-dimensional sphere in four-dimensional space; so SO(3) is the space obtained from the three-dimensional sphere by gluing each pair of opposite points into one point. This topological property means that there are no coordinates made of three numbers that are continuous and free of singularities for all rotations. Section 4.5 already pointed out that gimbal lock is not a defect of one particular Euler-angle convention but is unavoidable for any three-parameter representation; in the language of this section, Euler angles are only a "local map" of SO(3), which must fail somewhere. Program 11.3.1 computes the Jacobian matrix (9×3) of the map from ZYX Euler angles to rotation matrices: in a general orientation its rank is {{ex11_3.rank_gen}}; at a pitch angle of 90° the rank drops to {{ex11_3.rank_sing}}, and the three angles can describe rotations in only two directions.

The joints of a real robot have limits, and its configuration space is part of the space given in the table. The UR5e has one peculiarity: in the parts-library model every joint except joint 3 (the elbow, ±180°) has a range of ±360°. The joint angles θ and θ + 360° put the arm in the same configuration, but the controller regards them as different, because the cable is twisted differently in the two cases. The controller's "space of joint values" is therefore larger than the configuration space, and one configuration corresponds to several points in it. This needs attention in planning (Engineer's notes).

### 11.3.4 Explicit and implicit representations

There are two ways of representing a point of the configuration space in a computer.

The first is an **explicit representation**: a minimal set of coordinates, as many as there are degrees of freedom, for example the joint angles $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$, Euler angles, or latitude and longitude. Its advantage is that it uses the fewest numbers and is easy to understand; its drawback is that, by Theorem 11.3.1 and its generalizations, on most configuration spaces such coordinates must jump or become singular somewhere.

The second is an **implicit representation**: the configuration space is regarded as a "surface" in an ordinary space of higher dimension, represented by more numbers, together with the constraints that these numbers satisfy. For example, a point on the circle is represented by the two numbers $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, with the constraint that the sum of their squares is 1; a rotation is represented by the 9 numbers of a rotation matrix, with the constraints $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ and $\det R = 1$ (6 independent constraints, Chapter 4), or by the 4 numbers of a unit quaternion, with the constraint that its norm is 1. An implicit representation has no singularities; the price is more numbers and the need to maintain the constraints (the orthogonalization of Section 4.8 is exactly a way of maintaining the constraints).

Robot software usually uses both representations side by side: joint angles are stored as explicit angles, but wrap-around is taken into account when computing distances and interpolating; orientations are stored implicitly as rotation matrices or quaternions and converted to Euler angles only for display to people.

For joint angles, wrap-around is easy to handle. The shortest angle along the circle between two angles $\theta_a$ and $\theta_b$ is

$$
\Delta\theta = \mathrm{wrap}(\theta_b - \theta_a), \qquad \mathrm{wrap}(x) = x - 2\pi\left\lfloor \frac{x + \pi}{2\pi} \right\rfloor , \tag{11.3.1}
$$

where ⌊·⌋ denotes rounding down to an integer, and wrap(x) brings any angle into [−π, π). Equation (11.3.1) gives the shortest turn about each joint on the torus.

**Example 11.3.1** Find the shortest joint motion along the torus between the configurations A and B of Section 11.3.1, and check it for collision.

Subtracting directly gives $\theta_B - \theta_A = (-270^\circ, 120^\circ)$. By Eq. (11.3.1), the shortest turn of joint 1 is wrap(−270°) = {{ex11_3.d1_torus:.0f}}°, and that of joint 2 is {{ex11_3.d2:.0f}}°. Program 11.3.1 takes 2,001 points along each of the two paths and computes the shortest distance from the two links (treated as line segments) to the centre of the post. On the first path (joint 1 turning {{ex11_3.d1_naive:.0f}}°) the distance falls below 0.10 m at $\theta_1 \approx {{ex11_3.th1_hit:.0f}}^\circ$, and a collision occurs; the second path (joint 1 turning +90°) is collision-free throughout. The end-effector positions at the ends of the two paths are exactly the same.

### 11.3.5 Configuration-space obstacles

The post is a circle in the workspace; what does it look like in the configuration space? Divide the configuration space into a 1° grid and check, at each grid point, whether the two-link arm touches the post. The grid points where it does form the "shadow" of the post in the configuration space, called a **configuration-space obstacle** (the grey region in Figure 11.3.2b). Its shape bears no resemblance to the post: it is a curved band. Program 11.3.1 finds that it occupies {{ex11_3.frac_pct:.2f}}% of the configuration space; checking a random sample of 200,000 configurations gives {{ex11_3.frac_mc_pct:.2f}}%, in agreement.

In the configuration space the robot shrinks to a point, the obstacles become grey forbidden regions, and a collision-free motion is a curve that does not enter them. In this way robots and obstacles of every shape are brought together into one problem: "finding a path for a point in a region". The price is that configuration-space obstacles can hardly ever be written down as analytic expressions, and as the dimension grows the number of grid points rises sharply: in two dimensions 360 × 360 gives about 130,000 grid points, in six dimensions the number is of the order of $360^6$, far too many to check one by one. Chapter 53 will introduce the probabilistic roadmap and the rapidly exploring random tree, which check only a small sample of configurations.

### 11.3.6 Task space

The configuration space describes the robot itself. But tasks are usually not stated in terms of joint angles: "put the part at (0.5, 0.3, 0.1) m with its opening facing down", "follow this weld seam", "aim the camera at this point". The space used to describe a task is often different from the configuration space.

**Definition 11.3.3 (Task space)** The space, made up of the position and/or orientation of the end-effector, that is used to describe a task is called the **task space**.

The choice of task space depends on the task itself. Ordinary grasping and assembly require the end-effector to take any position and orientation; the task space is SE(3), six-dimensional. A 2R arm drawing lines on a plane cares only about the position of the pen tip, and its task space is the plane $\mathbb R^2$. The moving platform of a Delta robot can only translate, and in picking only the position of the suction cup matters, so the task space is $\mathbb R^3$. A SCARA picking and placing parts on an assembly line cares about the position and the heading about the vertical axis, and its task space is $\mathbb R^3 \times S^1$, four-dimensional; this kind of motion, "three-dimensional translation plus rotation about one fixed direction", is called Schoenflies motion after the German mathematician Arthur Schoenflies, and the SCARA and many four-axis parallel picking robots realize this motion. In spray painting, the rotation of the spray gun about its own axis does not matter, and the task space can consist of the position and the spray direction alone, five-dimensional.

Forward kinematics is a map from the configuration space to the task space: given a configuration, it computes where the end-effector is in the task space. The product-of-exponentials formula of Chapter 12 is a concrete way of writing this map. The map has two properties worth noting.

First, it is in general not one-to-one: different configurations can give the same end-effector position. For the planar 2R arm, the end-effector reaches (0.55, −0.25) m with two sets of joint angles: elbow up $({{ex11_3.up1:.2f}}^\circ, {{ex11_3.up2:.2f}}^\circ)$ and elbow down $({{ex11_3.dn1:.2f}}^\circ, {{ex11_3.dn2:.2f}}^\circ)$; Program 11.3.1 verifies with forward kinematics that the two solutions give the same end-effector position. The UR5e has 8 solutions for a general end-effector pose (Chapter 14), and the redundant Panda has infinitely many (Chapter 17).

Second, it is in general not "onto": only part of the task space can be reached. The part that can be reached is called the workspace, and is the subject of the next section.

Comparing the dimensions of the two spaces gives a first judgement of how well a robot matches a task. Let the configuration space be n-dimensional and the task space k-dimensional. If n < k, the robot cannot perform a general task; for example, a SCARA cannot tilt its tool. If n = k, in general each reachable end-effector pose corresponds to a finite number of configurations. If n > k, the robot is redundant for this task, and one end-effector pose corresponds to an (n − k)-dimensional set in the configuration space. Redundancy is relative to the task: the UR5e is not redundant for general grasping, but used for spray painting (k = 5) it is redundant by one dimension.

### 11.3.7 An everyday example: the two hands of a clock

The hour hand and the minute hand on a clock face are each a revolute joint, and the configuration space of the two hands together is the torus $T^2$, as for the planar 2R arm. But the gears inside the clock couple the two hands: for each full turn of the minute hand, the hour hand turns 1/12 of a turn, that is, $\theta_m = 12\,\theta_h$ (modulo 360°). So the configurations the two hands can actually take are only a closed curve on the torus: it goes once around the torus in the hour-hand direction while going 12 times around in the minute-hand direction (the orange curve in Figure 11.3.1). The constraint of the gearing shrinks the two-dimensional configuration space to a one-dimensional curve; this is exactly the kind of constraint to be discussed in Section 11.5.

The two hands overlap where this curve meets the diagonal $\theta_m = \theta_h$. Counting the intersection points on the square, or solving $6t - 0.5t = 360k$ (t in minutes; the minute hand turns 6° per minute, the hour hand 0.5°) to get $t = 720k/11$, shows that in 12 hours the two hands overlap {{ex11_3.crossings}} times, not 12 times. The first overlap after 12 o'clock is at {{ex11_3.t_first:.2f}} minutes, that is, at about {{ex11_3.t_first_s:.0f}} seconds past 1:05. Program 11.3.1 counts the same 11 intersection points numerically.

The configuration space tells us which configurations the robot can be in; the task space tells us in what terms a task is described. The next section answers the question that lies between the two: which parts of the task space can the robot's end-effector actually reach?

**Engineer's notes**

- Handle wrap-around before interpolating in joint space. For a joint that can turn ±360° (such as every joint except the elbow in the parts-library UR5e model), the same target angle has two values within the range; choose the one closer to the current value that does not exceed a limit. Otherwise the robot may "go the long way round", turning through most of a circle, and may even pull on its cables.
- Some joints can turn without limit (such as the end-effector flange axis of some robots); in planning software they should be declared as "continuous joints", with distances computed by Eq. (11.3.1). If they are declared wrongly, the planned paths will contain pointless full turns.
- Represent orientations internally by rotation matrices or quaternions; do not interpolate or integrate Euler angles. Use Euler angles only for display and manual input, and issue a warning when the pitch angle approaches ±90°.
- Configuration-space obstacles are continuous near the joint limits and across the ±180° edges; when checking them on a plot, remember that the edges of the square are "glued", and do not mistake an obstacle for two separate pieces.
