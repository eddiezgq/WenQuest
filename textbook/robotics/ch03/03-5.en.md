### 3.5.1 The problem

The transformation formula (3.4.5) of Section 3.4 reads

$$
(A_a)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{3}\sum_{l=1}^{3} r_{ik}\,r_{jl}\,(A_b)_{kl} .
$$

This formula is not complicated, but it already carries two summation signs. When we come to the inertia tensor of Chapter 23, the recursive dynamics of Chapter 25 and the elasticity of Chapter 31, we often have to handle quantities of third and fourth order, and a single formula may contain four or five summation signs: tedious to write and easy to misread. On the other hand, many vector identities (the vector triple product formula, for example) take a long string of terms to verify by expanding them directly in components.

This section introduces a compact way of writing: **index notation** and the **summation convention**. It is the common language of continuum mechanics, relativity and many robotics papers, and it has a direct counterpart in programs, the NumPy function `einsum`. This section is advanced material; beginners may skip it at first and come back to it when needed. The main text of the other chapters of this book uses it only in a few derivations.

### 3.5.2 Index notation

Components are denoted by letters with subscripts: the components of a vector **a** in some frame are written $a_i$ (i = 1, 2, 3), and the entries of the component matrix of a tensor are written $A_{ij}$. Writing $a_i$ does not mean one particular component; it stands for any one of the three. An equation in which i runs through 1, 2, 3 stands for three equations. For example, $c_i = a_i + b_i$ is shorthand for the three equations $c_1 = a_1 + b_1$, $c_2 = a_2 + b_2$, $c_3 = a_3 + b_3$, that is, **c** = **a** + **b**.

In this section all components are taken in the same frame, so the subscript that names the frame is left out; when two frames have to be distinguished, we write $(p_a)_i$, $(A_b)_{kl}$ and so on.

### 3.5.3 The summation convention

**Definition 3.5.1 (Summation convention)** If an index appears exactly twice in a term, the term is summed over that index from 1 to 3, and the summation sign is not written.

Under this convention,

$$
\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b} = a_ib_i = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3 . \tag{3.5.1}
$$

The product of a matrix and a vector, the product of two matrices, and the trace are written

$$
(Av)_i = A_{ij}v_j , \qquad (AB)_{ij} = A_{ik}B_{kj} , \qquad \mathrm{tr}\,A = A_{ii} . \tag{3.5.2}
$$

The two transformation laws of Sections 3.3 and 3.4 become

$$
(p_a)_i = r_{ij}\,(p_b)_j , \qquad (A_a)_{ij} = r_{ik}\,r_{jl}\,(A_b)_{kl} , \tag{3.5.3}
$$

where the $r_{ij}$ are the entries of $R_{ab}$. An index that appears twice and is summed over is called a **dummy index**, such as j, k and l above; an index that appears only once and is not summed is called a **free index**, such as i, and i and j in the second formula. A dummy index may be renamed at will: $a_ib_i$ and $a_kb_k$ are exactly the same, just as the variable of integration in a definite integral may be renamed. A free index, on the other hand, counts the equations, and every term on both sides of an equation must have the same free indices.

Using the summation convention requires only three rules:

1. In one term, an index may appear at most twice. An index appearing twice is summed; one appearing once is not. A term such as $a_ib_ic_i$, with an index appearing three times, has no meaning and must be rewritten, for example as $\sum_i a_ib_ic_i$.
2. Every term on both sides of an equation must have the same free indices. $a_i = b_j$ is wrong.
3. The position of an index (which subscript it is) must agree with its meaning in the matrix: $A_{ij}v_j$ is Av, whereas $A_{ji}v_j$ is $A^{\mathsf T}v$.

The summation convention was introduced by Albert Einstein in *The Foundation of the General Theory of Relativity*, published in 1916. According to a friend's recollection, Einstein once remarked half in jest that he had made a "great discovery" in mathematics: wherever an index appearing twice was summed over, he had left out the summation sign.

### 3.5.4 The Kronecker delta

**Definition 3.5.2 (Kronecker delta)** 

$$
\delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1, & i = j, \\ 0, & i \neq j. \end{cases} \tag{3.5.4}
$$

The **Kronecker delta** $\delta_{ij}$ is simply the entries of the identity matrix I. It is named after the German mathematician Leopold Kronecker. The fact that the three axes of a frame are mutually perpendicular and of length 1 can be written as one formula, $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_i\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_j = \delta_{ij}$.

The basic use of δ is "renaming": multiplying by it and summing over one of its indices replaces that index by the other one,

$$
\delta_{ij}v_j = v_i , \qquad \delta_{ij}A_{jk} = A_{ik} , \qquad \delta_{ii} = 3 . \tag{3.5.5}
$$

The first formula holds because, of the three terms in the sum over j, only the one with j = i is non-zero. The last formula is the trace of the identity matrix. With δ, the orthogonality of the direction cosine matrix, $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$, is written $r_{ki}r_{kj} = \delta_{ij}$.

### 3.5.5 The Levi-Civita symbol

**Definition 3.5.3 (Levi-Civita symbol)** 

$$
\varepsilon_{ijk} = \begin{cases} +1, & (i, j, k)\ \text{is an even permutation of}\ (1, 2, 3)\ \text{:}\ (1,2,3), (2,3,1), (3,1,2), \\ -1, & (i, j, k)\ \text{is an odd permutation of}\ (1, 2, 3)\ \text{:}\ (1,3,2), (2,1,3), (3,2,1), \\ 0, & i, j, k\ \text{not all different}. \end{cases} \tag{3.5.6}
$$

Only 6 of the 27 values are non-zero. One way to remember them: place 1, 2, 3 on a circle; a triple read in the direction 1 → 2 → 3 → 1 gives +1, and one read the other way gives −1 (Figure 3.5.1(a)). This is exactly the cyclic order in the cross product formulas (3.2.8) and (3.2.9). Swapping any two indices changes the sign of ε; cycling all three indices leaves ε unchanged. The symbol is named after the Italian mathematician Tullio Levi-Civita, who together with Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro founded tensor analysis (Section 3.4).

The **Levi-Civita symbol** writes the cross product, the cross-product matrix, the determinant and the scalar triple product each in one line:

$$
(\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b})_i = \varepsilon_{ijk}\,a_jb_k , \qquad [a]_{ij} = -\varepsilon_{ijk}\,a_k , \tag{3.5.7}
$$

$$
\det A = \varepsilon_{ijk}\,A_{i1}A_{j2}A_{k3} , \qquad (\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b})\cdot\boldsymbol{c} = \varepsilon_{ijk}\,a_ib_jc_k . \tag{3.5.8}
$$

To verify the first formula, take i = 1. Of the nine terms in the sum over j and k, only the two with (j, k) = (2, 3) and (3, 2) are non-zero, namely $+a_2b_3$ and $-a_3b_2$: exactly the first component of Eq. (3.2.9). The cases i = 2, 3 are the same. The second formula follows from the first and $[a]b = a\times b$: $[a]_{ij}b_j = \varepsilon_{ikj}a_kb_j = -\varepsilon_{ijk}a_kb_j$. The third formula is another way of writing the expansion of a determinant by columns, and the fourth combines it with Theorem 3.2.6. From the antisymmetry of ε, the fourth formula at once gives the invariance of the scalar triple product under cyclic permutation and its change of sign when two vectors are swapped.

### 3.5.6 The ε–δ identity

The product of two ε's summed over one index can be expressed with δ. This is the single most useful formula in index notation.

**Theorem 3.5.1 (ε–δ identity)** 

$$
\varepsilon_{ijk}\,\varepsilon_{imn} = \delta_{jm}\delta_{kn} - \delta_{jn}\delta_{km} . \tag{3.5.9}
$$

*Proof*　The left side is summed over i. If j = k or m = n, the left side is zero; the right side for j = k is $\delta_{jm}\delta_{jn} - \delta_{jn}\delta_{jm} = 0$, and for m = n it is zero in the same way. Now let j ≠ k and m ≠ n. For the left side to be non-zero, i must differ from both j and k, so i is the number in {1, 2, 3} other than j and k, and {m, n} must be the same pair of numbers as {j, k}. If m = j and n = k, the two ε's are equal and their product is +1, while the right side is 1 − 0 = 1; if m = k and n = j, the two ε's differ by one swap and their product is −1, while the right side is 0 − 1 = −1; if {m, n} ≠ {j, k}, the left side is zero and both terms on the right are zero too. In every case the two sides are equal.　∎

The free indices j, k, m, n have 3⁴ = 81 combinations in all. Program 3.5.1 checks every one of them; in {{ex3_5.n_nonzero_cases}} of them the two sides are non-zero.

With Eq. (3.5.9), the vector triple product formula takes only three lines:

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\boldsymbol{a}\times(\boldsymbol{b}\times\boldsymbol{c})]_i
&= \varepsilon_{ijk}\,a_j\,(\varepsilon_{kmn}b_mc_n) = \varepsilon_{kij}\,\varepsilon_{kmn}\,a_jb_mc_n \\
&= (\delta_{im}\delta_{jn} - \delta_{in}\delta_{jm})\,a_jb_mc_n \\
&= b_i\,(a_jc_j) - c_i\,(a_jb_j) .
\end{aligned}
$$

The first line uses the cyclic invariance $\varepsilon_{ijk} = \varepsilon_{kij}$ to give the two ε's the same first index, so that Eq. (3.5.9) applies; the third line renames with δ. The result is exactly Eq. (3.2.14). Compared with the proof in Section 3.2.6, which chose a special frame, this proof is pure mechanical computation and needs no geometric idea at all. That is the strength of index notation.

### 3.5.7 ε and δ are tensors too

δ and ε take the same values in every frame. This may look self-evident, but it is in fact a property that has to be checked. By Eq. (3.5.3), the components of δ as a second-order tensor in another frame should be $r_{ik}r_{jl}\delta_{kl} = r_{ik}r_{jk} = (RR^{\mathsf T})_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$, so δ is indeed unchanged. ε has three indices; if it is regarded as a third-order tensor, under a change of frame it should become

$$
r_{il}\,r_{jm}\,r_{kn}\,\varepsilon_{lmn} = (\det R)\,\varepsilon_{ijk} . \tag{3.5.10}
$$

For fixed (i, j, k), the left side is the determinant of the 3×3 matrix whose three rows are rows i, j and k of R (Eq. (3.5.8) written by rows): when (i, j, k) = (1, 2, 3) it is det R; swapping two of the indices swaps two rows, and both sides change sign; when two indices are equal, the matrix has two equal rows and both sides are zero. For a transformation between right-handed frames, det R = 1 and ε is unchanged; for a mirror reflection, det R = −1 and ε changes sign. This is the same fact as "the cross product changes sign under a mirror reflection" in Section 3.3.4. A tensor whose components are the same in every frame is called an isotropic tensor, and δ and ε are the two most important examples.

### 3.5.8 Using the summation convention in programs

The NumPy function `einsum` accepts the summation convention directly: a string states the indices of each array and which indices the result keeps. For example:

```python
import numpy as np
c   = np.einsum("i,i->", a, b)               # a_i b_i, dot product
Av  = np.einsum("ij,j->i", A, v)             # A_ij v_j, matrix times vector
trA = np.einsum("ii->", A)                   # A_ii, trace
A_a = np.einsum("ik,jl,kl->ij", R, R, A_b)   # Eq. (3.5.3): tensor to another frame
axb = np.einsum("ijk,j,k->i", eps, a, b)     # Eq. (3.5.7): cross product
```

An index that appears twice on the left of the arrow and not on the right is a dummy index and is summed over; the indices on the right of the arrow are the free indices. A program written this way matches the formulas in the book one to one, and the subscripts are hard to get wrong. For a simple matrix product, writing `R @ A_b @ R.T` directly is faster; the strength of `einsum` lies in handling quantities with three or more indices.

**Example 3.5.1** Use index notation to compute the component $(P_a)_{23}$ of the projection tensor of Example 3.4.1.

By Eq. (3.5.3), $(P_a)_{23} = r_{2k}\,r_{3l}\,(P_b)_{kl}$, a sum of nine terms over k and l. Since $P_b = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, 0)$, the six terms with k ≠ l and the one with k = l = 3 are all zero, leaving only

$$
(P_a)_{23} = r_{21}r_{31} + r_{22}r_{32} .
$$

$R_{ab}$ is a turn of 20° about the x axis, so $r_{21} = r_{31} = 0$, $r_{22} = \cos 20^\circ$ and $r_{32} = \sin 20^\circ$, and therefore $(P_a)_{23} = \cos 20^\circ\sin 20^\circ = {{ex3_5.P23:.4f}}$, which agrees with the entry in row 2, column 3 of $P_a$ in Example 3.4.1. Program 3.5.1 computes it in three ways, by summing term by term, by matrix multiplication and with `einsum`, and gets the same result.

### 3.5.9 An everyday example

A supermarket receipt is one application of the summation convention: if item i has unit price $p_i$ and quantity $q_i$, the total is $p_iq_i$. The SUMPRODUCT function of a spreadsheet does exactly this. Index notation merely extends this everyday arithmetic of "multiply matching entries, then add" to quantities with two or three indices.

**Engineer's notes**

- When writing a program with `einsum`, first write the formula with indices on paper, then copy it letter by letter into the string. Check two things: that the indices on the right of the arrow are exactly the free indices of the formula, and that each dummy index appears in exactly two places.
- The argument `optimize=True` of `numpy.einsum` chooses a faster order of computation automatically; the difference is marked with three or more arrays.
- In the literature indices may be upper or lower (such as $a^i$ and $a_i$); that is the notation for general oblique coordinate systems. This book uses only Cartesian frames, in which upper and lower indices make no difference, so all indices are written as subscripts.
- After finishing a derivation in index notation, it is best to check it once in a program with random data, as Program 3.5.1 does; this can catch sign errors in the derivation.
