### 3.2.1 The problem

When a robot does work such as polishing or deburring, the axis of the tool must be roughly perpendicular to the surface of the workpiece. The process specification often states that the angle between the tool axis and the surface normal must not exceed 5°. The controller knows the direction of the tool axis, and the vision system gives the direction of the surface normal; both are vectors. How do we compute the angle from their components?

Another problem comes from the force sensor. A six-axis force/torque sensor is mounted at the wrist of the arm, and the gripper holds a part. The centre of mass of the part is not directly below the centre of the sensor, so its weight not only pulls the sensor downwards but also exerts a twisting action on it; this is a **moment**. A moment has a magnitude and also a direction (the axis about which it twists), and it is determined jointly by the force and the point where the force acts. How do we compute the moment from the force and the position of the point of application?

Both problems require "multiplying" two vectors together: the first gives a number (the cosine of the angle), the second gives a new vector (the moment). These are the two products of this section: the dot product and the cross product. As before, we first define them geometrically, so that they do not depend on the frame; then we derive their component formulas for computation.

### 3.2.2 The dot product

**Definition 3.2.1 (Dot product)** The **dot product** of two vectors **a** and **b** is a number:

$$
\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b} = |\boldsymbol{a}|\,|\boldsymbol{b}|\cos\theta , \tag{3.2.1}
$$

where θ (0 ≤ θ ≤ π) is the angle between the two vectors. If either **a** or **b** is the zero vector, the dot product is defined to be 0.

The dot product uses only two lengths and an angle, so it does not depend on the frame. Its geometric meaning is a projection. If $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}$ is a unit vector, then

$$
\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{e}} = |\boldsymbol{a}|\cos\theta , \tag{3.2.2}
$$

which is exactly the signed length of the projection of **a** on the direction of $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}$ (Figure 3.2.1(a)): positive when θ is acute, negative when θ is obtuse, and 0 when θ = 90°. In general, **a**·**b** equals the length of the projection of **a** on the direction of **b** times |**b**|.

The definition gives at once: **a**·**b** = **b**·**a**; **a**·**a** = |**a**|²; two non-zero vectors are perpendicular if and only if their dot product is 0. Less obvious is the distributive law **a**·(**b** + **c**) = **a**·**b** + **a**·**c**. It suffices to prove it when **a** is a unit vector; for the general case, multiply both sides by |**a**|. In that case the equation says that the projection of **b** + **c** on the direction of **a** equals the sum of the projections of **b** and **c**. Place **b**, **c** and **b** + **c** head to tail to form a triangle, and project the triangle onto the line of **a**; the projections of the three sides also join head to tail, and the first two segments add up to the third. The distributive law follows.

**Theorem 3.2.1 (Component formula for the dot product)** If **a** and **b** have components $a_a = (a_1, a_2, a_3)^{\mathsf T}$ and $b_a = (b_1, b_2, b_3)^{\mathsf T}$ in a Cartesian frame {a}, then

$$
\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3 = a_a^{\mathsf T}\, b_a . \tag{3.2.3}
$$

*Proof*　Expand **a** and **b** by Eq. (3.1.1) and use the distributive law to split the dot product into nine terms $a_i b_j\,(\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_i\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_j)$, where $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_1, \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_2, \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_3$ stand for $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a, \hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a, \hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a$ in turn. The three axes are mutually perpendicular and of length 1, so $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_i\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_j = 1$ when i = j and 0 when i ≠ j. Of the nine terms only $a_1b_1$, $a_2b_2$, $a_3b_3$ remain.　∎

The left side of Eq. (3.2.3) does not depend on the frame by definition, and the right side is an operation on components. So whichever Cartesian frame $a_a^{\mathsf T} b_a$ is computed in, the result is the same: the dot product is another invariant. Taking **b** = **a** brings us back to Eq. (3.1.4).

In Eq. (3.2.3), take **b** to be the unit vector of one axis of {a}, for example $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a$, whose components are $(1, 0, 0)^{\mathsf T}$; the dot product is then $a_1$. This gives a result used again and again throughout the book.

**Theorem 3.2.2** The components of a vector in a frame equal its dot products with the unit vectors of the axes of that frame:

$$
p_1 = \boldsymbol{p}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a, \qquad p_2 = \boldsymbol{p}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a, \qquad p_3 = \boldsymbol{p}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a . \tag{3.2.4}
$$

This is just another way of writing Eq. (3.1.5). Its advantage is that the angles need not be found first: as long as the directions of the axes are known, taking dot products gives the components. Section 3.3 uses it to derive the formula that transforms components between two frames.

Extending "projection" from the axes to an arbitrary direction gives the **projection** formula. Let $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ be a unit vector. Any vector **v** can be split uniquely into a part along $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ and a part perpendicular to $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$:

$$
\boldsymbol{v}_\parallel = (\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\,\hat{\boldsymbol{u}} , \qquad
\boldsymbol{v}_\perp = \boldsymbol{v} - (\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\,\hat{\boldsymbol{u}} . \tag{3.2.5}
$$

The first is the vector "whose length is the projection and whose direction is $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$"; the second is what is left. It is easy to verify that $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_\perp = \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v} - (\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}) = 0$, that is, $\boldsymbol{v}_\perp$ really is perpendicular to $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$. The first step in deriving Rodrigues' formula in Chapter 4 is exactly this decomposition.

By Eqs. (3.2.1) and (3.2.3), the angle between two vectors can be found directly from their components:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{a_a^{\mathsf T} b_a}{|\boldsymbol{a}|\,|\boldsymbol{b}|} . \tag{3.2.6}
$$

**Example 3.2.1** In the base frame {a}, the axis of a polishing tool has the direction (0.05, 0.12, −0.99) (not normalized), and the outward normal of the workpiece surface is $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}} = (0,\ -\sin 10^\circ,\ \cos 10^\circ)^{\mathsf T}$. The tool should press onto the surface along the inward normal $-\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$. Check whether the angle meets the requirement of not exceeding 5°.

First normalize the tool axis: its length is {{ex3_2.u_norm}}, and the unit vector is $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}} = ({{ex3_2.u1:.4f}},\ {{ex3_2.u2:.4f}},\ {{ex3_2.u3:.4f}})^{\mathsf T}$. By Eq. (3.2.6),

$$
\cos\theta = \hat{u}_a^{\mathsf T}(-\hat n_a) = {{ex3_2.u2:.4f}} \times {{ex3_2.s10:.4f}} + {{ex3_2.u3:.4f}} \times (-{{ex3_2.c10:.4f}}) = {{ex3_2.cos_t}} ,
$$

so θ = {{ex3_2.theta:.2f}}°, which meets the requirement. Next, split $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ by Eq. (3.2.5): the part along the normal is $(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{n}})\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$, with $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{n}} = {{ex3_2.un}}$; the part along the surface is $u_\perp = {{ex3_2.u_perp}}$, of length {{ex3_2.u_perp_len:.4f}}, which is exactly sin θ. This part along the surface tells "which way the tool is leaning", and the controller uses it to decide in which direction to correct the orientation.

The most common use of the dot product in physics is power: when a force **F** acts on a point moving with velocity **v**, the power is P = **F**·**v**. Only the component of the force along the motion does work; the component perpendicular to the motion does none. Chapter 6 extends this relation to wrenches and twists.

### 3.2.3 The cross product

**Definition 3.2.2 (Cross product)** The **cross product** **a** × **b** of two vectors **a** and **b** is a vector. Its length is

$$
|\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b}| = |\boldsymbol{a}|\,|\boldsymbol{b}|\sin\theta , \tag{3.2.7}
$$

that is, the area of the parallelogram with **a** and **b** as adjacent sides, θ being the angle between them; its direction is perpendicular to **a** and **b** and is fixed by the **right-hand rule**, that is, when the fingers of the right hand curl from **a** towards **b** through the angle smaller than 180°, the thumb points in the direction of **a** × **b** (Figure 3.2.1(b)). If **a** and **b** are parallel (including the case where one is the zero vector), **a** × **b** = **0** by definition.

Like the dot product, this definition uses only lengths, an angle and a direction, so it does not depend on the frame. The definition gives directly:

- **Anticommutativity**: **b** × **a** = −**a** × **b**. When the fingers of the right hand curl from **b** towards **a**, the thumb points the opposite way;
- **a** × **a** = **0**; two non-zero vectors are parallel if and only if their cross product is the zero vector;
- (k**a**) × **b** = k(**a** × **b**).

The distributive law of the cross product, **a** × (**b** + **c**) = **a** × **b** + **a** × **c**, is not as obvious as that of the dot product. The argument below also reveals another geometric meaning of the cross product, which Chapter 4 will use again.

**Theorem 3.2.3** Let $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ be a unit vector. Then $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\times\boldsymbol{b}$ equals the vector obtained by turning the perpendicular part of **b**, $\boldsymbol{b}_\perp = \boldsymbol{b} - (\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{b})\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$, through 90° about $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ by the right-hand rule. Hence the cross product satisfies the distributive law.

*Proof*　$\boldsymbol{b}_\perp$ has length |**b**| sin θ, and turning it through 90° does not change its length, in agreement with Eq. (3.2.7). The turned vector is perpendicular to $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ (since it is turned in the plane perpendicular to $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$) and also perpendicular to $\boldsymbol{b}_\perp$, hence perpendicular to $\boldsymbol{b} = \boldsymbol{b}_\perp + (\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{b})\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$; and since it is turned by the right-hand rule, its direction also agrees with the definition. So the two are equal.

Distributive law: taking the perpendicular part is linear (by the distributive law of the dot product), and turning through 90° in the plane carries a parallelogram into a congruent parallelogram, so it also preserves addition. Hence $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\times(\boldsymbol{b} + \boldsymbol{c}) = \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\times\boldsymbol{b} + \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\times\boldsymbol{c}$. For a general **a** = |**a**|$\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$, multiply both sides by |**a**|.　∎

With the distributive law, the component formula can be derived as for the dot product. First look at the cross products of the axes. For a right-handed frame, the right-hand rule gives

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a\times\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a = \hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a , \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a\times\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a = \hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a , \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a\times\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a = \hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a , \tag{3.2.8}
$$

that is, in the cyclic order x → y → z → x, the cross product of the first two axes is the third; in the reverse order there is a minus sign. Eq. (3.2.8) can also serve as the definition of a right-handed frame: three mutually perpendicular unit axes form a right-handed frame if and only if $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a\times\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a = \hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a$.

**Theorem 3.2.4 (Component formula for the cross product)** In a right-handed Cartesian frame {a},

$$
(\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b})_a = \begin{pmatrix} a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2 \\ a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3 \\ a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{3.2.9}
$$

*Proof*　Expanding **a** × **b** gives nine terms $a_i b_j\,(\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_i\times\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}_j)$. The three terms with i = j are zero; the other six follow from Eq. (3.2.8) and anticommutativity, for example $a_1 b_2\,\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a\times\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a = a_1 b_2\,\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a$ and $a_2 b_1\,\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a\times\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a = -a_2 b_1\,\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a$. Collecting terms by axis gives the result.　∎

A way to remember Eq. (3.2.9): the 1st component is obtained from the 2nd and 3rd components by "multiplying crosswise and subtracting", the 2nd from the 3rd and 1st, and the 3rd from the 1st and 2nd, the subscripts always following the cyclic order 1 → 2 → 3 → 1. This formula holds only in a right-handed frame: in a left-handed frame, each equation in (3.2.8) acquires a minus sign, and so does Eq. (3.2.9). This is one of the reasons why the book uses only right-handed frames.

The cross product answers the second question at the start of this section. When a force **F** acts at a point P, its moment about a point O is defined as **m** = **r** × **F**, where **r** is the vector from O to P. The magnitude of the moment, |**r**||**F**| sin θ, equals the magnitude of the force times the distance from O to the line of action of the force (the lever arm), and its direction is the axis about which the force tends to turn the body about O, fixed by the right-hand rule.

**Example 3.2.2** The origin O of the force sensor frame {a} is at the centre of the sensor, with the z axis pointing vertically upwards. The gripper holds a part of mass 1.5 kg whose centre of mass is at $r_a = (0.08,\ 0.03,\ -0.10)^{\mathsf T}$ m relative to O. Taking g = 9.81 m/s², find the moment of the part's weight about O.

The weight is $F_a = (0,\ 0,\ {{ex3_2.Fz}})^{\mathsf T}$ N. By Eq. (3.2.9),

$$
m_a = \begin{pmatrix} 0.03\times({{ex3_2.Fz}}) - (-0.10)\times 0 \\ (-0.10)\times 0 - 0.08\times({{ex3_2.Fz}}) \\ 0.08\times 0 - 0.03\times 0 \end{pmatrix}
= {{ex3_2.m_vec}}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} .
$$

The magnitude of the moment is {{ex3_2.m_len:.4f}} N·m, and its direction is horizontal. This agrees with geometric intuition: the weight acts vertically downwards, and the distance from its line of action to O (the lever arm) is the horizontal distance from the centre of mass to "the vertical line through O", $\rho = \sqrt{0.08^2 + 0.03^2} = {{ex3_2.rho:.4f}}$ m, so the magnitude of the moment is ρ × {{ex3_2.mg}} N = {{ex3_2.m_len:.4f}} N·m; the weight tends to twist the part about a horizontal axis perpendicular to the horizontal line from O to the centre of mass (Figure 3.2.2). The z component is 0, which shows that the weight does not tend to turn the part about the vertical axis.

### 3.2.4 The skew-symmetric matrix form of the cross product

Each component in Eq. (3.2.9) is a linear combination of $b_1, b_2, b_3$, with coefficients depending only on **a**. In the language of Chapter 2, "taking the cross product with **a**" is a linear transformation, and can be written as a 3×3 matrix times $b_a$. Writing out the coefficients gives the following definition.

**Definition 3.2.3 (Cross-product matrix)** For $a_a = (a_1, a_2, a_3)^{\mathsf T}$, define

$$
[a] = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -a_3 & a_2 \\ a_3 & 0 & -a_1 \\ -a_2 & a_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} ,
\qquad [a]\, b_a = (\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b})_a . \tag{3.2.10}
$$

[a] is made from the components of the vector **a** in a particular frame; in another frame the matrix changes too (Section 3.4). Vectors with other letters are written in the same way; for example, the cross-product matrix of the angular velocity **ω** is written [ω]. Comparing row by row with Eq. (3.2.9) verifies the second equation.

[a] has a striking property: its transpose equals its negative,

$$
[a]^{\mathsf T} = -[a] . \tag{3.2.11}
$$

A square matrix with $A^{\mathsf T} = -A$ is called a **skew-symmetric matrix**. The entries on the main diagonal of a skew-symmetric matrix are all 0 (since $a_{ii} = -a_{ii}$), and the entries on either side of the main diagonal are negatives of each other, so a 3×3 skew-symmetric matrix has only three independent numbers.

**Theorem 3.2.5** The map $a_a \mapsto [a]$ is a one-to-one correspondence between $\mathbb{R}^3$ and the set of all 3×3 skew-symmetric matrices, and it is linear: $[\alpha a + \beta b] = \alpha[a] + \beta[b]$. Moreover, $[a]\,a_a = 0$ and $b_a^{\mathsf T}[a]\,b_a = 0$.

*Proof*　Every 3×3 skew-symmetric matrix has the form (3.2.10), and the three entries below the main diagonal, $a_3$, $-a_2$, $a_1$, determine $a_a$ uniquely; so the correspondence is one-to-one. Linearity is clear from the definition. $[a]a_a$ is the component column of **a** × **a** = **0**. $b_a^{\mathsf T}[a]b_a = \boldsymbol{b}\cdot(\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b}) = 0$, since **a** × **b** is perpendicular to **b**.　∎

Anticommutativity also gives $(\boldsymbol{b}\times\boldsymbol{a})_a = -[a]\,b_a = [a]^{\mathsf T} b_a$; that is, "taking the cross product with **a** on the right" corresponds to the matrix $[a]^{\mathsf T}$.

The matrix form of the cross product turns a geometric operation into matrix multiplication, so that it can appear in the same expression as rotation matrices, Jacobian matrices and so on. This is a basic technique of kinematics throughout the book: Rodrigues' formula in Chapter 4, twists in Chapter 6 and Jacobian matrices in Chapter 16 all use it. Different books write this matrix differently: Lynch and Park's *Modern Robotics* writes [ω], and this book follows that notation; the book by Murray, Li and Sastry writes $\hat\omega$.

For Example 3.2.2,

$$
[r] = {{ex3_2.skew_r}} ,
$$

and multiplying it by $F_a$ gives the same $m_a$ as Eq. (3.2.9). Program 3.2.1 computes the moment in three ways (the component formula, matrix multiplication, and lever arm times force) and checks them against each other.

### 3.2.5 The scalar triple product and the determinant

Combining the cross product and the dot product, (**a** × **b**)·**c** is a number, called the **scalar triple product** of the three vectors. It has a clear geometric meaning (Figure 3.2.3(a)): |**a** × **b**| is the area of the parallelogram with **a** and **b** as adjacent sides; the projection of **c** on the direction of **a** × **b** is the height h (with sign) of the parallelepiped that has this parallelogram as its base and **c** as a lateral edge. So the scalar triple product is the volume of the parallelepiped: positive when **c** and **a** × **b** are on the same side of the base, that is, when **a**, **b**, **c** in this order form a right-handed set; negative when they are on opposite sides. When the three vectors are coplanar, the volume is 0.

::: Figure 3.2.3
src: fig3_2_3
caption: (a) The scalar triple product (a×b)·c is the signed volume of the parallelepiped with edges a, b, c; (b) the geometric meaning of the vector triple product: each cross product with û turns the part perpendicular to û through 90° in the perpendicular plane, and after two of them it becomes −v⊥
:::

**Theorem 3.2.6 (Scalar triple product and determinant)** If **a**, **b**, **c** have components $a_a$, $b_a$, $c_a$ in a right-handed frame {a}, then

$$
(\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b})\cdot\boldsymbol{c} = \det\begin{pmatrix} a_a & b_a & c_a \end{pmatrix}
= \det\begin{pmatrix} a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \\ a_3 & b_3 & c_3 \end{pmatrix} , \tag{3.2.12}
$$

that is, the scalar triple product equals the determinant of the matrix whose columns are the three component columns. The scalar triple product is unchanged by cyclic permutation:

$$
(\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b})\cdot\boldsymbol{c} = (\boldsymbol{b}\times\boldsymbol{c})\cdot\boldsymbol{a} = (\boldsymbol{c}\times\boldsymbol{a})\cdot\boldsymbol{b} , \tag{3.2.13}
$$

and interchanging any two of the vectors changes its sign.

*Proof*　By Eqs. (3.2.3) and (3.2.9),

$$
(\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b})\cdot\boldsymbol{c} = c_1(a_2b_3 - a_3b_2) - c_2(a_1b_3 - a_3b_1) + c_3(a_1b_2 - a_2b_1) ,
$$

which is exactly the expansion of the determinant in Eq. (3.2.12) along the third column (by the Laplace expansion theorem of Section 2.1, expansion along any row or any column gives the same result). By Theorem 2.1.4, interchanging two columns changes the sign of the determinant; a cyclic permutation of the columns amounts to two interchanges, and leaves the determinant unchanged. This gives Eq. (3.2.13) and the last sentence.　∎

This theorem connects the determinant, defined algebraically in Chapter 2, with geometry: the determinant of a 3×3 matrix is the signed volume of the parallelepiped spanned by its three columns. A zero determinant means the three columns are coplanar; a negative determinant means the three columns form a left-handed set. In a 1773 paper on tetrahedra, J.-L. Lagrange had already written the dot product, the cross product and this volume formula in terms of components, more than seventy years before the word "vector" appeared.

For robotics, the most important consequence of Theorem 3.2.6 is a test for right-handed frames. Arrange the components in {a} of three mutually perpendicular unit vectors $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_b$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_b$ as a matrix $(\hat x_b\ \hat y_b\ \hat z_b)$; its determinant equals $(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b\times\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_b)\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_b$. By Eq. (3.2.8), this value is +1 when {b} is right-handed; when it is left-handed, $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b\times\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_b = -\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_b$ and the value is −1. For example, suppose a configuration file gives the three axes of a tool frame as x = (0, 1, 0), y = (1, 0, 0), z = (0, 0, 1). They are mutually perpendicular and all of length 1, so nothing seems wrong, but the determinant is {{ex3_2.det_lh:.0f}}: this is a left-handed frame, and one of its axes must have been written the wrong way round. Chapter 4 will show that this is exactly why a rotation matrix must satisfy det R = 1.

**Example 3.2.3** To check whether the top of a fixture is flat, the probe at the robot's end-effector touches four points on it, with readings in the base frame (in mm) $P_1 = (400, -200, 100)$, $P_2 = (700, -180, 103)$, $P_3 = (450, 150, 98)$, $P_4 = (680, 120, 104)$. The first three points determine a plane; find the distance from the fourth point to this plane.

Take $\boldsymbol{a} = \overrightarrow{P_1P_2}$, $\boldsymbol{b} = \overrightarrow{P_1P_3}$, $\boldsymbol{c} = \overrightarrow{P_1P_4}$, with components $a = {{ex3_2.a_vec}}$, $b = {{ex3_2.b_vec}}$, $c = {{ex3_2.c_vec}}$ mm. **a** × **b** is perpendicular to the plane of the first three points; by Eq. (3.2.9),

$$
a\times b = {{ex3_2.axb}}\ \mathrm{mm^2} ,
$$

and its length (twice the area of the triangle) is {{ex3_2.area:.1f}} mm². Eq. (3.2.12) gives the scalar triple product (**a** × **b**)·**c** = {{ex3_2.mixed:.0f}} mm³. The volume divided by the base area is the height:

$$
h = \frac{(\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b})\cdot\boldsymbol{c}}{|\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b}|} = {{ex3_2.h:.2f}}\ \mathrm{mm} .
$$

h is positive, which shows that $P_4$ is on the **a** × **b** side of the plane, that is, {{ex3_2.h:.2f}} mm above it. If the top must be flat to within 1 mm, it needs to be reworked. Incidentally, the z component of the unit normal shows that the plane through the first three points is tilted by {{ex3_2.tilt:.2f}}° from the horizontal.

### 3.2.6 The vector triple product

Taking two cross products in succession, for example **a** × (**b** × **c**), again gives a vector. It has a very useful expansion.

**Theorem 3.2.7 (Vector triple product formula)** For any vectors **a**, **b**, **c**,

$$
\boldsymbol{a}\times(\boldsymbol{b}\times\boldsymbol{c}) = \boldsymbol{b}\,(\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}) - \boldsymbol{c}\,(\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b}) . \tag{3.2.14}
$$

*Proof*　Both sides are vectors defined geometrically and do not depend on the frame. So it is enough to prove that the components of the two sides are equal in one right-handed frame. We pick the frame that makes the computation simplest: the x axis along **b**, and the y axis in the plane of **b** and **c** (if **b** and **c** are parallel, both sides are the zero vector and there is nothing to prove). In this frame,

$$
b = (b_1, 0, 0)^{\mathsf T}, \qquad c = (c_1, c_2, 0)^{\mathsf T}, \qquad a = (a_1, a_2, a_3)^{\mathsf T} .
$$

By Eq. (3.2.9), $b\times c = (0,\ 0,\ b_1c_2)^{\mathsf T}$, and taking another cross product: $a\times(b\times c) = (a_2b_1c_2,\ -a_1b_1c_2,\ 0)^{\mathsf T}$. The right side is

$$
b\,(a_1c_1 + a_2c_2) - c\,(a_1b_1) = (b_1a_1c_1 + b_1a_2c_2 - c_1a_1b_1,\ \ -c_2a_1b_1,\ \ 0)^{\mathsf T} = (a_2b_1c_2,\ -a_1b_1c_2,\ 0)^{\mathsf T} .
$$

The two sides are equal.　∎

This proof shows the basic idea of this chapter: since a geometric relation does not depend on the frame, it may be verified in whichever frame is most convenient. This technique of "choosing a suitable frame" will be used again in Chapter 4 to prove Euler's rotation theorem and to find the angle of a rotation.

Eq. (3.2.14) is popularly called the "BAC − CAB" formula: the right side reads b(a·c) − c(a·b). It shows that **a** × (**b** × **c**) lies in the plane of **b** and **c**, which agrees with geometric intuition: **b** × **c** is perpendicular to this plane, and a cross product with it comes back into the plane. The cross product is not associative, and the brackets cannot be moved. By anticommutativity and Eq. (3.2.14),

$$
(\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b})\times\boldsymbol{c} = -\boldsymbol{c}\times(\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b}) = \boldsymbol{b}\,(\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}) - \boldsymbol{a}\,(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}) , \tag{3.2.15}
$$

which in general is not equal to Eq. (3.2.14).

Written with cross-product matrices, the vector triple product formula gives the square of [a]. Take **b** = **a** in Eq. (3.2.14); for any **c**,

$$
[a]^2 c_a = \boldsymbol{a}\times(\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{c}) = \boldsymbol{a}\,(\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{c}) - \boldsymbol{c}\,|\boldsymbol{a}|^2 ,
$$

and the components of **a**(**a**·**c**) are $a_a(a_a^{\mathsf T}c_a) = (a_a a_a^{\mathsf T})c_a$. Therefore

$$
[a]^2 = a_a a_a^{\mathsf T} - |\boldsymbol{a}|^2 I , \qquad \text{in particular, when}\ |\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}| = 1,\ [\hat u]^2 = \hat u\hat u^{\mathsf T} - I . \tag{3.2.16}
$$

Compare this with the projection formula (3.2.5): $-[\hat u]^2 v_a = v_a - \hat u(\hat u^{\mathsf T} v_a)$ is exactly the component column of $\boldsymbol{v}_\perp$. Thus

$$
\boldsymbol{v}_\perp = -\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\times(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\times\boldsymbol{v}) , \qquad \boldsymbol{v} = (\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\,\hat{\boldsymbol{u}} - \hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\times(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\times\boldsymbol{v}) . \tag{3.2.17}
$$

By Theorem 3.2.3 this has an intuitive explanation (Figure 3.2.3(b)): one cross product with $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ turns $\boldsymbol{v}_\perp$ through 90° in the perpendicular plane; a second one turns it through another 90°, 180° in all, giving $-\boldsymbol{v}_\perp$. Continuing, one more cross product gives $-\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\times\boldsymbol{v}$, that is, $[\hat u]^3 = -[\hat u]$. These few relations are the whole algebraic basis for deriving Rodrigues' formula and the matrix exponential in Chapter 4.

The vector triple product also gives two frequently used identities (Exercises 3.2.3 and 3.2.4): **Lagrange's identity** $|\boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{b}|^2 = |\boldsymbol{a}|^2|\boldsymbol{b}|^2 - (\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{b})^2$, which is $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ obtained by combining Eqs. (3.2.1) and (3.2.7); and the **Jacobi identity** **a** × (**b** × **c**) + **b** × (**c** × **a**) + **c** × (**a** × **b**) = **0**, which shows that three-dimensional vectors together with the cross product form a "Lie algebra"; the screw algebra of Chapter 6 is built on exactly this structure.

### 3.2.7 An everyday example

When you tighten a bolt with a spanner, the farther your hand is from the bolt, the less effort you need; pushing perpendicular to the handle takes the least effort, and pushing or pulling along the handle does not turn the bolt at all. These three pieces of experience are exactly the three factors in the magnitude |**r**||**F**| sin θ of the moment **r** × **F**. When a spanner tightens a bolt, by the right-hand rule, with the fingers curled in the direction of turning, the thumb points in the direction in which the bolt advances: for an ordinary right-hand thread, turning clockwise to tighten moves the bolt away from you, and this is the direction of the moment vector.

When you push a shopping trolley up a ramp, only the component of the push along the ramp moves the trolley, while the component perpendicular to the ramp is taken by the ground; this is the projection meaning of the dot product.

**Engineer's notes**

- When finding an angle by Eq. (3.2.6), rounding errors can make the computed cosine slightly greater than 1, and calling `arccos` directly then gives NaN. The safe way is to clip the cosine to [−1, 1] first, or to find the angle with `atan2(|a×b|, a·b)`; the latter is also very accurate when the angle is close to 0 or 180°.
- `numpy.cross(a, b)` computes by Eq. (3.2.9) and assumes that both are components in a right-handed frame. If the data come from software that uses a left-handed frame, the direction of the cross product will be reversed.
- The part of a force sensor's reading that is "the moment due to the part's weight" changes when the orientation changes; before doing force control, subtract it as in Example 3.2.2. This is called gravity compensation.
- Cross products and scalar triple products are the simplest way to decide whether three points are collinear or four points coplanar, but a threshold comparable to the measurement error must be set; do not require the result to be exactly zero.
