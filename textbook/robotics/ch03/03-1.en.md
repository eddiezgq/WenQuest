### 3.1.1 The problem

On an assembly line, a UR5e arm picks parts off a conveyor. A camera mounted above the conveyor finds each part and tells the arm in which direction the part lies from the gripper and how far away it is. The arm's controller sets up a frame {a} based on the robot base, while the camera sets up a frame {b} based on itself. Because of the way it is mounted, the camera's axes are not parallel to those of the base: seen from above, {b} is turned through 30° relative to {a} about the vertical axis.

At some moment, let the directed line segment from the gripper centre G to the part centre P be called **d**. The controller, measuring in the base frame, gets the three numbers (0.30, 0.20, 0) m, while the camera, measuring in its own frame, gets a different set of three numbers. Both sets are correct, and they describe the same thing. What, then, is this "same thing"? And how are the two sets of numbers related?

The question may look trivial, but it runs through the whole of robotics. A robot usually has a dozen or even dozens of frames: the base, each link, the tool, the camera, the force sensor, the workpiece, the pallet. The same displacement, the same velocity, the same force has a different set of numbers in each frame. If we do not keep "the quantity itself" apart from "its numbers in some frame", programs end up with errors such as adding two sets of numbers directly, and errors of this kind are extremely hard to track down on the shop floor. What this chapter sets out to build is exactly the language that keeps the two apart: a **vector** is a quantity that does not depend on the frame, and its **components** are its reading in a particular frame.

### 3.1.2 Vectors

In everyday experience, some quantities are fully described by a single number, such as the mass of a part, 1.5 kg, or the temperature of a motor, 45 ℃; such quantities are called **scalars**. Other quantities have a direction as well as a size: how far the gripper has moved and in which direction, or how large the force on a part is and which way it points. Such a quantity can be represented by an arrow, whose length stands for the size and whose direction stands for the direction.

**Definition 3.1.1 (Vector)** A quantity that has both a magnitude and a direction, and that adds by the parallelogram rule below, is called a **vector**. A vector is drawn as a directed line segment (an arrow) and written as a bold italic letter, such as **a**, **d**, **v**. The size of a vector **a** (the length of the arrow) is called its magnitude or length and is written |**a**|; a vector of length 1 is called a **unit vector** and is often written with a "hat", $\hat{\boldsymbol{a}}$; the vector of length 0 is called the **zero vector**, written **0**, and it has no definite direction.

The definition speaks only of magnitude and direction; it does not say where the arrow starts. Two arrows with the same length and the same direction represent the same vector, and may be moved parallel to themselves at will. Such vectors are called **free vectors**. Displacements, velocities and the direction and magnitude of forces are examples of free vectors. By contrast, the arrow from a fixed origin O to a point P is called the **position vector** of P, and it depends on the choice of origin: if the origin is changed to O′, the position vector of P becomes $\overrightarrow{O'P} = \overrightarrow{OP} - \overrightarrow{OO'}$. This chapter first deals with free vectors and with frames that share the same origin; how to convert between frames with different origins is left to Chapter 5, which uses homogeneous transformations.

There are two basic operations on vectors, and both can be defined directly by drawing (Figure 3.1.1(a)).

**Definition 3.1.2 (Addition and scalar multiplication of vectors)** (1) Move the start of **b** to the end of **a**; the vector from the start of **a** to the end of **b** is called the sum of **a** and **b**, written **a** + **b**. Equivalently, draw the parallelogram with **a** and **b** as adjacent sides; the diagonal from the common starting point is **a** + **b**. This is called the **parallelogram rule**. (2) The product k**a** of a real number k and a vector **a** is a vector of length |k||**a**|; it points the same way as **a** when k > 0 and the opposite way when k < 0.

A drawing makes it easy to see that addition satisfies the commutative law **a** + **b** = **b** + **a** and the associative law, and that scalar multiplication satisfies the distributive law k(**a** + **b**) = k**a** + k**b**. The reason for the last one is that if the whole triangle is enlarged k times, its three sides are scaled in the same proportion and still form a triangle.

Note that Definitions 3.1.1 and 3.1.2 never mention a frame from beginning to end. Once the arrow is drawn on paper, its length and direction are fixed, and have nothing at all to do with which axes people choose to measure it with. This is what "independent of the frame" in the title of this section means.

That forces combine by the parallelogram rule is one of the earliest laws recognized in statics. The Dutch engineer Simon Stevin already used the triangle relation of forces in his 1586 work on statics; in his *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy* of 1687, Newton listed the parallelogram rule of forces as the first corollary of the laws of motion. The word "vector" came much later: Hamilton introduced it in 1844 in a paper on quaternions, together with the word "scalar". The vector algebra in general use today was separated out from quaternions by J. W. Gibbs and O. Heaviside in the 1880s.

Not every quantity "with a magnitude and a direction" is a vector. When a robot arm turns through an angle about some axis, this rotation can also be represented by an arrow: the arrow lies along the axis, and its length equals the angle. But when two such rotations are carried out one after the other, the result is not the sum of the two arrows by the parallelogram rule, and it depends on the order (Section 4.2 demonstrates this with a book). So finite rotations are not vectors. The clause "adds by the parallelogram rule" in Definition 3.1.1 is the key to deciding whether a quantity is a vector. Section 3.6 will show that the rate of rotation, the angular velocity, is nevertheless a true vector.

### 3.1.3 Frames and components

To handle vectors in a computer, the arrows must be turned into numbers. The way to do this is to choose a frame.

**Definition 3.1.3 (Cartesian frame)** A Cartesian frame {a} consists of an origin $O_a$ and three mutually perpendicular unit vectors $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a$, which point along the positive x, y and z axes respectively. If, when the fingers of the right hand curl from $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a$ towards $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a$, the outstretched thumb points along $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a$, {a} is called a **right-handed frame**.

This book uses only right-handed Cartesian frames, from now on simply called frames. Left-handed and right-handed frames are mirror images of each other; left-handed frames are still found in some graphics software, so take particular care when reading external data (see the engineer's notes of this section).

**Theorem 3.1.1** Once a frame {a} is chosen, every vector **p** can be written uniquely as

$$
\boldsymbol{p} = p_1\,\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a + p_2\,\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a + p_3\,\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a , \tag{3.1.1}
$$

where $p_1, p_2, p_3$ are real numbers.

*Proof*　Existence: place the start of **p** at $O_a$, and drop a perpendicular from its end P to the xy plane, with foot P′. $\overrightarrow{O_aP'}$ lies in the xy plane and $\overrightarrow{P'P}$ is parallel to the z axis, so $\overrightarrow{P'P} = p_3\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a$, where $p_3$ is the signed length of this segment (positive upwards). Then, in the xy plane, split $\overrightarrow{O_aP'}$ into two pieces along the x axis and the y axis, that is, $p_1\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a + p_2\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a$. By the triangle rule, the three pieces add up to **p**.

Uniqueness: suppose another set of numbers $p_1', p_2', p_3'$ satisfies the same equation. Subtracting the two equations gives $c_1\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a + c_2\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a + c_3\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a = \boldsymbol{0}$, where $c_i = p_i - p_i'$. If $c_3 \neq 0$, then $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a = -(c_1\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a + c_2\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a)/c_3$ lies in the xy plane, which contradicts $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a$ being perpendicular to that plane; so $c_3 = 0$. In the same way $c_1 = c_2 = 0$.　∎

**Definition 3.1.4 (Components)** The three numbers $p_1, p_2, p_3$ in Eq. (3.1.1) are called the **components** of **p** in the frame {a}. Arranging them in a column, written

$$
p_a = \begin{pmatrix} p_1 \\ p_2 \\ p_3 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^3 , \tag{3.1.2}
$$

gives the component column of **p** in {a}; the subscript a indicates the frame used.

There are two notations here, and they must be kept apart. The bold italic **p** stands for the vector itself, that is, the arrow; the italic $p_a$ with a subscript stands for a column of three numbers, the reading of this arrow in {a}. In another frame {b}, the arrow **p** is unchanged, but the reading becomes $p_b$. The "vector" of Chapter 2 is a column of n numbers, and $p_a$ is just such a column vector; from here on, this book uses the word "vector" (in bold italic) for the geometric arrow only, to keep the two apart. This is why, throughout the book, every program variable that holds components carries the name of its frame, for example `p_a`, `d_cam`.

With components, operations by drawing become operations on numbers. By Eq. (3.1.1) and the laws of addition and scalar multiplication,

$$
\boldsymbol{p} + \boldsymbol{q} = (p_1 + q_1)\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a + (p_2 + q_2)\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a + (p_3 + q_3)\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a ,
$$

and then, by the uniqueness in Theorem 3.1.1,

$$
(\boldsymbol{p} + \boldsymbol{q})_a = p_a + q_a , \qquad (k\boldsymbol{p})_a = k\,p_a . \tag{3.1.3}
$$

That is, in the same frame, adding vectors means adding components, and multiplying a vector by a number means multiplying its components by that number. The condition "in the same frame" cannot be dropped; Section 3.1.5 will show what happens if it is.

The length of a vector can also be computed from its components. In the proof of Theorem 3.1.1, $O_aP'P$ is a right triangle, and $O_aP'$ is in turn the hypotenuse of a right triangle in the xy plane whose legs are $|p_1|$ and $|p_2|$. Using Pythagoras' theorem twice gives

$$
|\boldsymbol{p}| = \sqrt{p_1^2 + p_2^2 + p_3^2} = \sqrt{p_a^{\mathsf T} p_a} . \tag{3.1.4}
$$

This formula depends on the three axes being mutually perpendicular and on $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a$ and the others having unit length. If the axes are oblique, Pythagoras' theorem no longer applies, and Eq. (3.1.4) no longer holds. This is one of the reasons why this book insists on Cartesian frames.

The geometric meaning of a component is a **projection**. Let the angle between **p** and $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a$ be $\alpha_1$ (0 ≤ $\alpha_1$ ≤ π), and drop a perpendicular from P to the x axis; the signed distance from the foot to the origin is $p_1$ (Figure 3.1.1(b)). By the relations between the sides and angles of a right triangle,

$$
p_1 = |\boldsymbol{p}|\cos\alpha_1 , \qquad p_2 = |\boldsymbol{p}|\cos\alpha_2 , \qquad p_3 = |\boldsymbol{p}|\cos\alpha_3 , \tag{3.1.5}
$$

where $\alpha_2$ and $\alpha_3$ are the angles between **p** and $\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a$. When the angle is greater than 90°, the cosine is negative and so is the component, meaning that the projection falls on the negative half of the axis. Eq. (3.1.5) is the starting point for the dot product of the next section.

### 3.1.4 One arrow, two sets of components

We now return to the problem at the start of this section.

**Example 3.1.1** The displacement **d** from the gripper to the part has components $d_a = (0.30,\ 0.20,\ 0)^{\mathsf T}$ m in the base frame {a}. The camera frame {b} and {a} both have their z axes pointing vertically upwards, and the x axis of {b} is turned anticlockwise through 30° from the x axis of {a} (seen from above). Find the components of **d** in {b}.

**d** lies in the horizontal plane, so its third component is 0 in both frames, and only the two components in the plane need to be dealt with. By Eq. (3.1.4), the length of **d** is

$$
|\boldsymbol{d}| = \sqrt{0.30^2 + 0.20^2} = {{ex3_1.L}}\ \mathrm{m} .
$$

The angle between **d** and $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a$ is $\varphi_a = \arctan(0.20/0.30) = {{ex3_1.phi_a_deg:.2f}}^\circ$. $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b$ is turned 30° further than $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a$, so the angle between **d** and $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b$ is only $\varphi_b = \varphi_a - 30^\circ = {{ex3_1.phi_b_deg:.2f}}^\circ$. Projecting **d** onto the two axes of {b} by Eq. (3.1.5):

$$
d_b = \begin{pmatrix} |\boldsymbol{d}|\cos\varphi_b \\ |\boldsymbol{d}|\sin\varphi_b \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} {{ex3_1.db1}} \\ {{ex3_1.db2}} \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{m} . \tag{3.1.6}
$$

The two sets of components differ a great deal: in {a}, **d** has sizeable components along both x and y; in {b}, it lies almost entirely along x. Yet computing the length from $d_b$ by Eq. (3.1.4) still gives {{ex3_1.L_b}} m, exactly the same as from $d_a$.

This example shows the basic relation between a vector and its components: when the frame changes, the components change, but the arrow itself, and the geometric quantities it determines (such as its length), do not. A quantity computed from components whose value does not change with the frame is called an **invariant**. Length is the first invariant we meet; the next section will show that the angle between two vectors is also an invariant.

In the example, $d_b$ was found from $d_a$ by first finding the length and the angle and then projecting again. This works in the plane but becomes clumsy in space. Section 3.3 will give a direct formula: $d_b$ equals a matrix, determined by the relative orientation of the two frames, times $d_a$. Program 3.1.1 already recomputes the example with this formula, and the result agrees with Eq. (3.1.6).

### 3.1.5 What "independent of the frame" means

Physical laws and geometric relations are, at bottom, statements about arrows, for example "the net force equals the mass times the acceleration", or "the displacement from the gripper to the fixture equals the displacement from the gripper to the part plus the displacement from the part to the fixture". Written in vector form, they do not mention a frame at all; written in component form, they require all the components in the equation to be taken in the same frame. As long as this condition is met, whichever frame we compute in, we get readings of the same arrow.

Conversely, if components from different frames are combined in one calculation, the resulting numbers correspond to no arrow at all.

**Example 3.1.2** Continuing Example 3.1.1, the displacement **e** from the part to the fixture has components $e_a = (-0.10,\ 0.25,\ 0)^{\mathsf T}$ m in {a}. Find the displacement **s** = **d** + **e** from the gripper to the fixture.

The correct way is to add in the same frame: $s_a = d_a + e_a = ({{ex3_1.s1:.2f}},\ {{ex3_1.s2:.2f}},\ 0)^{\mathsf T}$ m, of length {{ex3_1.s_len}} m.

If $d_b$ from the camera is mistakenly added to $e_a$, the result is $({{ex3_1.w1}},\ {{ex3_1.w2}},\ 0)^{\mathsf T}$ m. This set of numbers is neither the components of **s** in {a} nor its components in {b}. Moving by it, the gripper would miss the fixture by about {{ex3_1.err_mm:.0f}} mm. So even when two frames differ by only 30°, the error from mixing components is of the same order as the displacement itself.

There is another easily confused case: a column of numbers is not necessarily the components of some vector. The six joint angles of the UR5e arranged in a column, $(\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_6)^{\mathsf T}$, form a vector in the sense of Chapter 2, but not an arrow in space: if the robot is observed from another frame, not a single joint angle changes; and adding two sets of joint angles does not correspond to combining any two displacements. Likewise, the Euler angles of Chapter 4 are three numbers, but they are not the components of a vector either. So what kind of column of numbers is the components of a vector? The answer is: when the frame changes, it must change by a definite rule, and this rule must be exactly the projection rule shown in Example 3.1.1. Section 3.3 will write this down as another definition of a vector, and from it extend the idea to the tensors of Section 3.4.

### 3.1.6 An everyday example

A phone's navigation app has two display modes: "north-up" and "heading-up". The position of the destination relative to the car is a definite arrow. With north-up, the two axes of the map point east and north, and the app can say "the destination is 300 m due east and 400 m due north"; with heading-up, the axes turn with the car, and the same destination becomes "so many metres ahead and so many metres to the left". The numbers in the two descriptions are completely different, but the destination has not moved, and the straight-line distance computed from either description is 500 m. When the car turns a corner, the map rotates on the screen: this is exactly the frame {b} turning relative to {a} while the arrow stays the same.

Clocks, compasses and floor plans all show the same idea: a direction itself exists objectively, but what we use as a reference to describe it is up to us. When the reference changes, the description changes, but the descriptions are related by a definite conversion.

The next section discusses the two kinds of multiplication between vectors, the dot product and the cross product. They too are first defined geometrically, and so are independent of the frame; then their component formulas are derived, which makes them easy to compute.

**Engineer's notes**

- Every set of stored components should carry the name of its frame. The ROS message type `geometry_msgs/Vector3Stamped` carries a `header.frame_id` alongside the data for exactly this purpose; in your own programs, at least put the frame in the variable name, such as `d_base`, `d_cam`.
- Units, like frames, are "labels" on numbers. In 1999 the US Mars Climate Orbiter was lost, partly because the ground software output impulse in pound-force seconds while the navigation software read it in newton seconds. The most common errors of the same kind on robot shop floors are mixing millimetres with metres and degrees with radians.
- When reading coordinates from a supplier's software, a calibration file or a CAD model, first make sure whether the frame is right-handed or left-handed. Some graphics software and game engines use left-handed frames, or have the y axis up and the z axis out of the screen; copying the data directly will flip the sign of some component.
- To decide whether two component columns in a program may be added, ask just one question: are they readings in the same frame? If not, first use the transformation of Section 3.3 to bring them into the same frame.
