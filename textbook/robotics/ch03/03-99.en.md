The main thread of this chapter can be summed up in one sentence: **a quantity itself does not depend on the frame; its components change with the frame according to definite laws.**

A vector is an arrow, with a magnitude and a direction, and vectors add by the parallelogram rule. Once a right-handed Cartesian frame is chosen, the arrow splits into three pieces along the three axes, giving three components; in another frame the arrow stays the same while the components change. Quantities such as length and the dot product, which are computed from components but do not change with the frame, are called invariants. Mixing components from different frames is one of the most common errors in robot programs, and one of the hardest to track down.

The dot product gives projections and angles; the cross product gives areas, normals and moments. Both are first defined geometrically, and their component formulas are then derived in a Cartesian frame; the component formula of the cross product also requires a right-handed frame. The cross product can be written as a skew-symmetric matrix times a column of components, the scalar triple product of three vectors equals the determinant with them as columns, and the vector triple product expands as BAC − CAB. These few results are all the tools needed in Chapter 4 to derive the rotation matrix and Rodrigues' formula.

The relative orientation of two frames is described by nine direction cosines, arranged into the direction cosine matrix $R_{ab}$, whose columns are the components in {a} of the axes of {b}. The components of a vector transform by $p_a = R_{ab}p_b$; $R_{ab}$ is an orthogonal matrix with determinant +1, and its inverse equals its transpose. Three numbers that transform by this law are the components of a vector; this gives the algebraic definition of a vector.

A linear operator that turns vectors into vectors is a second-order tensor. In each frame it is represented by a 3×3 matrix that transforms by $A_a = R_{ab}A_bR_{ab}^{\mathsf T}$ under a change of frame, leaving the trace, the determinant and the eigenvalues unchanged; a symmetric tensor has three mutually perpendicular principal axes. The projection tensor, the inertia tensor and the stress tensor are all second-order tensors. Index notation and the summation convention write these transformations compactly and mechanically, and correspond to `einsum` in programs.

When a rigid body turns about a fixed axis, the rate of turning and the axis combine into an angular velocity vector **ω**, and the velocity of any point of the body is **v** = **ω** × **r**, with r measured from any point on the axis. Angular velocities can be added as vectors, while finite rotations cannot; this distinction will come up again and again in Chapters 4 and 6.

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| Vector | A quantity with a magnitude and a direction that adds by the parallelogram rule; it does not depend on the frame |
| Component | The reading of a vector in a particular frame; the frame must always be stated |
| Invariant | A quantity computed from components that does not change with the frame, such as a length, a dot product or the trace of a tensor |
| Right-handed frame | $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{y}} = \hat{\boldsymbol{z}}$; the component formula of the cross product holds only in a right-handed frame |
| Direction cosine matrix | Column j is the components in {a} of axis j of {b}; its inverse equals its transpose |
| Second-order tensor | A linear operator that turns vectors into vectors; its component matrix transforms as $RAR^{\mathsf T}$ |
| Principal axis | A symmetric tensor turns a vector along a principal axis into a vector in the same direction |
| Summation convention | An index appearing twice in a term means a sum |
| Angular velocity | A vector along the axis of rotation (right-hand rule) whose length is the rate of turning; angular velocities add as vectors |

### Historical notes

| Year | Person | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| 1586 | Simon Stevin | Used the triangle of forces in his work on statics |
| 1687 | Isaac Newton | Stated the parallelogram of forces as a corollary of the laws of motion |
| 1755 | Johann Andreas Segner | Proved that a rigid body has three mutually perpendicular axes of free rotation (the principal axes) |
| 1765 | Leonhard Euler | *Theoria motus corporum solidorum seu rigidorum*: named the moment of inertia and studied the principal axes of inertia systematically |
| 1771 | Leonhard Euler | Studied tables of coefficients satisfying the orthogonality conditions and expressed them with four parameters (submitted 1770) |
| 1773 | Joseph-Louis Lagrange | Wrote the dot product, the cross product and the volume in components in his study of tetrahedra |
| 1823 | Augustin-Louis Cauchy | The concept of stress: the stress on a section is determined linearly by the normal of the section (submitted 1822) |
| 1844 | William Rowan Hamilton | Introduced the words "vector" and "scalar" in his papers on quaternions |
| 1878 | William Kingdon Clifford | Named the scalar product and the vector product |
| 1881–1884 | Josiah Willard Gibbs | *Elements of Vector Analysis*: modern vector algebra and dyadics |
| 1893 | Oliver Heaviside | Used vector analysis systematically in electromagnetic theory |
| 1898 | Woldemar Voigt | Used the word "tensor" in today's sense in crystal physics |
| 1900 | Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro, Tullio Levi-Civita | "Méthodes de calcul différentiel absolu et leurs applications" (Methods of the absolute differential calculus and their applications): founded tensor analysis |
| 1916 | Albert Einstein | Introduced the summation convention in "The Foundation of the General Theory of Relativity" |
