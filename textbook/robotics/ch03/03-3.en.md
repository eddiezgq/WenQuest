### 3.3.1 The problem

The camera of Section 3.1 was turned only through an angle about the vertical axis, and plane geometry was enough to convert the components. A camera as actually installed is usually tilted as well: to see the parts on the conveyor clearly without getting in the way of the robot arm, the camera is normally mounted on a bracket with its optical axis pointing obliquely downwards. The relation between the camera frame {b} and the base frame {a} is then a general orientation in space, and the method of Section 3.1, finding the length first and then the angle, no longer gets us anywhere.

We need a general rule: given the relative orientation of two frames and the components of some vector in one of them, compute its components in the other directly. This section shows that the rule can be written as the product of a 3×3 matrix and the column of components, and that the nine entries of the matrix are the cosines of the nine angles between the two sets of axes. In turn, the rule gives another definition of a vector, which prepares the way for the generalization to tensors in the next section.

This section deals only with two frames whose origins coincide, or with free vectors, which do not depend on the origin. When the origins differ, a position vector must also have the displacement between the origins added to it; this is discussed in Chapter 5.

### 3.3.2 Direction cosines

First consider a single direction. Let $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ be a unit vector making angles α, β and γ with the three axes of {a}. By Theorem 3.2.2, its components in {a} are its dot products with the three axes, that is, the cosines of the three angles:

$$
\hat u_a = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha \\ \cos\beta \\ \cos\gamma \end{pmatrix} , \qquad \cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1 . \tag{3.3.1}
$$

The second equation is just $|\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}| = 1$. These three cosines are called the **direction cosines** of the direction $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$. A direction needs three direction cosines to describe it, but they are bound by Eq. (3.3.1), so only two of them are independent; this agrees with the fact that "a point on a sphere needs two numbers, longitude and latitude".

Next consider the orientation of one frame relative to another. Each axis of {b} is a direction with three direction cosines, nine in all.

**Definition 3.3.1 (Direction cosine matrix)** Let {a} and {b} be two right-handed Cartesian frames. Write the three axes of {a} as $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{1} = \hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_a$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{2} = \hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_a$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{3} = \hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a$, and the three axes of {b} similarly as $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{b}_{j}$. The 3×3 matrix whose entry in row i and column j is the cosine of the angle $\alpha_{ij}$ between $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{i}$ and $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{b}_{j}$,

$$
R_{ab} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha_{11} & \cos\alpha_{12} & \cos\alpha_{13} \\ \cos\alpha_{21} & \cos\alpha_{22} & \cos\alpha_{23} \\ \cos\alpha_{31} & \cos\alpha_{32} & \cos\alpha_{33} \end{pmatrix} ,
\qquad (R_{ab})_{ij} = \cos\alpha_{ij} = \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{i}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{b}_{j} , \tag{3.3.2}
$$

is called the **direction cosine matrix** of {b} relative to {a}; the subscript ab is read "{b} relative to {a}".

Both the columns and the rows of this matrix have a clear meaning (Figure 3.3.1). Fix j: the three entries of column j are the dot products of $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{b}_{j}$ with the three axes of {a}, which by Theorem 3.2.2 are exactly the components of axis j of {b} in {a}. Fix i: row i consists of the dot products of $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{i}$ with the three axes of {b}, that is, the components of axis i of {a} in {b}. In short:

> The three columns of $R_{ab}$ are the three axes of {b} written in {a}; the three rows are the three axes of {a} written in {b}.

The most direct way to write down a direction cosine matrix is to fill it in column by column following this sentence: see where the x axis of {b} points in {a} and write that as the first column, and so on.

Writing a coordinate transformation with the cosines of the nine angles between two sets of axes was the practice of analytic geometry and rigid-body mechanics in the 18th century. In a paper submitted in 1770, Euler made a special study of 3×3 tables of coefficients satisfying conditions like Eq. (3.3.5), and gave a way of expressing them with four parameters; Section 4.6 will return to this work. In aerospace engineering this matrix is still often called the direction cosine matrix (DCM); in robotics it is usually called the rotation matrix, and Chapter 4 will look at it again from the point of view of rotation.

### 3.3.3 How the components transform

**Theorem 3.3.1 (Transformation rule for the components of a vector)** Let the components of a vector **p** in {a} and {b} be $p_a$ and $p_b$. Then

$$
p_a = R_{ab}\, p_b , \tag{3.3.3}
$$

$$
p_b = R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}\, p_a = R_{ba}\, p_a . \tag{3.3.4}
$$

*Proof*　Expanding in {b}, $\boldsymbol{p} = \sum_{j=1}^{3} (p_b)_j\,\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{b}_{j}$. By Theorem 3.2.2, the i-th component of **p** in {a} is its dot product with $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{i}$:

$$
(p_a)_i = \boldsymbol{p}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{i} = \sum_{j=1}^{3} (p_b)_j\,(\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{b}_{j}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{i}) = \sum_{j=1}^{3} (R_{ab})_{ij}\,(p_b)_j ,
$$

which is row i of Eq. (3.3.3). Exchanging the roles of {a} and {b} gives likewise $p_b = R_{ba}p_a$. And $(R_{ba})_{ij} = \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{b}_{i}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{j} = (R_{ab})_{ji}$, that is, $R_{ba} = R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}$, which gives Eq. (3.3.4).　∎

Eq. (3.3.3) can also be read by columns: $R_{ab}p_b = (p_b)_1\,(\text{column 1}) + (p_b)_2\,(\text{column 2}) + (p_b)_3\,(\text{column 3})$; that is, the three numbers of $p_b$ are used as coefficients to add up the three axes of {b} (expressed by their components in {a}). This is exactly "**p** equals the sum of the three components times the three axes", written in {a}.

**Theorem 3.3.2** The direction cosine matrix between two right-handed Cartesian frames satisfies

$$
R_{ab}^{\mathsf T} R_{ab} = R_{ab} R_{ab}^{\mathsf T} = I , \qquad \det R_{ab} = 1 . \tag{3.3.5}
$$

*Proof*　Substituting Eq. (3.3.4) into Eq. (3.3.3) gives $p_a = R_{ab}R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}p_a$, which holds for every $p_a$, so $R_{ab}R_{ab}^{\mathsf T} = I$; likewise $R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}R_{ab} = I$. It can also be seen directly: the (i, j) entry of $R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}R_{ab}$ is the dot product of column i and column j, that is, $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{b}_{i}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{b}_{j}$, which is 1 when i = j and 0 otherwise. For the determinant: by Theorem 3.2.6, $\det R_{ab}$ equals the scalar triple product of the three columns, $(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b\times\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_b)\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_b$; since {b} is right-handed, by Eq. (3.2.8) this equals $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_b\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_b = 1$.　∎

A square matrix satisfying $Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I$ is called an **orthogonal matrix** (Definition 2.4.1). Theorem 3.3.2 says that the direction cosine matrix between two right-handed Cartesian frames is an orthogonal matrix with determinant +1. Its inverse is its transpose, so finding the inverse transformation in Eq. (3.3.4) needs no equations to be solved: a transpose is enough. If one of the frames is left-handed, the determinant becomes −1; Chapter 4 will denote the set of all 3×3 orthogonal matrices with determinant +1 by SO(3).

The nine direction cosines are bound by the six independent conditions in Eq. (3.3.5) (each of the three columns has unit length, and the three columns are mutually perpendicular), so only three of them are independent. Describing the orientation of a frame in space takes three numbers; this is the starting point for the discussion of Euler angles, the axis-angle representation and quaternions in Chapter 4.

Two conversions can be made one after the other. Suppose there is a third frame {c}. By Eq. (3.3.3), $p_a = R_{ab}p_b = R_{ab}(R_{bc}p_c) = (R_{ab}R_{bc})p_c$; also $p_a = R_{ac}p_c$, and this holds for every $p_c$, so

$$
R_{ac} = R_{ab}\,R_{bc} . \tag{3.3.6}
$$

The way to remember it is that the two adjacent identical subscripts b "cancel". Eqs. (3.3.3) and (3.3.4) follow the same rule: in $R_{ab}p_b$ the two b's cancel, leaving a. Chapter 5 will extend this to homogeneous transformations, which include translation, and call it the subscript cancellation rule.

**Example 3.3.1** In Example 3.1.1, {b} is turned through 30° about the z axis relative to {a}. The x axis of {b} in {a} is (cos 30°, sin 30°, 0)ᵀ, the y axis is (−sin 30°, cos 30°, 0)ᵀ and the z axis is (0, 0, 1)ᵀ; filling these in column by column gives

$$
R_{ab} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos 30^\circ & -\sin 30^\circ & 0 \\ \sin 30^\circ & \cos 30^\circ & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

By Eq. (3.3.4), $d_b = R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}d_a$; the first component is 0.30 cos 30° + 0.20 sin 30° and the second is −0.30 sin 30° + 0.20 cos 30°, exactly as in Eq. (3.1.6). The method of Section 3.1, finding the length and the angle first, has been replaced by a single matrix multiplication.

**Example 3.3.2** A camera looks down on a conveyor (Figure 3.3.2). The z axis of the base frame {a} points vertically upwards. Following the custom in machine vision, the z axis of the camera frame {b} points forwards along the optical axis. As installed, the optical axis of the camera points downwards and leans 20° from the vertical towards the base, and the three axes of {b} in {a} are

$$
\hat x_b = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\hat y_b = \begin{pmatrix} -\cos 20^\circ \\ 0 \\ \sin 20^\circ \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\hat z_b = \begin{pmatrix} -\sin 20^\circ \\ 0 \\ -\cos 20^\circ \end{pmatrix} .
$$

The camera measures the displacement from the camera centre to a part as $d_b = (0.12,\ -0.05,\ 0.60)^{\mathsf T}$ m in {b}. Find its components in {a}.

Filling in column by column gives the direction cosine matrix

$$
R_{ab} = {{ex3_3.R_ab}} .
$$

First make two checks. The columns have unit length and are mutually perpendicular: for example, for the second column $\cos^2 20^\circ + \sin^2 20^\circ = 1$, and the dot product of the second and third columns is $\cos 20^\circ\sin 20^\circ - \sin 20^\circ\cos 20^\circ = 0$. The determinant equals the scalar triple product of the three columns, which works out to 1, so {b} is right-handed. Angles can also be read from the matrix: for example, $(R_{ab})_{33} = -\cos 20^\circ$, so the optical axis $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_b$ makes an angle of {{ex3_3.ang33:.0f}}° with the upward vertical $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_a$, and 20° with the downward vertical; $(R_{ab})_{21} = -1$, so the x axis of the camera points exactly opposite to the y axis of the base.

By Eq. (3.3.3),

$$
d_a = R_{ab}\,d_b = {{ex3_3.d_a}}\ \mathrm{m} .
$$

The z component is negative: the part is {{ex3_3.depth:.4f}} m lower than the camera, as it should be for a camera looking down. Computing back with Eq. (3.3.4), $R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}d_a$ returns $d_b$; the length computed from either set of components is {{ex3_3.L:.4f}} m.

### 3.3.4 Quantities unchanged by the transformation

Section 3.1 said that length does not depend on the frame; Section 3.2 said that the dot product and the cross product are both defined geometrically and also do not depend on the frame. We can now prove these conclusions again, starting from the transformation rule, and obtain them in a more general form.

**Theorem 3.3.3** Let Q be a 3×3 orthogonal matrix and u, v any two columns of components. Then

$$
(Qu)^{\mathsf T}(Qv) = u^{\mathsf T}v , \qquad |Qu| = |u| . \tag{3.3.7}
$$

*Proof*　$(Qu)^{\mathsf T}(Qv) = u^{\mathsf T}Q^{\mathsf T}Qv = u^{\mathsf T}v$. Taking v = u gives the second equation.　∎

Taking $Q = R_{ab}$ and u, v as the components of two vectors in {b}, the theorem says that the dot product computed in {a} is the same as that computed in {b}. This is an algebraic proof that the dot product does not depend on the frame.

Theorem 3.3.3 can also be read another way, which Chapter 4 will use again and again. Regard Q as an operation on columns of components: the frame stays the same, and the vector u is turned into Qu. The theorem says that this operation leaves all lengths and angles unchanged; in other words, it turns any figure into a congruent figure. Chapter 4 will show that what an orthogonal matrix with determinant +1 does is precisely a rotation, so "rotations preserve the dot product".

The case of the cross product is slightly more complicated, because the definition of the cross product uses the right-hand rule.

**Theorem 3.3.4** Let R be a 3×3 orthogonal matrix with determinant +1. Then for any u and v,

$$
R\,(u\times v) = (Ru)\times(Rv) , \tag{3.3.8}
$$

where × is computed by the component formula (3.2.9). If Q is an orthogonal matrix with determinant −1, then $Q(u\times v) = -(Qu)\times(Qv)$.

*Proof*　Take any column of components w. By Theorem 3.2.6, $((Ru)\times(Rv))^{\mathsf T}(Rw) = \det(Ru\ \ Rv\ \ Rw) = \det\big(R\,(u\ \ v\ \ w)\big) = \det R\,\det(u\ \ v\ \ w)$. On the other hand, by Theorem 3.3.3, $(R(u\times v))^{\mathsf T}(Rw) = (u\times v)^{\mathsf T}w = \det(u\ \ v\ \ w)$. When det R = 1 the two are equal. Since w is arbitrary, Rw can be any column of components, so $(Ru)\times(Rv) = R(u\times v)$. When det Q = −1 an extra minus sign appears.　∎

For $R = R_{ab}$, Eq. (3.3.8) means: computing the cross product in {b} by the component formula and then converting it to {a} gives the same result as first converting the two vectors to {a} and then computing the cross product. This guarantees that Eq. (3.2.9) gives the same geometric vector in every right-handed frame. If Eq. (3.2.9) is applied in a left-handed frame, the result is the reverse of the true cross product.

Quantities defined by a cross product or by the right-hand rule, such as a moment or the angular velocity of Section 3.6, pick up this minus sign under a mirror reflection; they are sometimes called **axial vectors**, to distinguish them from ordinary vectors such as displacement and force. Robotics uses only rotations and right-handed frames and never meets mirror images, so this book does not distinguish the two again; but when handling data in a left-handed frame or mirror-symmetric mechanisms (for example a left arm and a right arm), remember this point.

### 3.3.5 A second definition of a vector

Section 3.1 of this chapter said that not every column of three numbers is the set of components of some vector. Theorem 3.3.1 gives the criterion: a true vector has a set of components in every frame, and the components in different frames are converted by Eq. (3.3.3). Conversely, if a quantity is represented in every frame by three numbers, and these are converted by Eq. (3.3.3), the three numbers can be regarded as readings of one and the same arrow. Hence the following definition.

**Definition 3.3.2 (Transformation definition of a vector)** If a quantity is represented in every right-handed Cartesian frame {a} by three numbers $p_a$, and for any two frames {a} and {b}

$$
p_a = R_{ab}\,p_b , \tag{3.3.9}
$$

then the quantity is called a vector, or a first-order tensor. A scalar, which stays unchanged under a change of frame, is called a zeroth-order tensor.

This definition is equivalent to Definition 3.1.1, but it needs no arrows to be drawn: it only looks at how the numbers change with the frame. Its advantage is that it can be generalized: the next section will use the same idea to define a quantity represented in every frame by nine numbers that transform by another rule, namely the second-order tensor.

Let us check the examples of Section 3.1 against this criterion. The six joint angles of the UR5e are the same six numbers in any frame and do not change with the frame, so they are six scalars, not the components of a vector. The three numbers of a set of Euler angles change under a change of frame by a rule entirely different from Eq. (3.3.9), so they are not the components of a vector either.

### 3.3.6 An everyday example

Lay a mobile phone flat on a table, then slowly stand it up: the picture on the screen automatically switches from portrait to landscape or the other way round. The phone relies on its accelerometer: at rest, it senses an "arrow" pointing vertically upwards with a magnitude of about 9.81 m/s² (Chapter 38 explains why it points up), and gives the three components of this arrow in the phone's own frame. The arrow itself always stays vertical; when the phone turns, only the components change. Lying flat there is only the component normal to the screen; standing up, this component becomes zero and the arrow lies in the plane of the screen. The phone judges its own orientation from the relative sizes of the three components, an everyday use of the transformation rule $p_b = R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}p_a$ of this section.

The two faces of a vector are now clear: geometrically it is an arrow; algebraically it is a set of components in every frame that transforms by Eq. (3.3.3). Robotics has another kind of quantity, such as inertia and stress, that turns one vector into another and is represented in every frame by a 3×3 matrix. The next section studies how such quantities change under a change of frame.

**Engineer's notes**

- Write a direction cosine matrix column by column: column j is the components of axis j of the "new" frame {b} in the "old" frame {a}. When it is written, first check three things: each column has unit length, the columns are mutually perpendicular, and the determinant is +1.
- The conventions in software and in the literature are not uniform: some give $R_{ab}$, some give its transpose $R_{ba}$, and some call it "the transformation matrix from {a} to {b}" with exactly the opposite meaning. When you receive data, checking it once with an axis of known direction is more reliable than reading the documentation.
- The conventions for camera frames differ from those for robots: in OpenCV and other vision libraries, the z axis points forwards along the optical axis, the x axis to the right and the y axis downwards (in the image). The matrix obtained by hand-eye calibration is precisely the direction cosine matrix between these two conventions (Chapter 50).
- For the inverse transformation use the transpose, not the matrix inverse. Numerically the transpose is exact, whereas a general-purpose inversion routine introduces rounding errors and is much slower.
