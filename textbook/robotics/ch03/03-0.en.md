title: Chapter Overview
---

A robot carries many frames: the base, each link, the tool, the camera, the force sensor, the workpiece. The same displacement, the same velocity, the same force has a different set of numbers in each frame. If we do not keep "the quantity itself" apart from "its reading in some frame", programs end up with errors such as adding two sets of readings directly, or using a matrix from one frame in another. This chapter builds the language that keeps the two apart: vectors and tensors are quantities that do not depend on the frame, and components are their readings in a particular frame; when the frame changes, the components change by a definite rule, while the quantity itself does not.

The chapter proceeds in this order:

- Section 3.1 defines vectors geometrically, introduces components once a frame is chosen, and uses the example of a camera and a robot arm to show "one arrow, two sets of components";
- Section 3.2 defines the dot product and the cross product, derives their component formulas, and gives the relation between the scalar triple product and the determinant, the vector triple product formula and the skew-symmetric matrix form of the cross product; these are the tools with which Chapter 4 derives rotation matrices and Rodrigues' formula;
- Section 3.3 obtains from direction cosines the transformation rule for components, $p_a = R_{ab}p_b$, and from it gives a second definition of a vector;
- Section 3.4 extends this idea to second-order tensors, obtains the transformation rule $A_a = R_{ab}A_bR_{ab}^{\mathsf T}$, and looks ahead to the inertia tensor and the stress tensor;
- Section 3.5 is an advanced section introducing index notation and the summation convention;
- Section 3.6 studies rotation about a fixed axis and introduces the angular velocity vector and the velocity formula **v** = **ω** × **r**.

This chapter uses the matrix operations, determinants, transposes, traces and eigenvalues of Chapter 2. Its results are the foundation of many later chapters: Chapter 4 uses the cross-product matrix, the vector triple product formula and the angular velocity of this chapter to derive Rodrigues' formula and the matrix exponential; Chapter 5 extends the change of frame, which this chapter treats for rotations only, to homogeneous transformations that include translations; the twists of Chapter 6 start from **v** = **ω** × **r**; Chapter 10 studies the motion of a point in a rotating reference frame; Chapters 23 and 31 discuss the inertia tensor and the stress tensor in detail.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. distinguish a vector from its components, label every set of components in a program with its frame, and judge whether a column of numbers forms the components of a vector;
2. compute dot products, cross products and scalar triple products from components, use them to find angles, projections, moments, areas and volumes, write down the skew-symmetric matrix of a cross product, and use the vector triple product formula;
3. write down the direction cosine matrix from two sets of axes, check its orthogonality and its determinant, and use it to transform the components of a vector;
4. transform the component matrix of a second-order tensor by $RAR^{\mathsf T}$, check the result with invariants such as the trace, the determinant and the eigenvalues, and find the principal axes of a symmetric tensor;
5. read and write index notation and the summation convention, and implement tensor operations with `einsum`;
6. write down the angular velocity vector of a rotation about a fixed axis, and use **v** = **ω** × **r** to compute the velocity and the centripetal acceleration of each point of a rigid body.
