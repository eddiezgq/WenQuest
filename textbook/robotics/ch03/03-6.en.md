### 3.6.1 The problem

An operator drags a UR5e in teach mode. The arm is in the zero position, that is, all joint angles are 0 and the whole arm reaches out horizontally; only the base joint (joint 1) turns, at 60°/s. How large is the velocity of the flange centre at this moment, and in which direction does it point?

This is not only a kinematics question but also a safety question. The industrial robot safety standard ISO 10218-1:2025 (3rd edition, replacing the 2nd edition of 2011; adopted identically in the United States as ANSI/A3 R15.06-2025) specifies that in manual reduced-speed mode the speed of the robot must not exceed 250 mm/s, the same limit as in the 2011 edition. The speed meant here is that of the tool centre point, which, with no tool fitted, is the speed of the flange centre. The controller has to compute the velocities of the points of the end-effector from the joint rates at every moment and judge whether the limit is exceeded. The further the arm reaches out, the larger the end-effector velocity produced by the same joint rate, so the threshold for limiting the joint rates also depends on the configuration of the arm.

This section studies the simplest kind of rigid-body motion: turning about a fixed axis. We shall see that the rate of turning and the direction of the axis combine into one vector, the angular velocity vector **ω**, and that the velocity of any point of the rigid body is given by a concise formula, **v** = **ω** × **r**. This formula is the common starting point for deriving the matrix exponential in Chapter 4, for setting up twists in Chapter 6 and for studying rotating frames of reference in Chapter 10.

### 3.6.2 Rotation in the plane

Look first at the planar case. Let a rigid body turn in the plane about a point O, with the angle of rotation θ taken positive anticlockwise (the same convention as in Chapter 4) and measured in radians. A **rigid body** is a body in which the distance between any two points stays constant; when it turns about O, the distance from each of its points to O does not change, every point moves on a circle centred at O, and in the same interval of time all points turn through the same angle.

Let the distance from a point P to O be ρ. When the body turns through dθ, P travels along the arc a distance ρ dθ (the definition of the radian). Dividing by the time dt taken gives the speed of P,

$$
v = \rho\,\dot\theta , \qquad \dot\theta = \frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t} , \tag{3.6.1}
$$

where $\dot\theta$ is called the angular velocity (rad/s). The velocity is along the tangent of the circle, pointing towards the side to which the body turns. The further a point is from the centre of rotation, the larger its velocity: this is exactly the "the longer the arm, the faster the end-effector" mentioned at the start of this section.

In components it is just as simple. Take a Cartesian frame with origin O, and let P lie at $(x_0, y_0) = (\rho\cos\varphi_0,\ \rho\sin\varphi_0)$ when θ = 0, where $\varphi_0$ is the angle between $\overrightarrow{OP}$ and the x axis. After the body has turned through θ, the distance from P to O is still ρ and the angle has become $\varphi_0 + \theta$, so $x = \rho\cos(\varphi_0 + \theta)$ and $y = \rho\sin(\varphi_0 + \theta)$. Expanding with the angle-sum formulas gives $x = x_0\cos\theta - y_0\sin\theta$ and $y = x_0\sin\theta + y_0\cos\theta$ (Chapter 4 writes this as a rotation matrix, Eq. (4.1.4)). Differentiating with respect to time, bearing in mind that θ changes with time:

$$
\dot x = -\dot\theta\,(x_0\sin\theta + y_0\cos\theta) = -\dot\theta\,y , \qquad \dot y = \dot\theta\,(x_0\cos\theta - y_0\sin\theta) = \dot\theta\,x . \tag{3.6.2}
$$

That is, the velocity is $\dot\theta\,(-y,\ x)$: its length is $\dot\theta\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = \rho\dot\theta$, and its direction is that of the position vector (x, y) turned 90° anticlockwise, in agreement with Eq. (3.6.1).

### 3.6.3 The angular velocity vector

In space a rigid body can turn about an axis in any direction; a single number $\dot\theta$ is no longer enough, and the direction of the axis must be stated as well.

**Definition 3.6.1 (Fixed-axis rotation)** If, while a rigid body moves, a straight line in it stays fixed all the time, the body is said to be in **fixed-axis rotation** about this line, and the line is called the axis of rotation.

When a robot arm turns only one joint, the whole part of the arm beyond that joint is in fixed-axis rotation about the joint axis.

**Definition 3.6.2 (Angular velocity vector)** Let a rigid body turn about an axis at the rate $\dot\theta$, and let $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ be the unit vector along the axis whose direction is related to the sense of turning by the right-hand rule: when the fingers of the right hand curl in the sense of turning, the thumb points along $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$. The vector

$$
\boldsymbol{\omega} = \dot\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} \tag{3.6.3}
$$

is called the **angular velocity** vector of the body; its unit is rad/s.

The angular velocity vector combines the rate of turning (the length $|\dot\theta|$) and the direction of the axis. If $\dot\theta < 0$, **ω** points opposite to $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$, which agrees with the fact that "turning backwards is turning forwards about the opposite axis"; so a given rotation corresponds to only one **ω**. Section 3.1 pointed out that finite rotations are not vectors; that angular velocity is a vector will be shown by Theorem 3.6.2 below.

### 3.6.4 The velocity formula v = ω × r

**Theorem 3.6.1** When a rigid body turns about a fixed axis with angular velocity **ω**, the velocity of any point P of the body is

$$
\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{r} , \tag{3.6.4}
$$

where **r** is the vector from any point O on the axis to P. In any frame {a},

$$
v_a = [\omega_a]\,r_a . \tag{3.6.5}
$$

*Proof*　Take $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ as the direction of the axis and, by the projection formula (3.2.5), split **r** into a part $\boldsymbol{r}_\parallel$ along the axis and a part $\boldsymbol{r}_\perp$ perpendicular to it. P moves on a circle in the plane perpendicular to the axis; the centre of the circle is the foot of the perpendicular from P to the axis, and the radius is ρ = |$\boldsymbol{r}_\perp$|. By Eq. (3.6.1), the speed of P is $\rho|\dot\theta|$, and its direction is along the tangent of the circle, that is, perpendicular to the axis and also to $\boldsymbol{r}_\perp$.

Now look at **ω** × **r**. Its length is $|\dot\theta|\,|\boldsymbol{r}|\sin\alpha$, where α is the angle between **r** and the axis, and $|\boldsymbol{r}|\sin\alpha = |\boldsymbol{r}_\perp| = \rho$, so the length equals $\rho|\dot\theta|$. Its direction is perpendicular to **ω** and **r**, hence perpendicular to the axis and to $\boldsymbol{r}_\perp$, that is, along the tangent of the circle. Finally, by Theorem 3.2.3, $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times\boldsymbol{r}$ is the vector $\boldsymbol{r}_\perp$ turned through 90° about $\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ by the right-hand rule, which points exactly in the direction in which P turns by the right-hand rule. All three agree, so **v** = **ω** × **r**. Eq. (3.6.5) is a direct application of Eq. (3.2.10).　∎

The reference point O in the theorem can be any point on the axis. If it is replaced by another point O′ on the axis, the corresponding vector becomes $\boldsymbol{r}' = \boldsymbol{r} + \lambda\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}$ (λ is the signed distance from O′ to O), and

$$
\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{r}' = \boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{r} + \lambda\,\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}} = \boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{r} , \tag{3.6.6}
$$

because the cross product of parallel vectors is zero (Figure 3.6.1). But the reference point may not be taken off the axis: if O is not on the axis, **ω** × **r** does not give the velocity of P. Chapter 6 will show that when the reference point is not on the axis, another term must be added to the velocity, and this term together with **ω** is exactly the twist.

**Example 3.6.1** Answer the question at the start of this section. In the base frame {s} of the UR5e, the position of the flange centre in the zero position is $p_s = {{ex3_6.p}}$ m (the same as in Example 12.1.2 of Chapter 12). The axis of joint 1 is the vertical line through the point (0, 0, 0.163) m, with direction $\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}_s$. Joint 1 turns at 60°/s, that is, {{ex3_6.w1:.4f}} rad/s. Find the velocity of the flange centre.

The angular velocity is $\omega_s = (0,\ 0,\ {{ex3_6.w1:.4f}})^{\mathsf T}$ rad/s. Take the point (0, 0, 0.163) m on the axis as the reference point, so $r_s = p_s - (0, 0, 0.163)^{\mathsf T}$; by Eq. (3.2.9),

$$
v_s = \omega_s\times r_s = {{ex3_6.w1:.4f}}\times(0.234,\ -0.817,\ 0)^{\mathsf T} = {{ex3_6.v1}}\ \mathrm{m/s} .
$$

The speed is {{ex3_6.v1_len:.4f}} m/s, and the direction is horizontal, perpendicular to the horizontal line from the axis to the flange centre (Figure 3.6.2(a)). If the origin on the axis is taken as the reference point instead, $r_s = p_s$; the z component is different, but the cross product is the same: this is Eq. (3.6.6). Check with Eq. (3.6.1): the distance from the flange centre to the axis of joint 1 is $\rho = \sqrt{0.817^2 + 0.234^2} = {{ex3_6.rho1:.4f}}$ m, and $\rho\dot\theta$ = {{ex3_6.v1_len:.4f}} m/s. Program 3.6.1 also turns the flange centre through a very small angle about the axis and divides the difference in position by the time to obtain a numerical derivative; the result agrees with the formula.

This speed is more than three times the reduced-speed limit of 250 mm/s. To meet the limit, the rate of joint 1 must not exceed 0.25 m/s ÷ {{ex3_6.rho1:.4f}} m = {{ex3_6.w1_max:.4f}} rad/s, that is, {{ex3_6.w1_max_deg:.1f}}°/s. When the arm is drawn in, ρ becomes smaller and the permitted joint rate rises accordingly. This is exactly how the controller of a collaborative robot limits the joint velocities in real time according to the current configuration.

**Example 3.6.2** Still in the zero position, let joint 2 turn at 30°/s instead. The axis of joint 2 is the horizontal line through the point $q_s = (0,\ -0.138,\ 0.163)^{\mathsf T}$ m with direction $-\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_s$ (Table 12.1.1 in Chapter 12). Find the velocity of the flange centre.

The angular velocity is $\omega_s = (0,\ -{{ex3_6.w2:.4f}},\ 0)^{\mathsf T}$ rad/s, and $r_s = p_s - q_s = {{ex3_6.r2}}$ m, so

$$
v_s = \omega_s\times r_s = {{ex3_6.v2}}\ \mathrm{m/s} ,
$$

with magnitude {{ex3_6.v2_len:.4f}} m/s. The velocity has no y component, because it must be perpendicular to the axis, which lies along y; the z component is negative, so the arm swings down (Figure 3.6.2(b)). The distance from the flange centre to the axis of joint 2 is {{ex3_6.rho2:.4f}} m, in agreement with $|\boldsymbol{v}|/\dot\theta$.

### 3.6.5 Composition of angular velocities and centripetal acceleration

The most direct evidence that angular velocity is a vector is that angular velocities combine by the parallelogram rule.

**Theorem 3.6.2** Let a rigid body take part in two rotations at the same time, about two axes through the same point O, with angular velocities $\boldsymbol{\omega}_1$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}_2$. Then the velocity of any point of the body is

$$
\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{\omega}_1\times\boldsymbol{r} + \boldsymbol{\omega}_2\times\boldsymbol{r} = (\boldsymbol{\omega}_1 + \boldsymbol{\omega}_2)\times\boldsymbol{r} , \tag{3.6.7}
$$

that is, the combined motion is a rotation about an axis through O with angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\omega}_1 + \boldsymbol{\omega}_2$.

*Proof*　Velocities add as vectors, and the velocity contributed by each rotation is given by Theorem 3.6.1; the second equality is the distributive law of the cross product (Theorem 3.2.3).　∎

This contrasts with finite rotations in Section 4.2: the composition of two finite rotations depends on their order, whereas two angular velocities can simply be added, in either order. The reason is that angular velocity describes rotation during an infinitesimal time; the difference caused by the order is a second-order small quantity and vanishes on differentiation. Gyroscopes, the wrists of robot arms and the attitude of quadrotors all need the composition of angular velocities: for example, Chapter 16 will show that when several joints turn at once, the angular velocity of the end-effector is the vector sum of the angular velocities of the joints (all expressed in the same frame).

When a rigid body turns at a constant rate, the speed of each point is constant but its direction keeps changing, so there is an acceleration. Differentiate Eq. (3.6.4): **ω** is constant, and the rate of change of **r** is **v**:

$$
\boldsymbol{a} = \boldsymbol{\omega}\times\dot{\boldsymbol{r}} = \boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{r}) = -|\boldsymbol{\omega}|^2\,\boldsymbol{r}_\perp . \tag{3.6.8}
$$

The last step uses Eq. (3.2.17). The acceleration points towards the axis and has magnitude $\rho\dot\theta^2$: this is the **centripetal acceleration**. For Example 3.6.1, the centripetal acceleration of the flange centre is $a_s = {{ex3_6.a1}}$ m/s², with magnitude {{ex3_6.a1_len:.4f}} m/s², pointing horizontally from the flange centre towards the axis of joint 1. When a gripper holds a part, the gripping force must be large enough to supply the centripetal force on the part, or the part will be flung out; Chapter 33 takes this term into account when computing grasp forces. When the angular velocity itself changes, there is a further term $\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\times\boldsymbol{r}$, discussed in Chapter 10.

Finally, a fact that Chapter 4 will need. A unit vector fixed in a rigid body, such as the axis $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b$ of the body frame {b}, turns with the body too. Regarding it as a vector starting from a point on the axis, by Eq. (3.6.4) its rate of change is $\dot{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}}_b = \boldsymbol{\omega}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_b$. Writing this once for each of the three axes and arranging the components in a matrix gives

$$
\dot R_{sb} = [\omega_s]\,R_{sb} , \tag{3.6.9}
$$

where the three columns of $R_{sb}$ are the components in {s} of the three axes of {b}. This differential equation for the rotation matrix leads to the matrix exponential in Section 4.4 of Chapter 4, and becomes the starting point for angular velocity and so(3) in Section 6.2 of Chapter 6.

### 3.6.6 An everyday example

The Earth turns about its axis; its angular velocity vector points from the South Pole to the North Pole and has magnitude $\omega_E = {{ex3_6.wE:sci6}}$ rad/s (relative to the stars, one turn takes about {{ex3_6.T_sid_h:.2f}} h). Treating the Earth as a sphere of radius 6,371 km, a point on the equator moves with the Earth at $\omega_E R$ = {{ex3_6.v_eq:.0f}} m/s; Shanghai lies at about 31.2° north, at a distance R cos 31.2° from the Earth's axis, and moves at about {{ex3_6.v_sh:.0f}} m/s. We move with the ground at this speed without noticing anything, because everything around us moves at the same speed.

The platter of a record player turns at 33⅓ r/min, that is, 200° per second, and the linear speed of the record under the stylus varies with the radius: fast on the outer grooves, slow on the inner ones. This is a direct instance of v = ρθ̇, and also one reason why the outer grooves of a vinyl record often sound better: the same stretch of sound is cut into a longer length of groove there.

**Engineer's notes**

- To convert a joint velocity limit into an end-effector velocity, use the distance ρ from the end-effector to the joint axis, not the distance to the origin of the base. For horizontal axes such as those of joints 2 and 3, ρ varies greatly with the configuration of the arm.
- For an angular velocity given by a controller, simulation software or an IMU, always make sure in which frame it is expressed: the gyroscope of an IMU gives components in the sensor's own frame, whereas kinematic computations often need components in the base frame, and the two differ by a rotation matrix (Chapter 6 distinguishes the spatial angular velocity from the body angular velocity).
- Three units of angular velocity are common: rad/s, °/s and r/min, with 1 r/min = 6°/s = π/30 rad/s. Motor manuals mostly use r/min, controllers mostly rad/s, and teach pendants mostly °/s.
- When a velocity is estimated by dividing a difference in position by the time, too long a sampling interval underestimates the velocity (the chord is shorter than the arc), and too short a one amplifies the measurement noise. The faster the rotation, the shorter the sampling interval needs to be.
