### 3.4.1 The problem

A robot is polishing the tilted surface of a workpiece. The control program must make the tool move along the surface while staying in contact with it, neither leaving it nor digging into it. Suppose the operator gives a desired velocity $\boldsymbol{v}_{\rm cmd}$ through the teach pendant; what the program has to do is keep only its part along the surface and remove its part along the normal. This step turns one vector into another and does not depend on the frame: whatever frame it is described in, "removing the part along the normal" is one and the same thing.

In the surface's own frame {b} (x and y axes in the surface, z axis along the normal) the operation is very simple: keep the first two components and set the third to zero; written as a matrix, this is diag(1, 1, 0). The trouble is that the velocity command and the motion of the robot are computed in the base frame {a}. What is the matrix of this operation in {a}? Can diag(1, 1, 0) be used there directly?

Another example comes from rotation. A gripper holds a plate-shaped part and spins it about the tool axis. If the plate is tilted relative to the tool axis, its angular momentum does not point along the axis of rotation, and as a result the bearings and the wrist joints are subjected to a periodic moment that causes vibration. Finding the angular momentum from the angular velocity is also an operation that turns a vector into a vector; it too does not depend on the frame, yet it has different matrices in different frames.

Both kinds of operation are second-order tensors, the subject of this section. We shall see that their matrices in two frames satisfy a fixed relation, $A_a = R_{ab}A_bR_{ab}^{\mathsf T}$; this is what "transforming as R T Rᵀ" in the section title means.

### 3.4.2 Linear operators

**Definition 3.4.1 (Second-order tensor)** A correspondence **A** that turns each vector **v** into another vector **A** **v** and satisfies the linearity condition

$$
\boldsymbol{A}(\alpha\boldsymbol{u} + \beta\boldsymbol{v}) = \alpha\,\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{u} + \beta\,\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v}
\quad (\alpha, \beta\ \text{any real numbers}) \tag{3.4.1}
$$

is called a **linear operator**; in mechanics and engineering it is called a **second-order tensor**, or simply a tensor. Tensors are written as bold italic capital letters.

Like the definition of a vector in Section 3.1, this definition does not mention a frame. A few examples help to make it concrete:

- the identity tensor **I**: turns every vector into itself;
- projection onto the unit vector $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$: $\boldsymbol{v} \mapsto (\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$, by Eq. (3.2.5);
- the cross product with a fixed vector **a**: $\boldsymbol{v} \mapsto \boldsymbol{a}\times\boldsymbol{v}$, which is linear by the distributive law of the cross product;
- the rotations of Chapter 4: turning every vector through the same angle about some axis.

The linearity of each of them can be seen directly from the geometry. What linearity means is this: once we know what a tensor does to the three coordinate axes, we know what it does to every vector, because every vector is a linear combination of the three axes.

**Definition 3.4.2 (Components of a tensor)** Let the three axes of {a} be $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{1}, \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{2}, \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{3}$. The 3×3 matrix $A_a$ whose entry in row i and column j is

$$
(A_a)_{ij} = \hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{i}\cdot\big(\boldsymbol{A}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{j}\big) \tag{3.4.2}
$$

is called the component matrix of the tensor **A** in {a}. By Theorem 3.2.2, column j is the components in {a} of $\boldsymbol{A}\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{j}$ (the vector into which **A** turns axis j).

**Theorem 3.4.1** For any vector **v**,

$$
(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v})_a = A_a\,v_a . \tag{3.4.3}
$$

*Proof*　$\boldsymbol{v} = \sum_j (v_a)_j\,\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{j}$, and by linearity $\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{v} = \sum_j (v_a)_j\,\boldsymbol{A}\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{j}$. Taking the i-th component (the dot product with $\hat{\boldsymbol{e}}^{a}_{i}$) gives $\sum_j (A_a)_{ij}(v_a)_j$.　∎

This is the same idea as "the columns of a rotation matrix are the rotated coordinate axes" in Chapter 4: the columns of a component matrix are the images of the coordinate axes under the tensor.

The component matrices of the examples: the identity tensor is the identity matrix I in every frame; the component matrix of the cross product with **a** is the [a] of Eq. (3.2.10); the component matrix of the projection onto $\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ is $\hat u_a\hat u_a^{\mathsf T}$, because the components of $(\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}$ are $\hat u_a(\hat u_a^{\mathsf T}v_a) = (\hat u_a\hat u_a^{\mathsf T})v_a$.

The last example can be generalized. **Definition 3.4.3 (Dyad)** The **dyad** **u** ⊗ **w** of two vectors **u** and **w** is the tensor $\boldsymbol{v} \mapsto \boldsymbol{u}\,(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})$; its component matrix in {a} is $u_a w_a^{\mathsf T}$.

The dyad is the simplest tensor: it turns every vector into a multiple of **u**. In 1884, in the second part of his *Elements of Vector Analysis*, Gibbs made systematic use of dyads, then called dyadics; this was the beginning of second-order tensors in engineering calculation. The $a_aa_a^{\mathsf T}$ in Eq. (3.2.16) is the component matrix of **a** ⊗ **a**.

### 3.4.3 The transformation rule under a change of frame

The same tensor has two component matrices, $A_a$ and $A_b$, in {a} and {b}. The relation between them can be derived, as in Section 3.3, from "the tensor itself does not change".

**Theorem 3.4.2 (Transformation rule for the components of a second-order tensor)** Let $R_{ab}$ be the direction cosine matrix of {b} relative to {a}. Then

$$
A_a = R_{ab}\,A_b\,R_{ab}^{\mathsf T} , \qquad A_b = R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}\,A_a\,R_{ab} . \tag{3.4.4}
$$

*Proof*　Take any vector **v** and write **w** = **A** **v**. By Theorem 3.4.1, $w_b = A_bv_b$. By Theorem 3.3.1, $w_a = R_{ab}w_b$ and $v_b = R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}v_a$, so

$$
w_a = R_{ab}A_b R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}\,v_a .
$$

On the other hand $w_a = A_av_a$. Both hold for every $v_a$, so $A_a = R_{ab}A_bR_{ab}^{\mathsf T}$. Multiplying both sides by $R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}$ on the left and by $R_{ab}$ on the right, and using $R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}R_{ab} = I$, gives the second equation.　∎

In discussing the change of basis, Chapter 2 already obtained a relation of the same form from the matrix point of view: the similarity transformation (Eqs. (2.2.6) and (2.2.7)). The derivation here starts from "the tensor itself does not depend on the frame"; since $R_{ab}$ is an orthogonal matrix, the inverse matrix in the similarity transformation is just the transpose. Eq. (3.4.4) also reads naturally: $A_b R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}$ first converts components in {a} to {b} and applies $A_b$ in {b}, and $R_{ab}$ then converts back to {a}. In terms of entries, writing $r_{ij} = (R_{ab})_{ij}$,

$$
(A_a)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{3}\sum_{l=1}^{3} r_{ik}\,r_{jl}\,(A_b)_{kl} . \tag{3.4.5}
$$

Compare this with the transformation of a vector, $(p_a)_i = \sum_k r_{ik}(p_b)_k$: the components of a vector have one index, and under a change of frame they are multiplied by one direction cosine; the components of a tensor have two indices, and each index is multiplied by a direction cosine of its own. This suggests another definition of a tensor.

**Definition 3.4.4 (Transformation definition of a tensor)** If a quantity is represented in every right-handed Cartesian frame by nine numbers (a 3×3 matrix), and the matrices in any two frames transform by Eq. (3.4.4), it is called a second-order tensor.

On this view, a scalar (unchanged) is a zeroth-order tensor, a vector (one index, multiplied by one direction cosine) is a first-order tensor, and the tensors of this section (two indices, multiplied by two direction cosines) are second-order tensors. In the same way, a quantity with n indices, each multiplied by a direction cosine under a change of frame, is called an n-th-order tensor; the Levi-Civita symbol of Section 3.5 is a third-order example.

The history of the word "tensor" is somewhat tortuous. In 1846 Hamilton used it for the modulus of a quaternion, which has nothing to do with today's meaning. The present usage comes from the German physicist Woldemar Voigt, who in 1898, studying the elasticity of crystals, used the word for quantities such as stress and strain that transform by Eq. (3.4.5). In 1900 Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro and Tullio Levi-Civita published *Methods of the absolute differential calculus and their applications*, which set up tensor analysis in general coordinate systems; it later became the mathematical tool of Einstein's general theory of relativity.

Eq. (3.4.4) can also be read another way, corresponding to the two readings of an orthogonal matrix in Section 3.3.4. If the frame stays still and the body is actually turned through R (what Chapter 4 calls an active rotation), then the components in the same frame of a tensor carried by the body (its inertia, for example) become $RA R^{\mathsf T}$. This is because the body after the turn, seen in "the frame that turned along with it", is exactly the same as before. A rotation is itself a linear operator, and its matrix also changes by Eq. (3.4.4) under a change of frame; Section 4.3 uses precisely this to find the angle of an arbitrary rotation by moving to a frame whose z axis lies along the axis of rotation.

### 3.4.4 Quantities that do not change with the frame

**Theorem 3.4.3 (Invariants of a tensor)** The component matrices $A_a$ and $A_b$ of a second-order tensor in any two frames have the same trace, determinant and eigenvalues. If $A_b$ is a symmetric matrix, so is $A_a$; if $A_b$ is a skew-symmetric matrix, so is $A_a$.

*Proof*　By Eq. (3.4.4), $A_a = R_{ab}A_bR_{ab}^{-1}$; the two are similar matrices, and by Theorem 2.2.4 they have the same trace, determinant and eigenvalues. This can also be checked directly: $\mathrm{tr}(R A_b R^{\mathsf T}) = \mathrm{tr}(A_b R^{\mathsf T}R) = \mathrm{tr}\,A_b$ and $\det(RA_bR^{\mathsf T}) = \det R\,\det A_b\,\det R^{\mathsf T} = \det A_b$. Symmetry: $(RA_bR^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T} = RA_b^{\mathsf T}R^{\mathsf T}$, so when $A_b^{\mathsf T} = \pm A_b$, $A_a^{\mathsf T} = \pm A_a$.　∎

So "symmetric tensor" and "skew-symmetric tensor" are properties of the tensor itself, independent of the chosen frame; the trace, determinant and eigenvalues of a tensor are likewise attributes of the tensor itself. They extend the idea of an "invariant" from Section 3.1 to tensors.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between skew-symmetric tensors and vectors. By Theorem 3.2.5, every 3×3 skew-symmetric matrix is some [a]. The next theorem shows that this correspondence does not depend on the frame: when a vector changes frame, its cross-product matrix changes frame by exactly the rule for tensors.

**Theorem 3.4.4** Let R be a 3×3 orthogonal matrix with determinant +1. Then for any $a \in \mathbb{R}^3$,

$$
R\,[a]\,R^{\mathsf T} = [Ra] . \tag{3.4.6}
$$

*Proof*　For any v, by Theorem 3.3.4, $R[a]R^{\mathsf T}v = R\,(a\times R^{\mathsf T}v) = (Ra)\times(RR^{\mathsf T}v) = (Ra)\times v = [Ra]\,v$.　∎

Taking $R = R_{ab}$, Eq. (3.4.6) says that in {a} the component matrix $R_{ab}[a_b]R_{ab}^{\mathsf T}$ of the tensor "cross product with **a**" is exactly $[a_a]$, built from the components of **a** in {a}. This seemingly trivial identity is used very often later: Chapter 6 needs it to derive the adjoint transformation, Chapter 12 to verify Eq. (12.2.7), and Chapter 16 to move the Jacobian matrix from one frame to another.

Symmetric tensors have an important property. The spectral theorem of Chapter 2 (Theorem 2.3.4) tells us that a real symmetric matrix can be diagonalized by an orthogonal matrix, that is, it has three mutually perpendicular unit eigenvectors. In the language of tensors:

**Theorem 3.4.5 (Principal axes)** For any symmetric tensor **A** there is a frame {p} in which the component matrix is diagonal:

$$
A_p = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \lambda_3 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{3.4.7}
$$

Each of the three axes of {p} is called a **principal axis** of the tensor, and $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3$ are called its **principal values**.

Along a principal axis the tensor turns a vector into a vector in the same direction (stretched by the factor $\lambda_i$); in other directions **A** **v** is in general not parallel to **v**. In the plane this geometric picture is especially intuitive: apply the tensor once to every vector on the unit circle, and the unit circle becomes an ellipse whose two axes of symmetry are the principal axes and whose semi-axes are the principal values (Figure 3.4.1). The ellipse itself is a geometric image of the tensor and does not depend on the frame; in another frame, only the component matrix describing this ellipse changes.

### 3.4.5 Example: projecting a velocity onto a tilted surface

Back to the polishing problem at the start of this section. "Keeping only the part along the surface" is a tensor, written **P** and called the **projection tensor**. By Eq. (3.2.5), $\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{v} - (\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$, that is,

$$
\boldsymbol{P} = \boldsymbol{I} - \hat{\boldsymbol{n}}\otimes\hat{\boldsymbol{n}} , \qquad P_a = I - \hat n_a\hat n_a^{\mathsf T} , \tag{3.4.8}
$$

where $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ is the unit normal of the surface. Eq. (3.4.8) holds in every frame; one only has to substitute the components of the normal in that frame.

**Example 3.4.1** The surface of a workpiece is tilted 20° from the horizontal: the surface frame {b} is turned through 20° about the x axis relative to the base frame {a}, and the z axis of {b} lies along the normal of the surface. The desired velocity given by teaching is $v_{{\rm cmd},a} = (0.05,\ 0.10,\ 0)^{\mathsf T}$ m/s in {a}. Find the velocity after projection.

In {b}, $P_b = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, 0)$. The direction cosine matrix $R_{ab}$ is a turn of 20° about the x axis:

$$
R_{ab} = {{ex3_4.R_ab}} .
$$

By Eq. (3.4.4),

$$
P_a = R_{ab}\,P_b\,R_{ab}^{\mathsf T} = {{ex3_4.P_a}} .
$$

Alternatively, the normal in {a} is the third column of $R_{ab}$, $\hat n_a = {{ex3_4.n_a}}$, and substituting it into Eq. (3.4.8) gives the same matrix. The velocity after projection is

$$
v_a = P_a\,v_{{\rm cmd},a} = {{ex3_4.v}}\ \mathrm{m/s} ;
$$

its dot product with $\hat n_a$ is zero, so it does lie along the surface. The speed changes from {{ex3_4.cmd_speed:.4f}} m/s to {{ex3_4.v_speed:.4f}} m/s, and the x component is unchanged, because the x axis lies in the surface anyway.

If $P_b = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, 0)$ is used directly in {a}, the command velocity passes through unchanged (its third component is 0 to begin with), and the tool moves horizontally while the surface rises at a slope of 20°. The component of the velocity along the normal is then $-0.10\sin 20^\circ$, that is, the tool digs into the workpiece at {{ex3_4.dig_mm:.1f}} mm/s (Figure 3.4.2). This is the consequence of using a component matrix from one frame in another frame, the same mistake as mixing up vector components in Section 3.1.5.

::: Figure 3.4.2
src: fig3_4_2
caption: Example 3.4.1 (side view): the command velocity v_cmd lies along the surface once the projection tensor has removed its part along the normal; if diag(1, 1, 0) is wrongly used in the base frame, the tool digs into the surface
:::

The traces of the two component matrices are both 2, their determinants both 0, and their eigenvalues both 1, 1, 0, in agreement with Theorem 3.4.3. The meaning of the principal values 1, 1, 0 is clear: directions in the surface are kept unchanged, and the normal direction is squashed to zero. One can also check that if the surface frame is turned by any angle about the normal, $P_b$ is still diag(1, 1, 0) and $P_a$ does not change either. The projection tensor depends only on the direction of the normal, not on how the x and y axes in the surface are chosen.

In the hybrid force/position control of Chapter 45, "which directions control position and which control force" is described by exactly such a projection tensor, called there the selection matrix. When machining a curved surface, the normal changes with position, and the components of the selection matrix in the base frame must be updated continually by Eq. (3.4.4).

### 3.4.6 A first look at the inertia tensor and the stress tensor

The two most important second-order tensors in robotics are the inertia tensor and the stress tensor. They are treated fully in Chapter 23 and Chapter 31; here we only explain why they are tensors and how they change frame.

**Inertia tensor.** When a rigid body turns about a point with angular velocity **ω**, its angular momentum **L** about that point is determined linearly by **ω**:

$$
\boldsymbol{L} = \boldsymbol{I}\,\boldsymbol{\omega} , \qquad I_a = \sum_k m_k\big(|r_k|^2 I - r_k r_k^{\mathsf T}\big) = -\sum_k m_k\,[r_k]^2 , \tag{3.4.9}
$$

where the rigid body is regarded as many particles $m_k$, $r_k$ is the components in {a} of the position of a particle relative to the centre of rotation, and the second equality uses Eq. (3.2.16). The bold italic **I** is the **inertia tensor** and $I_a$ is its component matrix in {a}, in kg·m²; Chapter 23 will derive Eq. (3.4.9) from the $\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{r}$ of Section 3.6. The inertia tensor is a symmetric tensor, so by Theorem 3.4.5 it has three mutually perpendicular principal axes, and the corresponding principal values are called the principal moments of inertia. That a rigid body has three mutually perpendicular "free axes of rotation" was first proved by Johann Andreas Segner in 1755, in a work on spinning tops; in his *Theory of the Motion of Solid or Rigid Bodies* of 1765, Euler gave this quantity the name "moment of inertia" (momentum inertiae) and made a systematic study of the principal axes of inertia and of rotation about them.

**Example 3.4.2** A gripper holds a rectangular plate of length a = 0.30 m, width b = 0.20 m, thickness c = 0.02 m and mass 2.0 kg. In the plate's own frame {b} (origin at the centre of the plate, the three axes along its length, width and thickness), the inertia tensor is diagonal (Chapter 23 gives the formula):

$$
I_b = \frac{m}{12}\begin{pmatrix} b^2 + c^2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & a^2 + c^2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & a^2 + b^2 \end{pmatrix} = {{ex3_4.I_b}}\times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2} .
$$

The plate has not been gripped squarely: it is tilted 30° about the x axis relative to the tool frame {t}, with the origin still at the centre of the plate. By Eq. (3.4.4),

$$
I_t = R_{tb}\,I_b\,R_{tb}^{\mathsf T} = {{ex3_4.I_t}}\times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m^2} .
$$

An off-diagonal entry, {{ex3_4.Iyz:.3f}} × 10⁻³ kg·m², has appeared. Program 3.4.1 also divides the plate into tens of thousands of small pieces and sums directly in {t} by Eq. (3.4.9); the result agrees with this, the largest relative difference being ${{ex3_4.I_err:sci1}}$, which comes from the discretization error of the division. If the tool spins about its own z axis at one revolution per second (ω = 2π rad/s), the angular momentum is $L_t = I_t\,\omega_t = {{ex3_4.L}}\times 10^{-3}$ kg·m²/s, and it makes an angle of {{ex3_4.ang_Lw:.1f}}° with the axis of rotation (Figure 3.4.3(a)). The angular momentum rotates about the z axis together with the plate, so its direction keeps changing, and the wrist must keep supplying a moment to change it; this is the vibration caused by "dynamic imbalance". Only when the rotation is about a principal axis is **L** parallel to **ω**.

**Stress tensor.** Imagine cutting a loaded link along some section; the material on the two sides of the cut acts on each other through it. The force per unit area is called the stress vector **t**, and both its magnitude and its direction depend on the orientation of the cut, that is, on the unit normal $\hat{\boldsymbol{n}}$ of the cut. In work presented to the French Academy of Sciences in 1822 and published in 1823, Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved that this dependence is linear:

$$
\boldsymbol{t} = \boldsymbol{\sigma}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{n}} , \tag{3.4.10}
$$

**σ** is the **stress tensor**, and it too is a symmetric tensor. In its component matrix in a given frame, the diagonal entries are normal stresses (tension or compression) and the off-diagonal entries are shear stresses. Under a change of frame it transforms by Eq. (3.4.4); in the frame of its principal axes the shear stresses are zero, and the principal values are called the principal stresses. When Chapter 31 checks the strength of a link, what it compares with the allowable stress of the material is precisely the principal stresses; the strain gauges of Chapters 38 and 39 measure the component in a given direction of the strain tensor that corresponds to the stress.

### 3.4.7 An everyday example

Put a ski on the snow and give it a push. Pushed along its length, the ski slides away easily; pushed sideways, it hardly moves; pushed at a slant, it slides in a direction turned towards its length, not in the direction of the push. The relation between the push and the velocity is a symmetric tensor whose two principal axes lie along the length and the width of the ski, one principal value large and the other small. This is the same phenomenon as "only when the rotation is about a principal axis is the angular momentum parallel to the angular velocity" in Section 3.4.6: a tensor turns one vector into another, and keeps the direction unchanged only along a principal axis.

We have now described a tensor by its component matrix and its transformation by $R A R^{\mathsf T}$. The double sum in Eq. (3.4.5) already looks somewhat cumbersome, and the transformations of third- and fourth-order tensors are even more so. The index notation and summation convention introduced in the next section were invented precisely for writing such expressions.

**Engineer's notes**

- When you are handed a 3×3 matrix, first ask whether it is the component matrix of a tensor or a rotation matrix (the direction cosine matrix between two frames). The former changes by $RAR^{\mathsf T}$ under a change of frame; the latter is itself a relation between two frames, and how it changes depends on which two frames it connects.
- An inertia tensor given by CAD software or a parts library must state about which point and in which frame it was computed. In a URDF file, the inertia in `<inertial>` is given in the frame specified by `<origin>`, with the origin at the centre of mass; to move it to the link frame, use Eq. (3.4.4) for the rotational part, and the parallel-axis theorem of Chapter 23 for the translational part as well.
- To judge whether a component matrix is "reasonable", check its invariants: the trace of an inertia tensor must be positive, its principal values must not be negative (zero occurs only in idealized limiting cases such as a thin rod), and the sum of any two principal values is no less than the third; the trace of a projection tensor equals the number of dimensions kept.
- Stress results from finite element software usually have six components (a symmetric tensor has only six independent entries); when comparing results from different programs, make sure their frames and their definitions of shear stress agree.
