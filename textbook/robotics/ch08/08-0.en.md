title: Chapter Overview

Many of the things a robot has to do come down, in the end, to picking the best one out of many possible choices: to what angles the joints should turn so that the end-effector reaches the target; which of infinitely many configurations a redundant arm should take; how the next step should go when the joints are limited in position and in speed; how an AGV should get round the shelves; how to compute a robot's true link lengths from measured data. Write "best" as a function and the limitations as equations or inequalities, and these problems become optimization problems. This chapter presents the basic theory and methods for solving optimization problems. They are the common foundation of the inverse kinematics (Chapter 15), redundancy control (Chapter 17), trajectory planning (Chapter 22), parameter identification (Chapter 26), model predictive control (Chapter 48) and motion planning (Chapter 53) that come later.

The chapter proceeds from the simple to the complex:

- Section 8.1 discusses unconstrained optimization. Starting from the intuition of "walking downhill in fog", it introduces the gradient and the Hessian matrix, gives the first- and second-order conditions for a minimizer, and analyses the speed of convergence of gradient descent and of Newton's method;
- Section 8.2 adds equality constraints, derives the Lagrange multipliers, and shows that the multipliers are the constraint forces and the sensitivities of the optimal value;
- Section 8.3 adds inequality constraints, presents the KKT conditions and the active-set method with quadratic programming as the object of study, handles joint limits by sequential quadratic programming, and finds a minimum-energy trajectory by quadratic programming;
- Section 8.4 introduces convex optimization: convex sets, convex functions, local optima being global optima, separating hyperplanes, duality and the barrier method, and writes obstacle avoidance as a convex problem;
- Section 8.5 discusses nonlinear least squares and introduces the Gauss–Newton method and the Levenberg–Marquardt method, applied to the inverse kinematics of the UR5e, to kinematic calibration and to indoor positioning.

The chapter uses the matrix operations, eigenvalues, singular value decomposition, least squares and null spaces of Chapter 2, and Newton's method for nonlinear equations of Chapter 7. From calculus it uses only derivatives and Taylor's formula for functions of one variable; partial derivatives, gradients and Hessian matrices of functions of several variables are developed from the beginning in Section 8.1. The examples running through the whole chapter are the planar 2R and 3R arms seen from the side of the UR5e (with the same dimensions as in Chapter 12) and the UR5e itself. Every number in the book is computed by a program and checked independently with `scipy.optimize`.

A note on notation: in Chapter 2, λ denotes an eigenvalue; in Sections 8.2 to 8.4 of this chapter it denotes the Lagrange multiplier of an equality constraint. Eigenvalues are still written λ where no confusion can arise, and as $\lambda_{\min}$, $\lambda_{\max}$ or $\lambda_i$ where necessary. The multiplier of an inequality constraint is written μ. The damping parameter of the Levenberg–Marquardt method in Section 8.5 is also written μ (following Kaj Madsen, Hans Bruun Nielsen and Ole Tingleff), because Section 8.5 will prove that it is exactly the multiplier of an inequality constraint (the trust-region constraint); the $\lambda^2$ in the damped least-squares formula (7.3.12) of Chapter 7 is the μ here.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. compute the gradient and the Hessian matrix of a function of several variables, and use the first- and second-order conditions to decide whether a stationary point is a minimizer, a maximizer or a saddle point;
2. solve unconstrained problems by gradient descent (with line search) and by Newton's method, and explain the speed of convergence of gradient descent by the condition number;
3. solve equality-constrained problems by the method of Lagrange multipliers, and explain the mechanical meaning of the multipliers and their meaning as sensitivities;
4. write down the KKT conditions of a problem with inequality constraints, solve quadratic programs by the active-set method, and build joint limits and speed limits into an optimization problem;
5. decide whether a problem is convex, explain why a local optimum of a convex problem is a global optimum, solve it by the barrier method and judge the accuracy by the duality gap;
6. solve inverse kinematics and calibration problems by the Gauss–Newton method and the LM method, and explain why the LM method is more reliable near singular configurations.
