### 8.4.1 The problem: is a local optimum good enough?

The inverse kinematics objective function of Section 8.1 has two minimizers, three saddle points and one maximizer, and iterations started from different initial values end up in different places. For inverse kinematics this is not yet serious: both minimizers are inverse solutions, and which one to choose depends on what is needed. In other problems, a local optimum can be very bad.

Imagine an AGV in a digital factory workshop that has to drive from the lower-left corner of the workshop, round a row of shelves, to a workstation at the upper right. If the waypoints of the path are taken as decision variables, "a short, smooth path" as the objective function and "not hitting the shelves" as the constraints, and the problem is solved by the methods of Sections 8.1 to 8.3, the result may depend on the initial value: iterations started on one side of the shelves stop at some path on that side; started on the other side, they stop on the other side. Worse, when the algorithm stops, we have no way of knowing whether a better path exists.

There is a class of problems free of this trouble: every local minimizer is a global minimizer, and there are reliable, efficient algorithms that compute the optimal value to a given accuracy and supply a proof of that accuracy. These are convex optimization problems. Their theory matured gradually from the 1950s on, and in 1994 Yurii Nesterov and Arkadi Nemirovskii proved that a large class of convex problems can be solved in polynomial time by interior-point methods. Today convex optimization is a basic tool in robot motion planning, control and estimation. Stephen Boyd and Lieven Vandenberghe say in their textbook that the difficulty and the art of applying convex optimization lie not in solving the problem but in recognizing it and writing it in convex form. This section first gives the definitions and basic properties of convex sets and convex functions and proves that for convex problems a local optimum is a global optimum; it then introduces duality and the barrier method, and finally uses two examples to show how obstacle avoidance can be written as a convex problem.

### 8.4.2 Convex sets and convex functions

**Definition 8.4.1 (Convex set)** A set $C \subseteq \mathbb R^n$ is called a **convex set** if for any $x, y \in C$ and any $s \in [0, 1]$,

$$
s\,x + (1 - s)\,y \in C . \tag{8.4.1}
$$

As s goes from 0 to 1, $sx + (1-s)y$ goes from y to x, tracing the segment joining the two points. So a convex set is a set in which "the segment between any two points lies in the set" (Figure 8.4.1 (a)). Solid discs, ellipses, squares and triangles are convex sets; annuli, L shapes and crescents are not. Figure 8.4.1 (b) shows the free space in a workshop that goes round the shelves; the line joining the start and the goal passes through the shelves, so it is not a convex set.

:::

The most common convex sets in robotics are given by linear inequalities. For a non-zero vector a and a number b, the set $\{x: a^{\mathsf T}x = b\}$ is called a **hyperplane** (a line in two dimensions, a plane in three), and $\{x : a^{\mathsf T}x \le b\}$ is called a **half-space**, with a as its outward normal. A half-space is a convex set: if $a^{\mathsf T}x \le b$ and $a^{\mathsf T}y \le b$, then $a^{\mathsf T}(sx + (1-s)y) = s\,a^{\mathsf T}x + (1-s)\,a^{\mathsf T}y \le sb + (1-s)b = b$. The intersection $\{x: Ax \le b\}$ of finitely many half-spaces is called a **polyhedron**; convex polygons in the plane and boxes and prisms in space are all polyhedra.

**Theorem 8.4.1** The intersection of any number of convex sets is a convex set. Hence polyhedra are convex sets.

*Proof*　Let x and y belong to every convex set $C_\alpha$. Then for every α the segment $sx + (1-s)y$ lies in $C_\alpha$, and hence in their intersection.　∎

**Definition 8.4.2 (Convex function)** Let $f: C \to \mathbb R$, where C is a convex set. If for any $x, y \in C$ and $s \in [0, 1]$,

$$
f\big(s\,x + (1-s)\,y\big) \le s\,f(x) + (1-s)\,f(y) , \tag{8.4.2}
$$

f is called a **convex function**; if the inequality holds strictly whenever $x \ne y$ and 0 < s < 1, f is called a **strictly convex function**.

The right-hand side of Eq. (8.4.2) is a point on the chord joining (x, f(x)) and (y, f(y)), and the left-hand side is the point of the graph at the same position, so a convex function is a function whose "chords always lie above its graph" (Figure 8.4.1 (c)). Eq. (8.4.2) is called **Jensen's inequality**; the Danish mathematician Johan Jensen made a systematic study of the functions satisfying it in 1906.

**Example 8.4.1** The inverse kinematics objective function of the planar 2R arm, $f(\theta) = \tfrac12\|p(\theta) - p_d\|^2$, is not a convex function. Take two inverse solutions $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$, with $f(\theta_A) = f(\theta_B) = 0$; at their midpoint, Program 8.4.1 computes $f = {{ex8_4.f_mid:.4f}}$ m², which is greater than the average 0 of the two ends, so Jensen's inequality fails (Figure 8.4.1 (d)). This also shows that its "valley floor" is not in one place only.

Deciding convexity directly from the definition is often inconvenient. For differentiable functions there are two equivalent conditions.

**Theorem 8.4.2 (First-order condition)** Let f be differentiable on an open convex set C. Then f is a convex function if and only if for any $x, y \in C$,

$$
f(y) \ge f(x) + \nabla f(x)^{\mathsf T}(y - x) . \tag{8.4.3}
$$

*Proof*　Let f be a convex function. By Eq. (8.4.2), for 0 < s ≤ 1, $f(x + s(y - x)) \le f(x) + s\,[f(y) - f(x)]$, that is, $\frac{f(x + s(y-x)) - f(x)}{s} \le f(y) - f(x)$; letting $s \to 0$, the left-hand side tends to the directional derivative $\nabla f(x)^{\mathsf T}(y - x)$ (Theorem 8.1.1), which gives Eq. (8.4.3). Conversely, suppose Eq. (8.4.3) holds, and write $z = sx + (1-s)y$. Apply Eq. (8.4.3) to x and z and to y and z: $f(x) \ge f(z) + \nabla f(z)^{\mathsf T}(x - z)$ and $f(y) \ge f(z) + \nabla f(z)^{\mathsf T}(y - z)$. Multiplying the first by s and the second by 1 − s and adding, the gradient terms give $\nabla f(z)^{\mathsf T}(sx + (1-s)y - z) = 0$, and we obtain $sf(x) + (1-s)f(y) \ge f(z)$.　∎

The geometric meaning of Eq. (8.4.3) is that the graph of a convex function always lies above every one of its tangent lines (tangent planes). It follows at once that if $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$, then $f(y) \ge f(x^*)$ for all y. In other words, a stationary point of a differentiable convex function is always a global minimizer. The trouble of Section 8.1, that "a stationary point may be a saddle point", does not exist for convex functions.

**Theorem 8.4.3 (Second-order condition)** Let f have continuous second partial derivatives on an open convex set C. Then f is a convex function if and only if the Hessian matrix $\nabla^2 f(x)$ is positive semidefinite for all $x \in C$.

*Proof*　Suppose the Hessian matrix is positive semidefinite everywhere. By the Lagrange form of the remainder used in the proof of Theorem 8.1.4, $f(y) = f(x) + \nabla f(x)^{\mathsf T}(y-x) + \tfrac12(y-x)^{\mathsf T}\nabla^2 f(\xi)(y-x)$, with ξ on the segment between x and y (inside C); the last term is non-negative, so Eq. (8.4.3) holds and f is a convex function. Conversely, if at some point x there is a d with $d^{\mathsf T}\nabla^2 f(x)d < 0$, then by Eq. (8.1.8), for s small enough, $f(x + sd) < f(x) + s\nabla f(x)^{\mathsf T}d$, contradicting Eq. (8.4.3).　∎

By Theorem 8.4.3, the quadratic function $\tfrac12x^{\mathsf T}Qx + c^{\mathsf T}x$ is a convex function if and only if Q is positive semidefinite; the least-squares objective function $\tfrac12\|Ax - b\|^2$ has Hessian matrix $A^{\mathsf T}A$ and is always a convex function (Program 8.4.1 spot-checks Jensen's inequality 1,000 times for a random A). Directly from the definition one can also verify that any norm ‖x‖ is a convex function (by the triangle inequality), that a non-negative weighted sum of convex functions is a convex function, and that the pointwise maximum of several convex functions is still a convex function. These rules let us build up and recognize convex functions like building blocks.

### 8.4.3 Convex optimization problems: a local optimum is a global optimum

**Definition 8.4.3 (Convex optimization problem)** A problem of the form

$$
\min_x\ f(x) \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad g_i(x) \le 0\ \ (i = 1, \ldots, m), \qquad Ex = d \tag{8.4.4}
$$

is called a **convex optimization** problem if f and all the $g_i$ are convex functions.

It is important that the equality constraints can only be linear: $h(x) = 0$ is equivalent to $h \le 0$ and $-h \le 0$, which requires both h and −h to be convex functions, and only linear functions satisfy this. The feasible set of a convex problem is a convex set: each $\{x: g_i(x) \le 0\}$ is a convex set (if $g_i(x) \le 0$ and $g_i(y) \le 0$, then by Eq. (8.4.2) $g_i(sx + (1-s)y) \le 0$), the solution set of linear equations is a convex set, and by Theorem 8.4.1 their intersection is a convex set. The convex QPs of Section 8.3 are convex problems; problems whose objective function and constraints are all linear are called linear programs, and they too are convex problems.

**Theorem 8.4.4** Every local minimizer of a convex optimization problem is a global minimizer. If f is a strictly convex function, there is at most one global minimizer.

*Proof*　Let $x^*$ be a local minimizer, and suppose there is a feasible point y with $f(y) < f(x^*)$. For 0 < s ≤ 1, $z_s = sy + (1-s)x^*$ is feasible (the feasible set is convex), and by Eq. (8.4.2), $f(z_s) \le sf(y) + (1-s)f(x^*) < f(x^*)$. For s small enough, $z_s$ is arbitrarily close to $x^*$, contradicting local minimality. If f is strictly convex and $x_1 \ne x_2$ are both global minimizers, with function value $p^*$, then the midpoint is feasible and $f(\tfrac12x_1 + \tfrac12x_2) < p^*$, a contradiction.　∎

**Theorem 8.4.5 (Sufficiency of the KKT conditions)** Let problem (8.4.4) be a convex problem with f and $g_i$ differentiable. If a feasible point $x^*$ and multipliers λ and $\mu \ge 0$ satisfy the KKT conditions (8.3.4), then $x^*$ is a global minimizer.

*Proof*　For any feasible point x, by Theorem 8.4.2 and the stationarity condition,

$$
f(x) \ge f(x^*) + \nabla f(x^*)^{\mathsf T}(x - x^*) = f(x^*) - \sum_i\mu_i\nabla g_i(x^*)^{\mathsf T}(x - x^*) - \lambda^{\mathsf T}E(x - x^*) .
$$

E(x − x*) = 0; further, applying Eq. (8.4.3) to the convex function $g_i$, $\nabla g_i(x^*)^{\mathsf T}(x - x^*) \le g_i(x) - g_i(x^*)$, and multiplying by $\mu_i \ge 0$ does not change the direction of the inequality. By complementary slackness $\mu_i g_i(x^*) = 0$, we get $f(x) \ge f(x^*) - \sum_i\mu_i g_i(x) \ge f(x^*)$, where the last step uses $\mu_i \ge 0$ and $g_i(x) \le 0$.　∎

The sufficiency part of Theorem 8.3.1 is just the special case of this theorem for QPs. Note that this theorem says only that "satisfying the KKT conditions means optimal", and it needs no constraint qualification. Conversely, the optimum of a convex problem need not have KKT multipliers: the example of Section 8.3.2, $\min x_1$ s.t. $x_1^2 + x_2^2 \le 0$, is a convex problem. Section 8.4.5 will show that with Slater's condition added, the KKT conditions are both necessary and sufficient for convex problems.

### 8.4.4 Separating hyperplanes and Farkas' lemma

The most useful geometric property of convex sets is that a hyperplane can separate them from points outside.

**Theorem 8.4.6 (Separating hyperplane theorem)** Let C be a non-empty closed convex set and y ∉ C a point. Then there exist a non-zero vector a and a number b such that

$$
a^{\mathsf T}x \le b < a^{\mathsf T}y \qquad \text{for all}\ x \in C . \tag{8.4.5}
$$

*Proof*　Let $x_0$ be the point of C closest to y (C is closed, so a closest point exists; this is a standard result of analysis). Let $a = y - x_0 \ne 0$ and $b = a^{\mathsf T}x_0$. Take any x ∈ C; for 0 < t ≤ 1, $z = x_0 + t(x - x_0) \in C$, so $\|y - z\|^2 \ge \|y - x_0\|^2$. Expanding,

$$
\|a - t(x - x_0)\|^2 - \|a\|^2 = -2t\,a^{\mathsf T}(x - x_0) + t^2\|x - x_0\|^2 \ge 0 .
$$

Dividing both sides by t and letting t → 0 gives $a^{\mathsf T}(x - x_0) \le 0$, that is, $a^{\mathsf T}x \le b$. Moreover $a^{\mathsf T}y - b = a^{\mathsf T}(y - x_0) = \|a\|^2 > 0$.　∎

The construction in the proof is intuitive: drop a "perpendicular" from y to C, with foot $x_0$; the hyperplane through $x_0$ perpendicular to $y - x_0$ has all of C on one side and y on the other. The same reasoning extends to two disjoint closed convex sets: as long as one of them is bounded, there is a hyperplane that strictly separates them (take the perpendicular bisector of the closest pair of points of the two sets). The example in Section 8.4.6 will use this. We now use Theorem 8.4.6 to supply the proof owed from Section 8.3.

*Proof of Lemma 8.3.1 (Farkas' lemma)*　Let $K = \{\sum_j\lambda_je_j + \sum_i\mu_ia_i : \lambda_j \in \mathbb R,\ \mu_i \ge 0\}$. K is a convex set and is closed under multiplication by positive numbers (it is a cone); a cone generated by finitely many vectors is closed (for the proof see the book by Boyd and Vandenberghe, or R. Tyrrell Rockafellar's *Convex Analysis*). What we must prove is $-g \in K$. If not, by Theorem 8.4.6 there exist d and b with $d^{\mathsf T}k \le b < -d^{\mathsf T}g$ for all k ∈ K. Since 0 ∈ K, b ≥ 0, so $g^{\mathsf T}d < 0$. Further, if some k ∈ K had $d^{\mathsf T}k > 0$, multiplying k by a large enough factor (it stays in K) would exceed b, so $d^{\mathsf T}k \le 0$ for all k ∈ K. Taking $k = a_i$ gives $a_i^{\mathsf T}d \le 0$; taking $k = \pm e_j$ gives $e_j^{\mathsf T}d = 0$. Such a d satisfies the hypothesis of the lemma, yet makes $g^{\mathsf T}d < 0$, a contradiction.　∎

### 8.4.5 Duality

For problem (8.4.4), write the Lagrangian $L(x, \lambda, \mu) = f(x) + \sum_i\mu_ig_i(x) + \lambda^{\mathsf T}(Ex - d)$.

**Definition 8.4.4 (Lagrange dual function)** For μ ≥ 0,

$$
q(\lambda, \mu) = \inf_x\ L(x, \lambda, \mu) \tag{8.4.6}
$$

is called the **Lagrange dual function**, where inf denotes the infimum (the minimum over all x; when the minimum is not attained, the infimum is taken).

**Theorem 8.4.7 (Weak duality)** For any μ ≥ 0, any λ and any feasible point x,

$$
q(\lambda, \mu) \le f(x) . \tag{8.4.7}
$$

Hence $q(\lambda, \mu) \le p^*$, where $p^*$ is the optimal value of problem (8.4.4).

*Proof*　Since x is feasible, $g_i(x) \le 0$ and Ex = d; also $\mu_i \ge 0$, so $L(x, \lambda, \mu) = f(x) + \sum_i\mu_ig_i(x) \le f(x)$; and q is the infimum of L over x, so $q \le L(x, \lambda, \mu)$.　∎

Weak duality holds for any problem; it does not need convexity. Its use is that it gives a lower bound on the optimal value: with a feasible point x and a set of multipliers (λ, μ) in hand, we know that $f(x) - p^* \le f(x) - q(\lambda, \mu)$, and the right-hand side is called the **duality gap**. A small duality gap proves that x is not far from optimal, and this does not require knowing $p^*$ itself. Finding the best lower bound, $\max_{\mu\ge0, \lambda} q(\lambda, \mu)$, is called the **dual problem**, and the original problem is correspondingly called the primal problem. Eq. (8.4.7) is called **weak duality**; if the two optimal values are equal, **strong duality** is said to hold. For a convex problem, if there is a strictly feasible point (all $g_i(x) < 0$ and Ex = d), strong duality always holds, and moreover, when the optimal value is finite, an optimal solution $(\lambda^*, \mu^*)$ of the dual problem exists, and it is exactly the KKT multipliers at the optimum of the primal problem. This condition is called **Slater's condition**, given by Morton Slater in 1950 (for the proof see the discussion of strong duality in Boyd and Vandenberghe's *Convex Optimization*). It is the most commonly used constraint qualification for convex problems: apart from linear constraints, as long as a point can be found that satisfies all the inequality constraints strictly, the KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient for optimality. The counterexample $x_1^2 + x_2^2 \le 0$ above has no strictly feasible point, so it does not satisfy Slater's condition.

### 8.4.6 The barrier method

How are convex problems solved? For linear inequality constraints $a_i^{\mathsf T}x \le b_i$, a natural idea is to "absorb" the constraints into the objective function: build a "wall" on the boundary of the feasible set so that the iterates can never cross it. Take the logarithmic barrier

$$
\phi(x) = -\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log\big(b_i - a_i^{\mathsf T}x\big) ,
$$

which is defined in the interior of the feasible set and tends to +∞ as the point approaches any boundary. Use it to approximate the original problem:

$$
\min_x\ t\,f(x) + \phi(x) , \tag{8.4.8}
$$

where t > 0 is a parameter. The larger t is, the more the objective-function part counts and the smaller the influence of the barrier, and the closer the solution $x^*(t)$ of Eq. (8.4.8) is to the solution of the original problem. $t\,f + \phi$ is a convex function (−log is a convex function, and a sum of convex functions is still a convex function) with no constraints, so it can be solved by Newton's method of Section 8.1; in the line search, we need only make sure that the new point stays in the interior of the feasible set. As t varies, $x^*(t)$ traces a curve called the **central path**.

**Theorem 8.4.8** Suppose the problem has only linear inequality constraints (and possibly linear equality constraints), f is a differentiable convex function, and $x^*(t)$ is the solution of Eq. (8.4.8). Then

$$
f(x^*(t)) - p^* \le \frac{m}{t} , \tag{8.4.9}
$$

where m is the number of inequality constraints.

*Proof*　$x^*(t)$ satisfies the stationarity condition of Eq. (8.4.8): $t\nabla f(x^*(t)) + \sum_i a_i/(b_i - a_i^{\mathsf T}x^*(t)) = 0$. Let

$$
\mu_i = \frac{1}{t\,\big(b_i - a_i^{\mathsf T}x^*(t)\big)} > 0 , \tag{8.4.10}
$$

Then the stationarity condition becomes $\nabla f(x^*(t)) + \sum_i\mu_ia_i = 0$. This says that $x^*(t)$ is a stationary point of the convex function $L(x, \mu) = f(x) + \sum_i\mu_i(a_i^{\mathsf T}x - b_i)$, and hence its global minimizer (the remark after Theorem 8.4.2). So $q(\mu) = L(x^*(t), \mu) = f(x^*(t)) - \sum_i\mu_i(b_i - a_i^{\mathsf T}x^*(t)) = f(x^*(t)) - m/t$. By weak duality $q(\mu) \le p^*$, which gives Eq. (8.4.9). With linear equality constraints, add a term $\lambda^{\mathsf T}(Ex - d)$ to L; the argument is the same.　∎

This proof also gives an upper bound m/t on the duality gap and a set of approximate multipliers (8.4.10). From this we obtain the barrier method: start from a strictly feasible point and a moderate t, find $x^*(t)$ by Newton's method (this is called centring), then enlarge t by some factor (say 10) and centre again starting from the previous centre, until m/t is below the required accuracy. The idea of using the logarithm as a barrier was put forward by Ragnar Frisch in 1955; Anthony V. Fiacco and Garth P. McCormick developed this "sequential unconstrained minimization" approach systematically in their 1968 book; in 1984 Narendra Karmarkar proposed a polynomial-time interior-point algorithm for linear programming, which set off a wave of research on interior-point methods; and the self-concordant function theory of Nesterov and Nemirovskii proved the polynomial complexity of the barrier method for a large class of convex problems. Methods of this kind, which advance through the interior of the feasible set, are collectively called **interior-point methods**; the one presented in this section is the simplest of them, the **barrier method**.

**Example 8.4.2** The shortest distance between a gripper and an obstacle. In a top view, the projection of the gripper is a 12 cm × 6 cm rectangle rotated by 25°, and the obstacle is a triangle; both are convex polygons, written as $A_1u \le b_1$ and $A_2w \le b_2$ (one inequality per edge, with unit outward normals). The shortest distance between them is given by

$$
\min_{u, w}\ \tfrac12\|u - w\|^2 \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad A_1u \le b_1,\quad A_2w \le b_2 , \tag{8.4.11}

a convex QP with 4 variables and 7 inequality constraints. **Example 8.4.3** The path of an AGV in convex corridors. In Figure 8.4.3 the free space of the workshop is L-shaped and is not a convex set. Split it into two convex regions: the lower corridor $R_1$ (whose upper edge is the slanting edge of the shelves) and the right-hand corridor $R_2$. The shelves have already been "inflated" outwards by the radius of the AGV, so the AGV need only be treated as a point. The path joins the start $x_0$, the goal $x_{10}$ and 9 waypoints $x_1, \ldots, x_9$; we require $x_1, \ldots, x_5$ to lie in $R_1$ and $x_5, \ldots, x_9$ in $R_2$ ($x_5$ in the intersection of the two). The problem is

$$
\min\ \sum_{k=0}^{9}\|x_{k+1} - x_k\|^2 \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad x_1, \ldots, x_5 \in R_1,\quad x_5, \ldots, x_9 \in R_2 , \tag{8.4.12}
$$

The objective function spreads the waypoints evenly and makes the path short and straight; it is a convex quadratic function. The constraints are linear inequalities, {{ex8_4.mc}} of them, and there are {{ex8_4.nvar}} variables.

The key point of this model is that the constraints guarantee that the whole path, and not just the waypoints, stays clear of the shelves. Two neighbouring waypoints lie in the same convex region, and by the definition (8.4.1) of a convex set, the whole segment joining them lies in that region; $x_5$ belongs to both regions and joins the two parts. Program 8.4.1 spot-checks 50 points on each segment, and all of them are in the corresponding region.

Solved by the barrier method, it takes {{ex8_4.outer_c}} centrings and {{ex8_4.steps_c}} Newton steps in all. The optimal path consists of two straight segments: from the start straight to the corner where the two regions meet, $x_5 = {{ex8_4.corner}}$ m, and then straight to the goal, with total length {{ex8_4.length:.4f}} m. The solution given by SciPy's SLSQP method differs from it by ${{ex8_4.sc_err:sci0}}$ m. The central path shows clearly what the barrier function does: the corner waypoint $x_5$ is at ${{ex8_4.cp1}}$ m when t = 1, far from all the boundaries; it moves to ${{ex8_4.cp10}}$ at t = 10, to ${{ex8_4.cp100}}$ at t = 100 and to ${{ex8_4.cp1000}}$ at t = 1,000, approaching the corner step by step.

In actual motion planning the difficulty lies in two things: how to find large enough convex regions among the obstacles, and which region each waypoint should belong to. For the first there are dedicated algorithms, such as the IRIS method of Robin Deits and Russ Tedrake (2015); the second is a combinatorial problem, commonly handled by mixed-integer programming or by first finding a rough path with a sampling-based planner (Chapter 53). Another approach is not to divide the region in advance, but to linearize the obstacle-avoidance constraints near the current path and solve convex problems repeatedly; this is called sequential convex optimization, and TrajOpt, by John Schulman and colleagues in 2014, plans manipulator motion in this way.

### 8.4.7 Everyday examples

Put a small ball in a smooth bowl, and wherever you put it, it rolls to the same lowest point. The shape of the bowl is convex: between any two points, the bottom of the bowl always lies below the line joining them, and there is nowhere for the ball to get stuck. Put a small ball on an egg tray, and it drops into the nearest dimple; put it in different places and it ends up in different places: the shape of the tray is not convex, and every dimple is a local lowest point.

The benefits of convexity can also be seen in everyday decisions. Divide a sum of money among several items of expenditure. If the "dissatisfaction" with each item grows faster and faster as the money spent on it shrinks (which is exactly a convex function), then simply moving money step by step from items with low "marginal dissatisfaction" to items with high marginal dissatisfaction is sure to reach the best allocation in the end, without getting stuck at a plan that "looks good enough". The portfolio problem of Section 8.3.6 is just such a convex problem.

Convex problems come with a guarantee of global optimality, but a great many problems in robotics are inherently non-convex, such as inverse kinematics and calibration. The next section returns to non-convex problems and discusses the most common class of them: nonlinear least squares, whose objective function is a sum of squared residuals.

**Engineer's notes**

- When you are given an optimization problem, first ask whether it is convex. If the objective function and the inequality constraints are convex functions and the equality constraints are linear, you can safely use a mature convex optimization solver (such as OSQP, ECOS or MOSEK, or the Python modelling tool CVXPY) and trust that what it computes is the global optimum.
- Non-convex problems can often be "convexified": split a non-convex free space into several convex regions; approximate the robot and the obstacles by convex hulls, spheres or capsules; linearize the non-convex constraints near the current solution and solve convex problems repeatedly.
- The barrier method needs a strictly feasible starting point. If none is at hand, first solve an auxiliary problem, for example $\min s$ s.t. $a_i^{\mathsf T}x - b_i \le s$; once s < 0, a strictly feasible point has been found.
- The duality gap is a reliable criterion for stopping. A duality gap reported by the solver that is smaller than the required accuracy proves the quality of the solution, with no need to compare it with other methods.
- In obstacle avoidance, leave a margin around the obstacles: inflate them according to the size of the robot, the localization error and the control error; otherwise the optimal path will just graze the edges and corners of the obstacles, like the corner in Example 8.4.3.
