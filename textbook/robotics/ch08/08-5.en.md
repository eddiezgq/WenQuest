### 8.5.1 The problem: a robot's dimensions are not exact

Ask a new industrial robot, fresh from the factory, to return again and again to the same taught point, and the error is usually only a few tens of micrometres; this is called pose repeatability. But if the joint angles are computed with the kinematic formulas from the nominal dimensions and the robot is sent to a point it has never been taught, the error may reach several millimetres; this is called pose accuracy, or absolute accuracy. The difference comes from machining errors in the link lengths, offsets of the joint zero positions introduced during assembly, gear backlash and the deformation of the links. When a robot is guided by vision or by offline programming, it is the absolute accuracy that matters, so a **kinematic calibration** must be done first: the actual kinematic parameters of the robot are measured.

Calibration is done as follows. The robot is put into N different configurations, and the joint angles $\theta_k$ are recorded, while an external instrument such as a laser tracker measures the actual position $y_k$ of the end-effector. Let the kinematic model contain the unknown parameters φ (link lengths, zero-position offsets and so on), and let the end-effector position predicted by the model be $p(\theta_k; \varphi)$; we must find the φ that brings prediction and measurement as close together as possible:

$$
\min_{x}\ F(x) = \tfrac12\|r(x)\|^2 = \tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^{m} r_i(x)^2 , \tag{8.5.1}
$$

Here x = φ, the residual r stacks the differences $p(\theta_k; \varphi) - y_k$ of all the measurements into one long vector, and m = 2N (planar) or 3N (spatial). When the residual is a nonlinear function of the parameters, Eq. (8.5.1) is called a **nonlinear least squares** problem. The linear least squares of Chapter 2 is the special case r(x) = Ax − b. The inverse kinematics of Section 8.1 also has this form, with x = θ and r = p(θ) − p_d there. Hand–eye calibration (Chapter 50), camera calibration (Chapter 54), identification of dynamic parameters (Chapter 26) and localization and mapping for mobile robots (Chapter 47) are all, at heart, nonlinear least squares.

Eq. (8.5.1) can of course be solved by the general methods of Section 8.1, but it has a special structure: the objective function is a sum of squares of residuals. Both methods of this section exploit this structure.

### 8.5.2 The Gauss–Newton method

Write $J(x) = \partial r/\partial x$ for the Jacobian matrix of the residual; it is an m×n matrix whose row i is $\nabla r_i^{\mathsf T}$. Exactly as in the derivation of Eqs. (8.1.6) and (8.1.9),

$$
\nabla F(x) = J(x)^{\mathsf T}r(x) , \tag{8.5.2}
$$

$$
\nabla^2 F(x) = J(x)^{\mathsf T}J(x) + \sum_{i=1}^{m}r_i(x)\,\nabla^2 r_i(x) . \tag{8.5.3}
$$

The first term of the Hessian matrix uses only first derivatives; the second term needs the second derivatives of every residual and costs much more to compute. The Gauss–Newton method drops the second term and uses $J^{\mathsf T}J$ in place of the Hessian, so the Newton step (8.1.15) becomes

$$
J^{\mathsf T}J\,\Delta = -J^{\mathsf T}r , \qquad x_{k+1} = x_k + \Delta . \tag{8.5.4}
$$

This step also has a more intuitive origin. Linearize the residual at $x_k$, $r(x_k + \Delta) \approx r + J\Delta$, and then find the Δ that minimizes the sum of squares of the linearized residual:

$$
\min_\Delta\ \tfrac12\|r + J\Delta\|^2 .
$$

This is a linear least squares problem, and its normal equations (2.5.4) are precisely Eq. (8.5.4). So the Gauss–Newton method is "linearize again and again, and solve one linear least squares problem each time". Numerically, this linear least squares problem should be solved by a QR factorization or a singular value decomposition, without forming $J^{\mathsf T}J$ explicitly (Theorem 2.5.4 in Chapter 2: the condition number of $J^{\mathsf T}J$ is the square of that of J). The method is named after Carl Friedrich Gauss: in *Theoria motus* (Theory of the motion of the heavenly bodies, 1809) Gauss gave his account of the method of least squares, which Adrien-Marie Legendre had published in 1805, and used it to determine the orbits of planets and asteroids from observations; the relation between the orbital parameters and the observed quantities is nonlinear, so it has to be linearized about an approximate solution and improved step by step.

When J is an invertible square matrix (m = n), Eq. (8.5.4) reduces to $J\Delta = -r$, which is just Newton's method of Chapter 7 for solving the system r(x) = 0. This is exactly what the numerical inverse kinematics of Chapter 15 does.

**Theorem 8.5.1 (Descent direction)** Let the columns of J be linearly independent and $\nabla F(x) \ne 0$. Then the Gauss–Newton step Δ is a descent direction: $\nabla F(x)^{\mathsf T}\Delta < 0$.

*Proof*　When the columns are linearly independent, $J^{\mathsf T}J$ is positive definite (see the proof of Theorem 2.5.1 in Chapter 2), and so is its inverse. Write $g = J^{\mathsf T}r = \nabla F \ne 0$; then $\nabla F^{\mathsf T}\Delta = -g^{\mathsf T}(J^{\mathsf T}J)^{-1}g < 0$.　∎

Compared with Newton's method, the Gauss–Newton method has two advantages: it needs only first derivatives; and $J^{\mathsf T}J$ is always positive semidefinite, so the step cannot point uphill or converge to a saddle point as Newton's method can (Example 8.1.4). The price is that the second term of Eq. (8.5.3) has been thrown away. How large is this term at the solution? If the residual of the problem is zero at the solution, that is, if the model fits the data exactly (inverse kinematics with a reachable target, calibration without noise), the term vanishes at the solution; such a problem is called a **zero-residual problem**. Near the solution the Gauss–Newton method is then almost the same as Newton's method, and it also converges quadratically. If the residual at the solution is not zero, the discarded term is not zero, and the convergence generally becomes linear; the larger the residual and the more curved the residual functions, the slower the convergence, and if the residual is too large the method may even fail to converge (see the book by Nocedal and Wright for a detailed analysis). The residuals of a calibration problem come from measurement noise and are usually far smaller than the residuals caused by the parameter errors, so the Gauss–Newton method is still fast there.

### 8.5.3 Weaknesses of the Gauss–Newton method

The Gauss–Newton method has two weaknesses, both related to linearization.

First, when the columns of J are nearly dependent (the arm is close to a singular configuration, or the calibration data are not enough to determine some parameters), $J^{\mathsf T}J$ is nearly singular, and the step obtained from Eq. (8.5.4) may be enormous. The linearization can be trusted only in a small neighbourhood, so such a large step is meaningless. If Example 8.5.1 is solved from the fully extended configuration θ = (0, 0, 0), the singular values of J are ${{ex8_5.sv0}}$ m, the smallest of them very small; the very first Gauss–Newton step turns joint 3 through {{ex8_5.step_gn1:.0f}}°, almost two full turns; it then oscillates between about 1,900° and 2,010°, and after {{ex8_5.k_gns}} steps it stops at ${{ex8_5.xgs}}$ (in degrees). Reduced to the range −180° to 180°, this happens to be exactly $\theta^*$, but that is mere luck: with another target it might stop at a different inverse kinematics solution, or not converge at all (Figure 8.5.1, right).

Second, even when J is well conditioned, the linearization may be quite inaccurate far from the solution, and the Gauss–Newton step may "overshoot" the bottom of the valley, so that F actually increases. Take the planar 2R arm of Figure 8.5.2 at θ = (30°, 30°), with the same target as in Section 8.1. The Gauss–Newton step is ${{ex8_5.d_gn2}}$ rad, at an angle of {{ex8_5.ang_gn2:.1f}}° to the negative gradient; after this step F increases from {{ex8_5.F0_2:.5f}} m² to {{ex8_5.Fgn_2:.5f}} m².

The remedy is to limit the length of each step, so that the linearization can still be trusted over the length of the step.

### 8.5.4 The Levenberg–Marquardt method

In 1944, Kenneth Levenberg, working at the US Army's Frankford Arsenal, proposed adding a "damping" term to the coefficient matrix of Eq. (8.5.4) to make the Gauss–Newton method more reliable:

$$
\big(J^{\mathsf T}J + \mu I\big)\,\Delta = -J^{\mathsf T}r , \qquad \mu > 0 . \tag{8.5.5}
$$

μ is called the **damping parameter**; it has the same units as $J^{\mathsf T}J$. In 1963, Donald Marquardt, working on nonlinear parameter estimation at DuPont, independently proposed the same method, replaced I by $\mathrm{diag}(J^{\mathsf T}J)$, and gave a strategy for adjusting μ automatically according to the progress of the iteration. Today the method is called the **Levenberg–Marquardt method**, or LM method for short. In his original paper Levenberg used a weighting factor w (the damping corresponds to 1/w), and Marquardt denoted the damping parameter by λ; this book writes μ (following Madsen, Nielsen and Tingleff), first because this chapter already uses λ for eigenvalues and for the multipliers of equality constraints, and second because Theorem 8.5.2 below will show that the damping parameter is precisely the multiplier of an inequality constraint, and this chapter writes the multipliers of inequality constraints as μ. The $\lambda^2$ in the damped least squares of Eq. (7.3.12) in Chapter 7 (where λ has units of m) is exactly the μ here.

**Theorem 8.5.2 (Properties of the LM step)** Let μ > 0, let Δ(μ) be the solution of Eq. (8.5.5), and let $J = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}$ be the singular value decomposition of J (with m ≥ n), with singular values $\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_n$ and with $u_i$, $v_i$ the columns of U and V. Then

1. $\Delta(\mu) = -\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{\sigma_i\,(u_i^{\mathsf T}r)}{\sigma_i^2 + \mu}\,v_i$, which is always defined, even when the columns of J are linearly dependent;

2. if $\nabla F \ne 0$, Δ(μ) is a descent direction;

3. $\|\Delta(\mu)\|$ decreases monotonically as μ increases; as μ → 0 (with the columns of J linearly independent) Δ tends to the Gauss–Newton step, and as μ → ∞, $\Delta \approx -\nabla F/\mu$, a very short gradient descent step;

4. Δ(μ) is the solution of the trust-region problem

$$
\min_\Delta\ \tfrac12\|r + J\Delta\|^2 \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad \|\Delta\| \le \|\Delta(\mu)\| \tag{8.5.6}
$$

that is, within the ball of radius ‖Δ(μ)‖ centred at the current point, it minimizes the linearized residual.

*Proof*　(1) $J^{\mathsf T}J + \mu I = V(\Sigma^{\mathsf T}\Sigma + \mu I)V^{\mathsf T}$, whose diagonal entries $\sigma_i^2 + \mu > 0$, so it is invertible; $J^{\mathsf T}r = \sum_i\sigma_i(u_i^{\mathsf T}r)v_i$, and dividing term by term gives the result. (2) $J^{\mathsf T}J + \mu I$ is positive definite; the rest is as in the proof of Theorem 8.5.1. (3) By (1) and the mutual orthogonality of the $v_i$, $\|\Delta\|^2 = \sum_i\sigma_i^2(u_i^{\mathsf T}r)^2/(\sigma_i^2 + \mu)^2$, and every term decreases as μ increases; as μ → 0 the coefficient of each term tends to $1/\sigma_i$, which is the Gauss–Newton step given by the pseudoinverse (2.5.8); for very large μ, $\sigma_i^2 + \mu \approx \mu$ and $\Delta \approx -J^{\mathsf T}r/\mu$. (4) Write $\delta = \|\Delta(\mu)\|$. Problem (8.5.6) is equivalent to minimizing a convex quadratic function subject to the constraint $\tfrac12(\|\Delta\|^2 - \delta^2) \le 0$, which is a convex problem. Take the multiplier of this constraint equal to the damping parameter μ ≥ 0: the Lagrangian is $\tfrac12\|r + J\Delta\|^2 + \tfrac{\mu}2(\|\Delta\|^2 - \delta^2)$, and the stationarity condition $J^{\mathsf T}(r + J\Delta) + \mu\Delta = 0$ is just Eq. (8.5.5); the constraint is active at Δ(μ), so complementary slackness holds. By Theorem 8.4.5, the KKT conditions are sufficient for a convex problem, so Δ(μ) is optimal.　∎

Property (4) gives the geometric interpretation of the LM method (Figure 8.5.2): the larger μ is, the smaller the trust region, the more cautious the step and the closer its direction to the negative gradient; the smaller μ is, the larger the trust region and the closer the step to the Gauss–Newton step. The LM method moves continuously between gradient descent and the Gauss–Newton method. Here the KKT conditions and the convexity theory of Sections 8.3 and 8.4 come into their own.

**How to adjust μ.** μ has to be adjusted during the iteration according to how things go: when the linearization is accurate, decrease μ to move towards the Gauss–Newton method and converge fast; when it is inaccurate, increase μ to shorten the step and guarantee descent. The quantity that measures whether the linearization is accurate is the gain ratio

$$
\rho = \frac{F(x) - F(x + \Delta)}{L(0) - L(\Delta)} , \qquad L(\Delta) = \tfrac12\|r + J\Delta\|^2 , \tag{8.5.7}
$$

The numerator is the actual decrease, and the denominator is the decrease predicted by the linearized model. Using Eq. (8.5.5), the denominator simplifies to

$$
L(0) - L(\Delta) = \tfrac12\,\Delta^{\mathsf T}\big(\mu\Delta - J^{\mathsf T}r\big) , \tag{8.5.8}
$$

which is always positive (both terms are non-negative, and the second is positive when Δ ≠ 0). A ρ close to 1 means the linearization is very accurate; a small or even negative ρ means it is not. A commonly used rule was given by Hans Bruun Nielsen in 1999: if ρ > 0, accept the step and set

$$
\mu \leftarrow \mu\cdot\max\Big\{\tfrac13,\ 1 - (2\rho - 1)^3\Big\},\qquad \nu \leftarrow 2 ; \tag{8.5.9}
$$

if ρ ≤ 0, reject the step, set μ ← νμ and ν ← 2ν, and solve again. The initial value is $\mu_0 = \tau\max_i(J^{\mathsf T}J)_{ii}$, with τ commonly taken as $10^{-3}$. ρ is called the **gain ratio**, and methods that adjust the range of the step on its basis are collectively called **trust-region** methods.

Back to the example of Figure 8.5.2: the gain ratio of the Gauss–Newton step (μ → 0) is $\rho = {{ex8_5.rho_gn2:.3f}}$, so the step should be rejected; with μ = 0.02 the step shortens to {{ex8_5.nd_lm2:.3f}} rad, F falls to {{ex8_5.F_lm2:.5f}} m², and the gain ratio is $\rho = {{ex8_5.rho_lm2:.3f}}$, so the step is accepted. Now look again at Example 8.5.1 started from the fully extended configuration: the first LM step turns the joint through only {{ex8_5.step_lm1:.0f}}°, and after {{ex8_5.k_lms}} steps the method converges directly to $\theta^* = {{ex8_5.xls}}$ (in degrees), without winding round (Figure 8.5.1, right).

Marquardt's purpose in replacing I by $D = \mathrm{diag}(J^{\mathsf T}J)$ was this: when the parameters have different units and magnitudes (in calibration, lengths in m and angles in rad), μI "damps" the parameters unevenly; with D instead, changing the unit of a parameter does not change the behaviour of the algorithm. In 1978 Jorge J. Moré gave a reliable implementation of the LM method together with its convergence theory; it went into the MINPACK library, and it is what SciPy's `least_squares` function (`method="lm"`) calls. In robot inverse kinematics, Eq. (8.5.5) is well known under the name "damped least squares"; it was introduced independently in 1986 by Charles W. Wampler and by Yoshihiko Nakamura and Hideo Hanafusa to handle joint velocities near singular configurations (Chapter 15).

### 8.5.5 Kinematic calibration

**Example 8.5.2** Kinematic calibration of a planar 3R arm. The unknown parameters are the three link lengths and the zero-position offsets $\beta_i$ of the three joints (when the encoder reads $\theta_i$, the actual joint angle is $\theta_i + \beta_i$), six in all:

$$
y_k \approx p(\theta_k + \beta;\ L_1, L_2, L_3) , \qquad k = 1, \ldots, 12 , \tag{8.5.10}
$$

where p is Eq. (8.1.1) extended to three links. The nominal parameters are L = (425, 392, 100) mm and β = 0. To test the method, the data are generated by the program: take the "true" parameters $L = {{ex8_5.phi_true_L}}$ mm and $\beta = {{ex8_5.phi_true_b}}$ (in degrees), choose 12 sets of joint angles at random, compute the end-effector positions, and add measurement noise with a standard deviation of 0.05 mm (a typical level for a laser tracker). The residual has 24 components. The columns of the Jacobian matrix are easy to write down: the partial derivative with respect to $L_i$ is the unit vector along link i; the partial derivative with respect to $\beta_i$ equals the partial derivative of the end-effector position with respect to $\theta_i$, that is, column i of the Jacobian matrix of the planar 3R arm (with joint angles $\theta_k + \beta$), which is written in the same way as Eq. (2.1.9) in Chapter 2, only with one more joint.

Starting from the nominal parameters, the Gauss–Newton method converges in {{ex8_5.k_gc}} steps and the LM method in {{ex8_5.k_lc}}, while SciPy evaluates the residual {{ex8_5.sp_c_nfev}} times; all three agree:

$$
\hat L = {{ex8_5.L_hat}}\ \mathrm{mm}, \qquad \hat\beta = {{ex8_5.b_hat}}\ (°) .
$$

Compared with the "true" parameters, the length errors are ${{ex8_5.dL_mm}}$ mm and the zero-position errors are ${{ex8_5.dB_deg}}$ (in degrees), all far smaller than the errors of the nominal parameters. The root mean square of the residual falls from {{ex8_5.rms_before:.3f}} mm before calibration to {{ex8_5.rms_after:.4f}} mm, comparable with the measurement noise: what remains of the residual is already noise, which the model cannot and should not fit. This is exactly the criterion for judging whether a calibration has succeeded.

The real test of a calibration is on configurations that did not take part in it. The program takes another 50 sets of joint angles and compares the prediction errors of the end-effector position: with the nominal parameters, {{ex8_5.ev_nom_mean:.2f}} mm on average and {{ex8_5.ev_nom_max:.2f}} mm at most; with the calibrated parameters, {{ex8_5.ev_cal_mean:.3f}} mm on average and {{ex8_5.ev_cal_max:.3f}} mm at most (Figure 8.5.3). The absolute accuracy has improved by about two orders of magnitude.

A calibration can succeed only if the parameters can be determined from the data, that is, if the columns of J are linearly independent, and not too close to being dependent either. The condition number of J at the solution is {{ex8_5.cond_c:.1f}}, so the problem is well conditioned. If all 12 configurations varied only within a small range, the effects of some parameters could hardly be told apart in the data, J would be nearly column-dependent, and the result of the calibration would be extremely sensitive to noise. This is the same reason as "the orientations must differ widely" in the TCP calibration of Chapter 2. A real calibration of a six-axis robot usually has an error model with twenty or thirty parameters, and the choice of configurations is itself an optimization problem (the 1996 survey by John M. Hollerbach and Charles W. Wampler discusses the various calibration methods).

### 8.5.6 An everyday example

Indoor positioning of a mobile phone often uses ultra-wideband (UWB) anchors that measure distances: each anchor measures its distance to the phone, and the position of the phone is found from several distances. This is a nonlinear least squares problem: the unknown is the coordinate vector x of the phone, the i-th residual is $\|x - a_i\| - \rho_i$, $a_i$ is the position of the anchor and $\rho_i$ is the measured distance. The gradient of the residual with respect to x is the unit vector pointing from the anchor to the phone, and the Jacobian matrix is made up of these unit vectors.

**Example 8.5.3** A room of 6 m × 4 m has 4 anchors: three mounted in corners at a height of 2.5 m, and one at a height of 0.3 m (if all were mounted at the same height, it would be impossible to tell whether the phone is above or below that plane). The phone is actually at ${{ex8_5.phone}}$ m, and the measured distances, which carry random errors with a standard deviation of 5 cm, are ${{ex8_5.rho}}$ m. Starting from the centre of the room, the Gauss–Newton method converges in {{ex8_5.k_u}} steps to ${{ex8_5.xu}}$ m, {{ex8_5.pos_err_cm:.1f}} cm from the actual position, in agreement with the result of SciPy. With four distances and three unknowns, the residual at the solution is not zero, so the convergence is linear and takes a few more steps than in Example 8.5.1.

Satellite navigation receivers work on the same principle, except that the distances are computed from the travel time of the signals, and the error of the receiver clock has to be solved for as a fourth unknown, so at least four satellites are needed. The navigation chip of every phone and every car solves a nonlinear least squares problem like this every second.

Starting from "finding the lowest point", this chapter has discussed unconstrained optimization, equality constraints, inequality constraints, convex optimization and nonlinear least squares. Why least squares takes the sum of "squares", how data with different noise levels should be weighted, and how uncertain the estimates are: these questions must be answered in the language of probability, which is the subject of Chapter 9.

**Engineer's notes**

- The method of choice for nonlinear least squares is the LM method. Ready-made implementations (`scipy.optimize.least_squares`, Ceres Solver, g2o and others) are all mature; if you implement it yourself, the gain ratio and the update rule (8.5.9) for μ are the key points.
- Use analytic expressions for the Jacobian matrix whenever possible. Difference quotients are convenient, but they cost n extra residual evaluations per iteration, and their accuracy depends on the step. Once the analytic expressions are written, always check them once against difference quotients.
- When the residuals have different units and accuracies (positions in mm, orientations in rad), first weight them by the measurement accuracy, that is, divide each residual by its standard deviation, and only then sum the squares. Otherwise the less accurate data will dominate the result.
- After calibration, check three things: whether the root mean square of the residual is comparable with the measurement noise; whether the residuals show any obvious pattern (a systematic error that varies with the configuration means that the model is missing parameters); and whether the accuracy improves equally at test configurations that did not take part in the calibration.
- A measurement with a particularly large residual is often a wrong measurement (an outlier). The sum of squares is very sensitive to outliers; they can be removed first, or a "robust" cost function that grows more slowly (such as the Huber function) can be used instead; libraries such as Ceres all provide this.
- When the LM method (damped least squares) is used in inverse kinematics, μ should not be too small: near a singular configuration, too small a μ makes the joint velocities too large. Many controllers adjust μ automatically according to the smallest singular value (Chapter 15).
