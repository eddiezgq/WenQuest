### 8.1.1 The problem: inverse kinematics as "finding the lowest point"

Seen from the side, the upper arm and forearm of the UR5e form a planar two-link arm, that is, a planar 2R arm: the two links have lengths $L_1 = 0.425$ m and $L_2 = 0.392$ m, and the axes of both revolute joints are perpendicular to the page (Figure 8.1.1, left). The joint angle $\theta_1$ is measured from the x-axis, and $\theta_2$ is the angle through which the second link has turned relative to the first; both are positive anticlockwise and measured in radians. The position of the end-effector is

$$
p(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \theta = \begin{pmatrix} \theta_1 \\ \theta_2 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{8.1.1}
$$

Now the end-effector is required to reach the target point $p_d = {{ex8_1.pd}}$ m. To what angles should the two joints turn? This is the inverse kinematics problem. For the planar 2R arm the answer can be written down directly with the law of cosines, and Chapter 14 will discuss such analytic solutions systematically. But analytic solutions depend on special geometry of the mechanism: joint axes that are parallel or intersect, links without offsets. Arms with wrist offsets, seven-axis redundant arms and real robots with calibration errors mostly have no analytic solution. We need a method that does not depend on special geometry and works for any mechanism.

Look at the problem from another angle. For any set of joint angles θ there is a distance between the end-effector and the target; what we are looking for is exactly the θ that makes this distance zero. When a zero distance cannot be achieved (the target lies outside the workspace), we settle for the next best thing and look for the θ that makes the distance smallest. Inverse kinematics has thus become a problem of "finding a minimum":

$$
\min_{\theta}\ f(\theta), \qquad f(\theta) = \tfrac12\,\|p(\theta) - p_d\|^2 . \tag{8.1.2}
$$

We take the square of the distance rather than the distance itself because the square is smooth everywhere and easy to differentiate; the factor ½ in front only removes a factor 2 from the expression for the derivative and does not change the location of the minimum. The unit of f is m².

Problems of this kind, in which the best set of values is picked out of a set of quantities that are allowed to vary, according to some criterion, are called **optimization** problems. The quantities allowed to vary (here θ) are called the **decision variables**, and the function that measures how good they are (here f) is called the **objective function**. In general the decision variables are written $x \in \mathbb R^n$ and the problem is written $\min_x f(x)$. A problem of finding a maximum only needs f replaced by −f, so this chapter discusses only minima. If x may take any value in the whole of $\mathbb R^n$, the problem is called unconstrained optimization; if x must also satisfy certain conditions (for example, a joint angle may not exceed its limit), it is called constrained optimization, which Sections 8.2 to 8.4 discuss.

**Definition 8.1.1 (Minimizer)** Let $f: \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R$. If $f(x^*) \le f(x)$ for all x, $x^*$ is called a **global minimizer** of f. If there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $f(x^*) \le f(x)$ for all x satisfying $\|x - x^*\| < \varepsilon$, $x^*$ is called a **local minimizer**; if moreover the strict inequality $f(x^*) < f(x)$ holds for these $x \ne x^*$, it is called a strict local minimizer.

A local minimizer is compared only with nearby points; a global minimizer has to be compared with all points. The difference between the two is visible everywhere in inverse kinematics. Figure 8.1.1, right, shows the **level curves** of f in the $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ plane: f has the same value along each curve, just like the contour lines on a map. The figure has two minimizers, A and B, where f equals zero; they are the two inverse solutions with the elbow down and the elbow up. Because a joint angle returns to where it started after every 360°, the left and right edges of the figure are joined, and so are the top and bottom edges.

The computer cannot see this picture. All it can do is compute the value of f at a given θ and (as we shall see) the rate of change of f. This is very much like a person walking down a mountain in thick fog: unable to see where the valley is, one can only feel which way the slope underfoot tilts, take a step in the downhill direction and then judge again. Both methods of this section proceed step by step in this way and are called iterative methods: starting from an initial value $x_0$, they compute $x_{k+1}$ from $x_k$ by some rule until the required accuracy is reached. For this we first have to make "which way the slope tilts" precise.

### 8.1.2 The gradient and the directional derivative

The derivative $g'(s)$ of a function of one variable $g(s)$ at s is its rate of change, and also the slope of the tangent to its graph. A function of several variables $f(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ has n independent variables; we can let just one of them vary, keep the others fixed and differentiate with respect to that one, which gives the **partial derivative** $\partial f / \partial x_i$. Arrange the n partial derivatives in a column vector:

**Definition 8.1.2 (Gradient)** Suppose all the partial derivatives of f exist. The vector

$$
\nabla f(x) = \begin{pmatrix} \partial f / \partial x_1 \\ \vdots \\ \partial f / \partial x_n \end{pmatrix} \tag{8.1.3}
$$

is called the **gradient** of f at x.

The partial derivatives describe only the rates of change along the coordinate axes. What about walking at a slant? Let u be a unit vector; starting from x and walking a distance s along u, we arrive at $x + su$. The rate of change of the function value

$$
D_u f(x) = \lim_{s \to 0} \frac{f(x + su) - f(x)}{s} \tag{8.1.4}
$$

is called the **directional derivative** of f at x along u. The theorem below shows that the directional derivative is completely determined by the gradient.

**Theorem 8.1.1 (First-order Taylor expansion)** Suppose the partial derivatives of f exist and are continuous near x. Then for any $d \in \mathbb R^n$,

$$
f(x + d) = f(x) + \nabla f(x)^{\mathsf T} d + o(\|d\|) , \tag{8.1.5}
$$

where $o(\|d\|)$ denotes a term that is negligible compared with $\|d\|$, that is, one that tends to zero as $d \to 0$ after division by $\|d\|$. Consequently $D_u f(x) = \nabla f(x)^{\mathsf T} u$.

*Proof*　Take n = 2 as an example; the general case is exactly the same. Split the change from $(x_1, x_2)$ to $(x_1 + d_1, x_2 + d_2)$ into two stages, first changing the second component and then the first:

$$
f(x+d) - f(x) = \big[f(x_1 + d_1, x_2 + d_2) - f(x_1, x_2 + d_2)\big] + \big[f(x_1, x_2 + d_2) - f(x_1, x_2)\big] .
$$

In each bracket only one variable changes, so by Lagrange's mean value theorem for functions of one variable, the first term equals $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}(\xi_1, x_2 + d_2)\,d_1$ and the second equals $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}(x_1, \xi_2)\,d_2$, where $\xi_1$ lies between $x_1$ and $x_1 + d_1$ and $\xi_2$ between $x_2$ and $x_2 + d_2$. As $d \to 0$, both intermediate points tend to x, and by the continuity of the partial derivatives the two partial derivatives tend to $\partial f/\partial x_1(x)$ and $\partial f/\partial x_2(x)$ respectively, the differences tending to zero. A difference multiplied by $d_1$ or $d_2$ and then divided by $\|d\|$ still tends to zero (because $|d_i| \le \|d\|$). This gives Eq. (8.1.5). Substituting $d = su$ into Eq. (8.1.4) gives $D_u f = \nabla f^{\mathsf T}u$.　∎

What Eq. (8.1.5) says is this: near x, f can be approximated by a linear function, and the gradient holds the coefficients of this linear function. This is the starting point of every algorithm in the chapter.

**Theorem 8.1.2 (Steepest descent direction)** Suppose $\nabla f(x) \ne 0$. Among all unit vectors u, the directional derivative $D_u f(x)$ is largest for $u = \nabla f/\|\nabla f\|$, where it equals $\|\nabla f(x)\|$, and smallest for $u = -\nabla f/\|\nabla f\|$, where it equals $-\|\nabla f(x)\|$. The gradient is perpendicular to the level curve through x.

*Proof*　Let φ be the angle between u and $\nabla f$. Then $D_u f = \nabla f^{\mathsf T}u = \|\nabla f\|\cos\varphi$, which is largest at φ = 0 and smallest at φ = π. Next let $x(s)$ be a level curve through x, so that $f(x(s))$ is constant. Differentiating with respect to s, by Eq. (8.1.5) (the chain rule), $\nabla f(x(s))^{\mathsf T}x'(s) = 0$. $x'(s)$ is the tangent vector of the level curve, so the gradient is perpendicular to the tangent.　∎

Figure 8.1.2 illustrates this result: the gradient $\nabla f$ points in the direction in which f increases fastest and is perpendicular to the level curve; the negative gradient $-\nabla f$ is the direction of fastest decrease, called the steepest-descent direction. The rate of change along any direction u is the projection of the gradient on u.

Now compute the gradient of the inverse kinematics objective function (8.1.2). Write the residual as $r(\theta) = p(\theta) - p_d$; it is the position error of the end-effector relative to the target, in m. Since $f = \tfrac12(r_x^2 + r_y^2)$, the partial derivative with respect to $\theta_j$ is

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_j} = r_x\frac{\partial p_x}{\partial \theta_j} + r_y\frac{\partial p_y}{\partial \theta_j} , \qquad j = 1, 2 .
$$

$\partial p_x/\partial\theta_j$ and $\partial p_y/\partial\theta_j$ are exactly column j of the Jacobian matrix J(θ) of Eq. (2.1.9) of Chapter 2. Therefore

$$
\nabla f(\theta) = J(\theta)^{\mathsf T}\, r(\theta) . \tag{8.1.6}
$$

This formula has a clear meaning: column j of J is the velocity of the end-effector when joint j turns at unit angular velocity, and component j of the gradient is the dot product of this velocity with the error r. The faster turning joint j moves the end-effector in the direction of the error (away from the target), the larger this component, and the more the joint should turn the opposite way. The unit of the gradient is m²/rad.

**Example 8.1.1** The starting configuration is $\theta_0 = {{ex8_1.th0}}$ (in degrees), that is, upper arm horizontal and forearm vertical. By Eq. (8.1.1) the end-effector is at $p(\theta_0) = {{ex8_1.p0}}$ m, and the residual is $r = {{ex8_1.r0}}$ m. By Eq. (2.1.9),

$$
J(\theta_0) = {{ex8_1.J0}}\ \mathrm{m}, \qquad \nabla f(\theta_0) = J^{\mathsf T} r = {{ex8_1.g0}}\ \mathrm{m^2/rad} .
$$

Both components are positive: both joints should turn in the negative (clockwise) direction. If the two joints turn by equal amounts at the same time, $u = (1, 1)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt 2$, and the directional derivative is $\nabla f^{\mathsf T}u = {{ex8_1.Du:.5f}}$ m²/rad; the steepest-descent direction is $-\nabla f/\|\nabla f\| = {{ex8_1.ud}}$, and the rate of change along it is $-\|\nabla f\| = -{{ex8_1.gnorm0:.5f}}$ m²/rad. The former is slightly smaller than $\|\nabla f\|$, because the directions of u and of the gradient differ by only a few degrees; walking along u increases f, walking along the steepest-descent direction decreases it, and the two rates of change are almost equal in absolute value. Program 8.1.1 checks both numbers with the difference quotient $[f(\theta + hu) - f(\theta - hu)]/(2h)$.

### 8.1.3 Conditions for a minimizer

At the lowest point of a valley the ground is level, and it no longer descends in any direction. This intuition can be written as a theorem.

**Theorem 8.1.3 (First-order necessary condition)** If $x^*$ is a local minimizer of f and the partial derivatives of f are continuous near $x^*$, then $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$.

*Proof*　Suppose $g = \nabla f(x^*) \ne 0$. Take $d = -sg$ with s > 0; by Eq. (8.1.5), $f(x^* - sg) = f(x^*) - s\|g\|^2 + o(s)$. For s small enough, $o(s)$ is smaller than $s\|g\|^2$, so $f(x^* - sg) < f(x^*)$, contradicting the local minimum.　∎

**Definition 8.1.3 (Stationary point)** A point satisfying $\nabla f(x) = 0$ is called a **stationary point** of f.

The converse of Theorem 8.1.3 does not hold: a stationary point is not necessarily a minimizer. The stationary points in Figure 8.1.1 are of three kinds. At the two minimizers A and B, f = 0. One maximizer lies at the configuration in which the arm is straight and points away from the target; there the end-effector is farthest from the target. And there are three **saddle points**: along some directions they are lowest points, yet along other directions they are highest points, like the centre of a saddle. One of them is the configuration in which the arm is straight and points at the target, $(\theta_1, \theta_2) = (\varphi, 0)$, where φ is the bearing of the target point. In this configuration the end-effector lies on the line joining the base and the target, still $L_1 + L_2 - \|p_d\|$ away from the target. Turning joint 1 alone moves the end-effector off the line along an arc centred on the base, and the distance grows; bending joint 2 alone moves the end-effector mainly sideways, and the distance grows too. But if, while bending joint 2, joint 1 is turned back through a suitable angle, the end-effector stays roughly on the line and is drawn back along it towards the base, that is, towards the target, and the distance decreases. So along some directions it is a lowest point, yet along other directions it is a highest point. To tell these kinds of stationary point apart, second derivatives are needed.

**Definition 8.1.4 (Hessian matrix)** Suppose all the second partial derivatives of f exist. The n×n matrix

$$
\nabla^2 f(x) = \begin{pmatrix} \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1^2} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1 \partial x_n} \\ \vdots & & \vdots \\ \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n \partial x_1} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n^2} \end{pmatrix} \tag{8.1.7}
$$

is called the **Hessian matrix** of f at x, named after the German mathematician Ludwig Otto Hesse.

If the second partial derivatives are continuous, the mixed partial derivatives do not depend on the order of differentiation, $\partial^2 f/\partial x_i\partial x_j = \partial^2 f/\partial x_j\partial x_i$ (Schwarz's theorem; see any textbook of multivariable calculus), so the Hessian matrix is symmetric. Its column j is the partial derivative of the gradient with respect to $x_j$, that is, the "rate of change" of the gradient. Following Theorem 8.1.1, a second-order approximation can be obtained:

**Theorem 8.1.4 (Second-order Taylor expansion)** Suppose the second partial derivatives of f are continuous near x. Then

$$
f(x + d) = f(x) + \nabla f(x)^{\mathsf T}d + \tfrac12\, d^{\mathsf T}\nabla^2 f(x)\, d + o(\|d\|^2) . \tag{8.1.8}
$$

*Proof*　Let $\varphi(s) = f(x + sd)$, a function of the single variable s. By the chain rule, $\varphi'(s) = \nabla f(x + sd)^{\mathsf T}d$; differentiating once more, $\varphi''(s) = d^{\mathsf T}\nabla^2 f(x + sd)\,d$. By Taylor's formula for functions of one variable with the Lagrange form of the remainder, there exists $\xi \in (0, 1)$ such that $\varphi(1) = \varphi(0) + \varphi'(0) + \tfrac12\varphi''(\xi)$, that is,

$$
f(x+d) = f(x) + \nabla f(x)^{\mathsf T}d + \tfrac12 d^{\mathsf T}\nabla^2 f(x+\xi d)\,d .
$$

By the continuity of the second partial derivatives, $\nabla^2 f(x + \xi d) - \nabla^2 f(x)$ tends to the zero matrix as $d \to 0$, so $d^{\mathsf T}[\nabla^2 f(x+\xi d) - \nabla^2 f(x)]d = o(\|d\|^2)$.　∎

**Theorem 8.1.5 (Second-order conditions)** Suppose the second partial derivatives of f are continuous near $x^*$.

1. Necessary condition: if $x^*$ is a local minimizer, then $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$ and $\nabla^2 f(x^*)$ is positive semidefinite;
2. Sufficient condition: if $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$ and $\nabla^2 f(x^*)$ is positive definite, then $x^*$ is a strict local minimizer.

*Proof*　(2) Write $H = \nabla^2 f(x^*)$. By the spectral theorem of Chapter 2 (Theorem 2.3.4), $H = Q\Lambda Q^{\mathsf T}$, so $d^{\mathsf T}Hd = \sum_i \lambda_i (q_i^{\mathsf T}d)^2 \ge \lambda_{\min}\|d\|^2$, and positive definiteness means that the smallest eigenvalue $\lambda_{\min} > 0$ (Theorem 2.3.5). Substituting into Eq. (8.1.8), since $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$,

$$
f(x^* + d) - f(x^*) \ge \tfrac12\lambda_{\min}\|d\|^2 + o(\|d\|^2) ,
$$

and for $\|d\|$ small enough the right-hand side is positive, so $x^*$ is a strict local minimizer. (1) The zero gradient follows from Theorem 8.1.3. If H were not positive semidefinite, there would be a d with $d^{\mathsf T}Hd < 0$, and by Eq. (8.1.8), $f(x^* + sd) - f(x^*) = \tfrac12 s^2 d^{\mathsf T}Hd + o(s^2)$, which is negative for s small enough, contradicting the local minimum.　∎

At a stationary point, if the eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix are all positive, it is a strict local minimizer; if all negative, a strict local maximizer; if some are positive and some negative, a saddle point; if there is a zero eigenvalue, the second-order conditions cannot decide.

For the objective function of inverse kinematics, differentiate Eq. (8.1.6) once more. Taking the partial derivative of component i of $\nabla f$, $\sum_c r_c\,\partial p_c/\partial\theta_i$, with respect to $\theta_j$ (c runs over x and y), the product rule gives two terms:

$$
\nabla^2 f(\theta) = J(\theta)^{\mathsf T}J(\theta) + \sum_{c} r_c(\theta)\,\nabla^2 p_c(\theta) . \tag{8.1.9}
$$

The first term comes from the first-order change of the residual with θ, the second from the "curvature" of the end-effector position ($\nabla^2 p_c$ is the Hessian matrix of the end-effector coordinate $p_c$). At an exact inverse solution r = 0, the second term vanishes and $\nabla^2 f = J^{\mathsf T}J$. $J^{\mathsf T}J$ is always positive semidefinite, because $d^{\mathsf T}J^{\mathsf T}Jd = \|Jd\|^2 \ge 0$; when J is invertible it is positive definite. So as long as the inverse solution is not at a singular configuration (Theorem 2.1.5 of Chapter 2: $\theta_2 \ne 0, \pi$), it is a strict local minimizer. This structure will play a key role again in Section 8.5.

**Example 8.1.2** The analytic inverse solution A is $\theta^* = {{ex8_1.ts_deg}}$ (in degrees). Program 8.1.1 gives

$$
\nabla^2 f(\theta^*) = J^{\mathsf T}J = {{ex8_1.Hs}}\ \mathrm{m^2/rad^2} ,
$$

with eigenvalues $\lambda_{\min} = {{ex8_1.lmin}}$ and $\lambda_{\max} = {{ex8_1.lmax}}$ (in m²/rad²), both positive, so A is a strict local minimizer. The ratio of the two eigenvalues, $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min} = {{ex8_1.kappa:.3f}}$, is the condition number of the Hessian matrix (Definition 2.4.3 of Chapter 2). Its geometric meaning is this: near the minimizer the level curves are approximately ellipses whose axes are in the ratio $\sqrt\kappa$, and the larger κ, the longer and narrower the valley. Near A in Figure 8.1.1 the level curves are indeed long, narrow, tilted ellipses. At the saddle point where the arm is straight and points at the target, the program finds the eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix to be ${{ex8_1.lam_s}}$, one positive and one negative, in agreement with the geometric analysis above; yet its two diagonal entries are both positive, so turning either joint alone cannot decrease f, and a direction of descent can only be a combination of the two joints. This is a reminder that to decide the type of a stationary point one must look at the eigenvalues, not just at the diagonal entries.

Before going on, look at the simplest class of functions. Let Q be an n×n symmetric positive definite matrix and

$$
f(x) = \tfrac12 x^{\mathsf T}Qx - b^{\mathsf T}x , \qquad \nabla f(x) = Qx - b , \qquad \nabla^2 f(x) = Q . \tag{8.1.10}
$$

Such a function is called a quadratic function; its level curves are a family of concentric ellipses (n = 2). By Theorems 8.1.3 and 8.1.5 it has a unique minimizer $x^* = Q^{-1}b$; and since $f(x) - f(x^*) = \tfrac12(x - x^*)^{\mathsf T}Q(x - x^*) \ge 0$, this minimizer is also global. The least-squares problem of Chapter 2 is an instance: $\tfrac12\|Ax - b\|^2 = \tfrac12 x^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}Ax - (A^{\mathsf T}b)^{\mathsf T}x + \tfrac12\|b\|^2$ with $Q = A^{\mathsf T}A$, and "zero gradient" is exactly the normal equations (2.5.4). Quadratic functions are the key to understanding general functions: by Eq. (8.1.8), any smooth function is approximately quadratic near a strict local minimizer, with Q the Hessian matrix there.

### 8.1.4 Gradient descent

The simplest way down the mountain is to walk a certain distance along the steepest-descent direction at every step:

$$
x_{k+1} = x_k - \alpha\,\nabla f(x_k) , \tag{8.1.11}
$$

where α > 0 is called the **step size**. This is the method of gradient descent, proposed by Augustin-Louis Cauchy in 1847 for solving systems of equations arising in astronomy. For inverse kinematics, θ is in rad and the gradient in m²/rad, so α is in rad²/m²; how large α should be depends on the scale of the problem itself, and there is no value that suits every case.

If α is too small, each step is too short and many steps are needed; if α is too large, a step may overshoot the bottom of the valley and land on the slope opposite. To see the pattern, first analyse the quadratic function (8.1.10). Let the error be $e_k = x_k - x^*$. Since $\nabla f(x_k) = Qx_k - b = Q(x_k - x^*) = Qe_k$, Eq. (8.1.11) becomes

$$
e_{k+1} = (I - \alpha Q)\, e_k . \tag{8.1.12}
$$

**Theorem 8.1.6 (Speed of convergence of gradient descent on a quadratic function)** Let Q be symmetric positive definite with eigenvalues $0 < \lambda_1 \le \cdots \le \lambda_n$. Gradient descent with a fixed step converges to $x^*$ from every initial value if and only if $0 < \alpha < 2/\lambda_n$. In that case

$$
\|e_k\| \le \rho^k\,\|e_0\| , \qquad \rho = \max_i\,|1 - \alpha\lambda_i| < 1 , \tag{8.1.13}
$$

and ρ takes its smallest value $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$ at $\alpha = 2/(\lambda_1 + \lambda_n)$, where $\kappa = \lambda_n/\lambda_1$.

*Proof*　By the spectral theorem, $Q = V\Lambda V^{\mathsf T}$, where the columns $v_i$ of V are orthonormal eigenvectors. Decompose the error along the eigenvectors, $e_k = \sum_i c_i^{(k)} v_i$. By Eq. (8.1.12), $(I - \alpha Q)v_i = (1 - \alpha\lambda_i)v_i$, so $c_i^{(k+1)} = (1 - \alpha\lambda_i)c_i^{(k)}$: each component is multiplied by its own fixed factor. $\|e_k\|^2 = \sum_i (c_i^{(k)})^2 \le \rho^{2k}\sum_i (c_i^{(0)})^2$, which gives Eq. (8.1.13). All components tend to zero if and only if $|1 - \alpha\lambda_i| < 1$ for every i, that is, $0 < \alpha\lambda_i < 2$; the most restrictive is the largest eigenvalue, giving $\alpha < 2/\lambda_n$. Finally, $\max(|1 - \alpha\lambda_1|, |1 - \alpha\lambda_n|)$ is smallest when the two are equal, $1 - \alpha\lambda_1 = \alpha\lambda_n - 1$, that is, $\alpha = 2/(\lambda_1 + \lambda_n)$, and then $\rho = (\lambda_n - \lambda_1)/(\lambda_n + \lambda_1) = (\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1)$.　∎

This theorem explains two features of gradient descent. First, the step size has an upper limit: beyond $2/\lambda_{\max}$, the error component along the steepest direction is amplified at every step and the iteration diverges. Second, the speed of convergence is determined by the condition number: the larger κ, the closer ρ is to 1 and the slower the convergence. Intuitively, in a long, narrow valley the negative gradient is almost perpendicular to the direction in which the valley runs; it points at the opposite wall of the valley rather than at its lowest point. With large steps the iteration zigzags back and forth; with small steps it takes a long time to get to the end.

**Definition 8.1.5 (Speed of convergence)** Let $x_k \to x^*$ and $e_k = \|x_k - x^*\|$. If there is a constant $0 < \rho < 1$ such that $e_{k+1} \le \rho\, e_k$ for all sufficiently large k, the convergence is called **linear convergence**; if there is a constant C such that $e_{k+1} \le C e_k^2$, it is called **quadratic convergence**.

With linear convergence the error shrinks by a fixed factor at every step, and the number of correct digits gained per step is roughly constant; with quadratic convergence the error is roughly squared at every step, and the number of correct digits roughly doubles at every step.

For a general function, near a strict local minimizer, Eq. (8.1.8) gives $\nabla f(x) \approx \nabla^2 f(x^*)(x - x^*)$, and gradient descent behaves as on the quadratic function with $Q = \nabla^2 f(x^*)$. So the conclusions of Theorem 8.1.6 hold approximately near the minimizer.

With the optimal step $2/(\lambda_{\min} + \lambda_{\max}) = {{ex8_1.alpha_best:.3f}}$, the factor per step could be lowered to $(\kappa - 1)/(\kappa + 1) = {{ex8_1.rate_best:.4f}}$, which still takes several dozen steps. **Line search.** A fixed step requires $\lambda_{\max}$ to be known in advance, which in practice is often impossible. A more practical approach is to choose the step on the spot at every step, which is called a **line search**. The most common is the backtracking method proposed by Larry Armijo in 1966: start from a fairly large trial step $\alpha_0$, and as long as

$$
f(x_k - \alpha\nabla f_k) \le f(x_k) - c_1\,\alpha\,\|\nabla f_k\|^2 , \qquad 0 < c_1 < 1 , \tag{8.1.14}
$$

is not satisfied, halve α, until it is satisfied. Eq. (8.1.14) is called the **Armijo condition**. It requires the actual decrease to be at least $c_1$ times the decrease predicted by the linear approximation (commonly $c_1 = 10^{-4}$), which rules out steps that "overshoot the bottom of the valley". By Eq. (8.1.5), $f(x_k - \alpha\nabla f_k) = f(x_k) - \alpha\|\nabla f_k\|^2 + o(\alpha)$; since $c_1 < 1$, Eq. (8.1.14) is certainly satisfied for α small enough, so the backtracking always stops. In Example 8.1.3, gradient descent with backtracking needs no knowledge of $\lambda_{\max}$ and converges in only {{ex8_1.ka}} steps, at the cost of a few extra evaluations of f per step.

### 8.1.5 Newton's method

Gradient descent uses only first-order information: it knows which way the slope goes, but not how the slope bends ahead, and so it does not know how far away the bottom of the valley is. Eq. (8.1.8) tells us that near $x_k$, f is approximately a quadratic function:

$$
m_k(d) = f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^{\mathsf T}d + \tfrac12 d^{\mathsf T}\nabla^2 f(x_k)\,d .
$$

If $\nabla^2 f(x_k)$ is positive definite, $m_k$ has a unique minimizer, given by $\nabla m_k(d) = 0$. So we jump straight to the lowest point of this quadratic model:

$$
\nabla^2 f(x_k)\, d_k = -\nabla f(x_k) , \qquad x_{k+1} = x_k + d_k . \tag{8.1.15}
$$

This is Newton's method for optimization, and $d_k$ is called the Newton step. Figure 8.1.4, left, illustrates it with a function of one variable: replace the function by a parabola and jump to the vertex of the parabola. Eq. (8.1.15) can also be seen as applying Newton's method for nonlinear equations of Chapter 7 to the system $\nabla f(x) = 0$: the "Jacobian matrix" of this system is the Hessian matrix. Isaac Newton around 1669 and Joseph Raphson in 1690 used this iteration to find roots of polynomial equations; Thomas Simpson extended it in 1740 to systems of equations in two unknowns and pointed out that, by setting the derivatives to zero, it can be used to find extrema (see the historical study by Tjalling Ypma, 1995).

**Theorem 8.1.7 (Local quadratic convergence of Newton's method)** Suppose the second partial derivatives of f are continuous, $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$, $\nabla^2 f(x^*)$ is positive definite, and the Hessian matrix satisfies a Lipschitz condition near $x^*$: there is a constant M with $\|\nabla^2 f(x) - \nabla^2 f(y)\| \le M\|x - y\|$. Then there exists $\varepsilon > 0$ such that, as long as $\|x_0 - x^*\| < \varepsilon$, Newton's method (8.1.15) converges to $x^*$, and the convergence is quadratic.

*Proof*　Write $e_k = x_k - x^*$ and $H_k = \nabla^2 f(x_k)$. Since $\nabla f(x^*) = 0$,

$$
e_{k+1} = e_k - H_k^{-1}\big[\nabla f(x_k) - \nabla f(x^*)\big] = H_k^{-1}\Big[H_k e_k - \big(\nabla f(x_k) - \nabla f(x^*)\big)\Big] .
$$

Applying the fundamental theorem of calculus to $s \mapsto \nabla f(x^* + s e_k)$, $\nabla f(x_k) - \nabla f(x^*) = \int_0^1 \nabla^2 f(x^* + s e_k)\,e_k\, ds$. So the bracket equals $\int_0^1 [H_k - \nabla^2 f(x^* + se_k)]e_k\,ds$, and by the Lipschitz condition its norm is at most $\int_0^1 M(1 - s)\|e_k\|^2 ds = \tfrac{M}{2}\|e_k\|^2$. Since $\nabla^2 f(x^*)$ is positive definite and the Hessian matrix is continuous, $H_k$ is invertible near $x^*$ and $\|H_k^{-1}\|$ is bounded by some constant β. Therefore

$$
\|e_{k+1}\| \le \tfrac{\beta M}{2}\,\|e_k\|^2 .
$$

Choose ε so that $\tfrac{\beta M}{2}\varepsilon < 1$. Then $\|e_{k+1}\| < \|e_k\|$, the iterates always stay near $x^*$, the error decreases monotonically to zero, and the definition of quadratic convergence is satisfied.　∎

Back to Example 8.1.3. Newton's method, from the same starting point, gives errors $\|\theta_k - \theta^*\|$ that go from {{ex8_1.e0:.4f}} rad through {{ex8_1.e1:.4f}}, ${{ex8_1.e2:sci2}}$, ${{ex8_1.e3:sci2}}$ to ${{ex8_1.e4:sci1}}$ rad, reaching machine precision in 4 steps. From step 2 on, the exponent of the error roughly doubles at every step, and the ratios $e_{k+1}/e_k^2$ are {{ex8_1.ratio2:.2f}} and {{ex8_1.ratio3:.2f}}, staying around 1: this is quadratic convergence. As an independent check, Program 8.1.1 also calls the BFGS method of SciPy, which converges to the same solution in {{ex8_1.bfgs_nit}} steps. BFGS is a **quasi-Newton method**: it does not compute the Hessian matrix, but builds up an approximation to it step by step from the differences between the gradients at successive steps. It was proposed independently in 1970 by Charles George Broyden, Roger Fletcher, Donald Goldfarb and David F. Shanno, and is today one of the most widely used algorithms for unconstrained optimization.

The price of Newton's method is that every step requires computing the Hessian matrix and solving an n×n system of linear equations. For an arm with no more than 7 joints this is no burden; for problems with thousands of variables (the trajectory optimization of Chapter 53), quasi-Newton methods or the sparse structure of the matrices must be used. More importantly, Theorem 8.1.7 guarantees convergence only near a minimizer. Far from it, the Hessian matrix may not be positive definite, the quadratic model $m_k$ has no lowest point, and the Newton step may point uphill or lead the iteration to a saddle point.

**Example 8.1.4** Starting from $\theta = (0, 0.3)^{\mathsf T}$ rad (the arm nearly straight), the eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix are ${{ex8_1.lam_b}}$, one positive and one negative: it is indefinite. Pure Newton's method converges in {{ex8_1.ks}} steps to $\theta = {{ex8_1.xs_deg}}$ (in degrees). The gradient is indeed zero, but this is the saddle point in which the arm is straight and points at the target: the end-effector is still {{ex8_1.dist_s:.4f}} m from the target, exactly $L_1 + L_2 - \|p_d\|$. Newton's method only looks for a point of zero gradient and cannot tell minimizers, maximizers and saddle points apart.

There are two common remedies. The first is to modify the Hessian matrix: replace its negative eigenvalues by their absolute values (and give the eigenvalues a lower bound) to obtain a positive definite matrix, then solve Eq. (8.1.15). The d computed in this way satisfies $\nabla f^{\mathsf T}d < 0$ and is always a descent direction. The second is to combine it with a line search: backtrack along d until a condition like Eq. (8.1.14) is satisfied. Newton's method with these two safeguards, as in Program 8.1.1, converges from the same starting point to the minimizer A in {{ex8_1.kg}} steps (Figure 8.1.4, right). Once the iteration is near the minimizer, the Hessian matrix is positive definite by itself, the step length 1 is accepted, and quadratic convergence is restored.

### 8.1.6 Everyday examples

Walking down a mountain in thick fog, you cannot see the valley; you can only feel which direction underfoot is steepest and walk some way in that direction. This is gradient descent. If the valley is narrow and long, the steepest direction is almost perpendicular to the direction in which the valley runs, and you walk from one wall of the valley to the other, zigzagging and making slow progress: this is the case of a large condition number. If you could see how the small patch of ground underfoot is curved, you could estimate roughly how far away the bottom of the valley is and get there in one stride: this is the idea of Newton's method.

Adjusting the water temperature of a shower is also gradient descent. The water is a little cold, so you turn the valve towards hot; turn it too far and the water is too hot, so you turn it back. How far you turn it each time corresponds to the step size: too small, and you wait a long time before it is right; too large, and it swings between hot and cold and never settles. The statement of Theorem 8.1.6 that "the iteration diverges when the step exceeds 2/λ_max" shows up here as the water getting hotter and colder the more you adjust it.

There is one feature of inverse kinematics we have not used yet: the joint angles are often limited, and the end-effector may also have to stay on some surface. The next section discusses optimization with equality constraints; there a zero gradient is no longer the condition for optimality, and its place is taken by Lagrange multipliers.

**Engineer's notes**

- The stopping criterion of an iterative method should look at several quantities together: the norm of the gradient, the change in x between consecutive steps, the decrease of the objective function, and a maximum number of iterations. Looking only at the gradient, the iteration stops too early in flat regions; looking only at the change in x, a very small step can be mistaken for convergence.
- In inverse kinematics all joint angles have the same unit (rad), but if the components of the objective function mix position (m) and orientation (rad), the weights must be chosen first; otherwise the gradient direction is dominated by whichever kind of error is numerically larger.
- The step size of gradient descent depends on the scale of the problem. If the units of the same arm are changed from metres to millimetres, f is multiplied by 10⁶ and the suitable step size is divided by 10⁶. A line search removes the need to tune the step size by hand.
- Newton's method requires the Hessian matrix to be positive definite. When the objective function is a sum of squares, the $J^{\mathsf T}J$ part is always positive semidefinite; the Gauss–Newton method of Section 8.5 exploits exactly this.
- Which solution an iterative method converges to depends on the initial value. In robot control the current joint angles are usually taken as the initial value, so that the solution obtained is the one closest to the current configuration, which keeps the arm from suddenly flipping over to another solution.
- Gradients and Hessian matrices you have written yourself must always be checked with difference quotients. Take the difference step around $10^{-6}$ (relative to the size of the variable); central difference quotients are much more accurate than one-sided ones.
