### 8.2.1 The problem: an arm held by a hand

Imagine a planar 3R arm (with the same dimensions as in Section 12.1: $L_1 = 0.425$ m, $L_2 = 0.392$ m, $L_3 = 0.1$ m), with a torsion spring fitted in each of its three joints. With no external force the springs are undeformed, and the arm rests in its natural configuration $\theta_c = (30°, 60°, -60°)$. Now grab the end-effector by hand, pull it to the target point $p_d = (0.55, 0.45)$ m and hold it there. Question: what configuration does the arm take? How large a force must the hand exert?

With three joints and two coordinates of the end-effector, once the end-effector position is fixed the arm still has one degree of freedom left and can take infinitely many configurations. The real arm takes only one of them: the one in which the elastic potential energy stored in the springs is least. This is the principle of minimum potential energy of statics: among all configurations allowed by the constraints, a stable equilibrium configuration minimizes the potential energy. Let the stiffness of the i-th spring be $k_i$ (in N·m/rad) and write $K = \mathrm{diag}(k_1, k_2, k_3)$. The potential energy of the springs is

$$
U(\theta) = \tfrac12\,(\theta - \theta_c)^{\mathsf T}K(\theta - \theta_c) .
$$

The problem thus becomes: among all θ satisfying $p(\theta) = p_d$, find the one that makes U smallest.

The same mathematical form is found everywhere in robotics. The inverse kinematics of a redundant arm has to pick one set out of infinitely many solutions; in engineering one often picks the set "closest to some comfortable configuration", in which case $\theta_c$ is the comfortable configuration and K holds the weights of the joints. Trajectory optimization has to pick, among all motions that satisfy the start point, the end point and the equations of dynamics, the one that uses least energy. In general such problems are written

$$
\min_{x \in \mathbb R^n}\ f(x) \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad h(x) = 0 , \tag{8.2.1}
$$

where s.t. is short for "subject to"; $h: \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m$ consists of m functions $h_1, \ldots, h_m$, and each $h_i(x) = 0$ is called an **equality constraint** (a **constraint** being a restriction on the decision variables). The set of x satisfying all the constraints is called the **feasible set**. For the spring arm, n = 3, m = 2 and $h(\theta) = p(\theta) - p_d$.

The methods of Section 8.1 cannot be used here directly: the point where $\nabla U = 0$ is $\theta_c$ itself, which does not satisfy the constraint. A naive idea is to use the constraints first to eliminate some of the variables and turn the problem into an unconstrained one. This works for simple problems but is hard to do for the arm: solving $p(\theta) = p_d$ for two joint angles as functions of the third is itself an inverse kinematics problem. We need a method that does not require elimination. To see the idea behind it, first look at a simple example in which elimination is possible.

### 8.2.2 Geometric intuition: two parallel gradients

A rectangular vegetable garden is to be fenced against a wall with 20 m of fence, no fence being needed on the side of the wall (Figure 8.2.1, left). Let the two sides perpendicular to the wall have length x and the side parallel to the wall length y (in m). How should the garden be fenced to make its area largest? This is

$$
\max\ S(x, y) = xy \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad h(x, y) = 2x + y - 20 = 0 . \tag{8.2.2}
$$

By elimination: from the constraint, y = 20 − 2x, so $S = x(20 - 2x)$; setting the derivative 20 − 4x = 0 gives x = 5 m, y = 10 m and S = 50 m².

Now look at it another way. Figure 8.2.1, right, shows the level curves of the area S (the hyperbolas xy = constant) and the constraint line. Walking along the line, we cross one level curve after another; at a certain point the line just touches a level curve, and going further we cross back into lower level curves. The point of tangency is the maximizer under the constraint. At a point where they are not tangent (such as (2, 16) in the figure), the line crosses the level curve, and walking along the line in one of the two directions still increases S, so it is not the optimum.

"Tangency" can be expressed through gradients. By Theorem 8.1.2 the gradient is perpendicular to the level curves; likewise, the gradient of the constraint function, $\nabla h = (2, 1)^{\mathsf T}$, is perpendicular to the constraint line (the line is the level curve h = 0 of h). When two curves are tangent their normals are parallel, so at the optimum

$$
\nabla S = \lambda\,\nabla h , \qquad \text{i.e.}\quad (y, x) = \lambda\,(2, 1)
$$

holds for some number λ. From this, y = 2λ and x = λ; substituting into the constraint 2x + y = 20 gives λ = 5, x = 5 and y = 10, the same as by elimination. This number λ is the Lagrange multiplier. It looks like a mere auxiliary unknown, but Section 8.2.4 will show that it has a definite meaning.

Now extend this intuition to n dimensions. Let $x^*$ be a local minimizer of problem (8.2.1). If we move from $x^*$ in a direction d without (to first order) violating the constraints, d must satisfy $\nabla h_i(x^*)^{\mathsf T}d = 0$ (i = 1, …, m), that is, d must be perpendicular to the gradient of every constraint. Such directions are called tangent directions of the feasible set at $x^*$. Moving along a tangent direction, f cannot decrease to first order, or else $x^*$ would not be a minimizer; the same holds with d replaced by −d, so

$$
\nabla f(x^*)^{\mathsf T}d = 0 \quad \text{for all}\ d\ \text{satisfying}\ \nabla h_i(x^*)^{\mathsf T}d = 0\ (i = 1, \ldots, m) . \tag{8.2.3}
$$

In other words, $\nabla f(x^*)$ is perpendicular to all tangent directions and can only lie in the subspace spanned by the $\nabla h_i(x^*)$. Below we make this argument rigorous.

### 8.2.3 The Lagrange multiplier theorem

Write the Jacobian matrix of the constraints as $A(x) = \partial h/\partial x$; it is an m×n matrix whose row i is $\nabla h_i(x)^{\mathsf T}$. The tangent directions in Eq. (8.2.3) are exactly the null space $N(A)$ of A (Eq. (2.6.3) of Chapter 2).

**Definition 8.2.1 (Lagrangian)** For problem (8.2.1), the function

$$
L(x, \lambda) = f(x) + \lambda^{\mathsf T}h(x) = f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_i h_i(x) \tag{8.2.4}
$$

is called the **Lagrangian**, and $\lambda = (\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_m)^{\mathsf T}$ are called the **Lagrange multipliers**.

**Definition 8.2.2 (Regular point)** If $\nabla h_1(x), \ldots, \nabla h_m(x)$ are linearly independent, that is, the rows of A(x) are linearly independent (its rank is m), x is called a **regular point** of the constraints h(x) = 0.

**Theorem 8.2.1 (Lagrange multiplier theorem)** Suppose the partial derivatives of f and h are continuous, and $x^*$ is a local minimizer of problem (8.2.1) and a regular point. Then there exists a unique $\lambda^* \in \mathbb R^m$ such that

$$
\nabla_x L(x^*, \lambda^*) = \nabla f(x^*) + A(x^*)^{\mathsf T}\lambda^* = 0 , \qquad \nabla_\lambda L(x^*, \lambda^*) = h(x^*) = 0 . \tag{8.2.5}
$$

Eq. (8.2.5) consists of n + m equations, and the unknowns x and λ also number n + m. The original constrained problem has become the problem of finding a stationary point of the Lagrangian.

*Proof*　First consider the case of linear constraints, h(x) = Ax − b with A a constant matrix. Then for any $d \in N(A)$, $x^* + sd$ is feasible for every s. The function of one variable $\varphi(s) = f(x^* + sd)$ has a local minimum at s = 0, so $\varphi'(0) = \nabla f(x^*)^{\mathsf T}d = 0$. This shows that $\nabla f(x^*)$ is orthogonal to N(A). By Theorem 2.6.3 of Chapter 2, the vectors in $\mathbb R^n$ orthogonal to the null space all lie in the row space $R(A^{\mathsf T})$, so there is a μ with $\nabla f(x^*) = A^{\mathsf T}\mu$, and taking $\lambda^* = -\mu$ gives Eq. (8.2.5). Since the rows of A are linearly independent, $A^{\mathsf T}\lambda = 0$ has only the zero solution, so $\lambda^*$ is unique.

When the constraints are nonlinear, $x^* + sd$ is in general no longer feasible, and the straight line has to be replaced by a curved feasible curve. The fact needed is the following lemma: at a regular point, for every $d \in N(A(x^*))$ there is a feasible curve $x(s)$ ($h(x(s)) = 0$ for s near 0) with $x(0) = x^*$ and $x'(0) = d$. With it, $\varphi(s) = f(x(s))$ has a local minimum at s = 0, $\varphi'(0) = \nabla f(x^*)^{\mathsf T}d = 0$, and the rest is the same as in the linear case.

The proof of the lemma uses the implicit function theorem. Write $A_* = A(x^*)$ and let $x(s) = x^* + sd + A_*^{\mathsf T}y(s)$, where $y(s) \in \mathbb R^m$ is to be determined, requiring $\Phi(s, y) = h(x^* + sd + A_*^{\mathsf T}y) = 0$. At (s, y) = (0, 0), Φ = 0, and the Jacobian matrix of Φ with respect to y is $A_*A_*^{\mathsf T}$; since the rows of $A_*$ are linearly independent, this m×m matrix is invertible (proof of Theorem 2.5.3 of Chapter 2). The implicit function theorem guarantees that near s = 0 there is a continuously differentiable y(s) with y(0) = 0 and $\Phi(s, y(s)) = 0$. Differentiating the identity, $A_*(d + A_*^{\mathsf T}y'(0)) = 0$; since $A_*d = 0$, this gives $A_*A_*^{\mathsf T}y'(0) = 0$, so y'(0) = 0 and $x'(0) = d$.　∎

The regularity condition cannot be dropped. For example, minimize $f = x_1$ in the plane subject to the constraint $h = x_1^2 + x_2^2 = 0$. The feasible set consists of the origin alone, which is of course the minimizer; but $\nabla h(0) = 0$, the origin is not a regular point, and $\nabla f = (1, 0)^{\mathsf T}$ cannot be written as $-\lambda\nabla h$. Conditions of this kind, which guarantee the existence of multipliers, are called **constraint qualifications**; regularity (linear independence of the constraint gradients) is the one most often used.

Theorem 8.2.1 is only a necessary condition: a point satisfying Eq. (8.2.5) may be a minimizer, but it may also be a maximizer or a saddle point, just like a stationary point in an unconstrained problem. Section 8.2.6 gives second-order conditions.

In his *Méchanique analitique* (Analytical mechanics) of 1788, Joseph-Louis Lagrange introduced this device of "multiplying by undetermined coefficients and adding" to deal with problems of mechanical equilibrium with constraints; in 1797, in his *Théorie des fonctions analytiques* (Theory of analytic functions), he stated it as a general method for finding conditional extrema: "it suffices to add to the function whose extremum is sought the functions that must be zero, each multiplied by an undetermined quantity." For the garden problem, take f = −S:

$$
L = -xy + \lambda\,(2x + y - 20), \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = -y + 2\lambda = 0, \quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} = -x + \lambda = 0, \quad 2x + y = 20 , \tag{8.2.6}
$$

whose solution is x = {{ex8_2.x_g:.0f}} m, y = {{ex8_2.y_g:.0f}} m and λ = {{ex8_2.lam_g:.0f}}, the same as before.

### 8.2.4 What a multiplier means: how much the optimal value changes when a constraint is relaxed a little

Replace the constraint of problem (8.2.1) by h(x) = c, where c is a constant vector that can be adjusted. The optimum and the optimal value both change with c; write them $x^*(c)$ and $f^*(c) = f(x^*(c))$.

**Theorem 8.2.2 (Sensitivity)** Suppose that, for c near 0, the local minimizer $x^*(c)$ of the problem $\min f(x)$ s.t. $h(x) = c$ is a regular point with multiplier $\lambda^*(c)$, and that $x^*(c)$ is continuously differentiable in c. Then

$$
\frac{\partial f^*}{\partial c} = -\lambda^{*\mathsf T} , \qquad \text{i.e.}\quad \frac{\partial f^*}{\partial c_i} = -\lambda_i^* . \tag{8.2.7}
$$

*Proof*　By the chain rule, $\partial f^*/\partial c = \nabla f(x^*)^{\mathsf T}\,\partial x^*/\partial c$. By Eq. (8.2.5), $\nabla f(x^*)^{\mathsf T} = -\lambda^{*\mathsf T}A(x^*)$. Differentiating the identity $h(x^*(c)) = c$ gives $A(x^*)\,\partial x^*/\partial c = I$. Multiplying the two gives Eq. (8.2.7).　∎

(With the Lagrangian written as $f + \lambda^{\mathsf T}(h - c)$, λ has the sign used here; some books write $f - \lambda^{\mathsf T}h$, and the multipliers then differ by a minus sign. When reading the literature, check the convention first.)

A multiplier is the rate of change of the optimal value with respect to the constraint: relax the constraint by one unit, and the optimal value changes by about $-\lambda$. For the garden, f = −S and c is the total length of the fence, so by Eq. (8.2.7), $dS^*/dc = \lambda = 5$ m²/m: 1 m more fence gives about 5 m² more area. Let us verify: the optimal area is $S^* = c^2/8$, whose derivative c/4 equals 5 at c = 20; Program 8.2.1 lengthens the fence by 1 cm, solves again, and obtains the difference quotient {{ex8_2.dS_dL:.4f}} m²/m. In economics a multiplier is called the "shadow price" of the constrained resource; in mechanics, as we shall see below, it is the constraint force.

### 8.2.5 The quadratic problem with equality constraints: a system of linear equations

When the objective function is quadratic and the constraints are linear equations, Eq. (8.2.5) becomes a system of linear equations. Let

$$
\min_x\ \tfrac12 x^{\mathsf T}Qx + c^{\mathsf T}x \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad Ax = b ,
$$

with Q an n×n symmetric matrix and A an m×n matrix. Eq. (8.2.5) reads $Qx + c + A^{\mathsf T}\lambda = 0$ and $Ax = b$, which together are

$$
\begin{pmatrix} Q & A^{\mathsf T} \\ A & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ \lambda \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -c \\ b \end{pmatrix} . \tag{8.2.8}
$$

The (n+m)×(n+m) matrix on the left is called the KKT matrix (KKT is an abbreviation of the surnames of three scholars; see Section 8.3).

**Theorem 8.2.3** Suppose the rows of A are linearly independent and Q is positive definite on the null space of A, that is, $z^{\mathsf T}Qz > 0$ for all $z \ne 0$ with Az = 0. Then the KKT matrix is invertible, and the solution x of Eq. (8.2.8) is the unique global minimizer of the problem.

*Proof*　Suppose the KKT matrix maps (z, μ) to zero: Qz + Aᵀμ = 0 and Az = 0. Multiplying the first equation on the left by $z^{\mathsf T}$, $z^{\mathsf T}Qz = -(Az)^{\mathsf T}\mu = 0$; by the assumption, z = 0; then $A^{\mathsf T}\mu = 0$, and by the linear independence of the rows, μ = 0. So the KKT matrix is invertible. Let (x*, λ*) be the solution, take any feasible point x and write $z = x - x^*$. Then Az = 0 and

$$
f(x) - f(x^*) = (Qx^* + c)^{\mathsf T}z + \tfrac12 z^{\mathsf T}Qz = -\lambda^{*\mathsf T}Az + \tfrac12 z^{\mathsf T}Qz = \tfrac12 z^{\mathsf T}Qz ,
$$

which is positive for z ≠ 0.　∎

Note that the theorem only requires Q to be positive definite on the null space; Q itself need not be positive definite, and may be zero or even negative in some directions. This point will come in useful in Section 8.2.6.

**Example 8.2.1** The planar 3R arm at $\theta_c = (30°, 60°, -60°)$ is required to move its end-effector with velocity $v = (0.1, 0)$ m/s. The three joint velocities $\dot\theta$ satisfy $J\dot\theta = v$: two equations in three unknowns. Example 2.5.2 of Chapter 2 chose the solution of smallest length; now we weight the joints and choose the solution that minimizes $\tfrac12\dot\theta^{\mathsf T}W\dot\theta$, where W is a diagonal positive definite weight matrix and joints with large weights move less. This is the case Q = W, c = 0, A = J, b = v of Eq. (8.2.8), and it can be solved directly: the first row, $W\dot\theta + J^{\mathsf T}\lambda = 0$, gives $\dot\theta = -W^{-1}J^{\mathsf T}\lambda$; substituting into $J\dot\theta = v$ gives $\lambda = -(JW^{-1}J^{\mathsf T})^{-1}v$, and so

$$
\dot\theta = W^{-1}J^{\mathsf T}\,(JW^{-1}J^{\mathsf T})^{-1}\,v . \tag{8.2.9}
$$

For W = I this is the minimum-norm solution (2.5.10) of Chapter 2. Program 8.2.1 gives

$$
J = {{ex8_2.J}}\ \mathrm{m}, \qquad \dot\theta\big|_{W = I} = {{ex8_2.thd_I}}, \qquad \dot\theta\big|_{W = \mathrm{diag}(20, 10, 5)} = {{ex8_2.thd_W}}\ \mathrm{rad/s} .
$$

With the weights, joint 1, which has the largest weight, turns more slowly, and joint 3, which has the smallest, turns faster. The weighted cost $\tfrac12\dot\theta^{\mathsf T}W\dot\theta$ falls from {{ex8_2.cost_I:.4f}} to {{ex8_2.cost_W:.4f}}. The program also solves the 5×5 KKT system (8.2.8) directly; the result is the same as from Eq. (8.2.9), and the multiplier is λ = ${{ex8_2.lam_v}}$.

### 8.2.6 Nonlinear constraints: the Lagrange–Newton method and second-order conditions

Back to the spring arm. Eq. (8.2.5) is a system of nonlinear equations in (θ, λ), which can be solved by Newton's method (Chapter 7). Writing the system as $F(x, \lambda) = 0$ and differentiating with respect to x and λ gives the system of linear equations to be solved at every Newton step:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \nabla_{xx}^2 L(x_k, \lambda_k) & A(x_k)^{\mathsf T} \\ A(x_k) & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \Delta x \\ \Delta\lambda \end{pmatrix} = -\begin{pmatrix} \nabla f(x_k) + A(x_k)^{\mathsf T}\lambda_k \\ h(x_k) \end{pmatrix} , \tag{8.2.10}
$$

where $\nabla_{xx}^2 L = \nabla^2 f + \sum_i\lambda_i\nabla^2 h_i$ is the Hessian matrix of the Lagrangian with respect to x. Then set $x_{k+1} = x_k + \Delta x$ and $\lambda_{k+1} = \lambda_k + \Delta\lambda$. This is called the Lagrange–Newton method. Eq. (8.2.10) has exactly the same form as Eq. (8.2.8): every step solves a quadratic problem with equality constraints, whose quadratic term is $\nabla_{xx}^2 L$ and whose linear constraints result from linearizing h at $x_k$. This observation is the starting point of the sequential quadratic programming of Section 8.3.

The Lagrange conditions are only necessary conditions. To decide whether a solution is a minimizer, second-order information is needed. The obvious idea is to check whether $\nabla_{xx}^2 L$ is positive definite, but that requirement is too strong. We saw in Section 8.2.2 that under the constraints one can move only along tangent directions; it is enough for the function to "curve upwards" along the tangent directions.

**Theorem 8.2.4 (Second-order sufficient condition)** Suppose the second partial derivatives of f and h are continuous, $(x^*, \lambda^*)$ satisfies Eq. (8.2.5), and

$$
z^{\mathsf T}\,\nabla_{xx}^2 L(x^*, \lambda^*)\,z > 0 \qquad \text{for all}\ z \ne 0,\ A(x^*)z = 0 . \tag{8.2.11}
$$

Then $x^*$ is a strict local minimizer of problem (8.2.1).

*Outline of proof*　For a feasible point x, h(x) = 0, so $f(x) = L(x, \lambda^*)$. The gradient of L with respect to x is zero at $x^*$, so by the second-order Taylor expansion (8.1.8), $f(x) - f(x^*) = \tfrac12 d^{\mathsf T}\nabla_{xx}^2 L\, d + o(\|d\|^2)$ with $d = x - x^*$. For feasible points nearby, the angle between d and the tangent space N(A) tends to zero (by the first-order expansion of h, $\|Ad\| = o(\|d\|)$), while on the tangent space the quadratic form is strictly positive; an argument by contradiction using compactness shows that the right-hand side is positive for $\|d\|$ small enough. For a complete proof, see the treatment of second-order conditions in *Numerical Optimization* by Jorge Nocedal and Stephen J. Wright (chapter references).　∎

In practical computation, take a basis of the null space of A as the columns of a matrix N (obtained, for example, from the singular value decomposition, Theorem 2.6.3 of Chapter 2); condition (8.2.11) is then that $N^{\mathsf T}\nabla_{xx}^2 L\,N$ be positive definite, which is called positive definiteness of the reduced Hessian matrix.

**Example 8.2.2** The spring arm: $K = \mathrm{diag}(20, 10, 5)$ N·m/rad and $\theta_c = (30°, 60°, -60°)$, at which the end-effector is at ${{ex8_2.pc}}$ m; the target is $p_d = {{ex8_2.pd}}$ m. The problem is

$$
\min_\theta\ \tfrac12(\theta - \theta_c)^{\mathsf T}K(\theta - \theta_c) \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad p(\theta) - p_d = 0 . \tag{8.2.12}
$$

Here $\nabla f = K(\theta - \theta_c)$, A = J(θ), and the Hessian matrices of the constraints, $\nabla^2 p_x$ and $\nabla^2 p_y$, are the same as in Eq. (8.1.9). Starting from $\theta_0 = \theta_c$, $\lambda_0 = 0$ and iterating with the Lagrange–Newton method (8.2.10), the norm of the difference between the two sides of Eq. (8.2.5) is successively {{ex8_2.r1:.3f}}, {{ex8_2.r2:.3f}}, {{ex8_2.r3:.4f}} and ${{ex8_2.r4:sci2}}$; machine precision is reached in {{ex8_2.it_ln}} steps in all, with quadratic convergence. The result is

$$
\theta^* = {{ex8_2.th_deg}}\ (°), \qquad \lambda^* = {{ex8_2.lam}}\ \mathrm{N}, \qquad U^* = {{ex8_2.fstar:.4f}}\ \mathrm{J} .
$$

The unit of the multipliers is N: U is in J = N·m, and the constraints are in m. Their meaning is given by Theorem 8.2.2: move the target point by δ, and the potential energy increases by about $-\lambda^{*\mathsf T}\delta$. On the other hand, the work done by the hand in moving the end-effector by δ is $F^{\mathsf T}\delta$, where F is the force the hand applies to the end-effector, and all this work becomes potential energy of the springs. Comparing the two,

$$
F = -\lambda^* = {{ex8_2.F}}\ \mathrm{N} .
$$

Program 8.2.1 moves $p_d$ by 1 μm along x and along y, solves again, and from the difference quotients obtains $\partial U^*/\partial p_d = {{ex8_2.grad_fstar}}$ N, in agreement with −λ*. Now look at the first equation of Eq. (8.2.5): $K(\theta^* - \theta_c) + J^{\mathsf T}\lambda^* = 0$. Writing the torques exerted by the springs on the joints as $\tau = -K(\theta^* - \theta_c) = {{ex8_2.tau}}$ N·m, this equation is

$$
\tau + J^{\mathsf T}F = 0 , \tag{8.2.13}
$$

that is, the spring torques balance the torques $J^{\mathsf T}F$ that the external force on the end-effector produces at the joints. This is exactly the basic relation of the statics of an arm (Chapter 16), obtained here naturally from the Lagrange conditions. Constraint forces like these were precisely what Lagrange introduced the multipliers for. Figure 8.2.2 shows the equilibrium configuration, the force of the hand and the torques of the three springs.

The direction of the force deserves a comment. The end-effector moves from ${{ex8_2.pc}}$ to ${{ex8_2.pd}}$, that is, down and to the right, yet the force of the hand points down and to the left. This is no contradiction: how readily the end-effector "gives way" to an external force differs from direction to direction. Look first at small displacements. By Eq. (8.2.13), the change in the spring torques, $-K\delta\theta$, cancels $J^{\mathsf T}F$, so $\delta\theta = K^{-1}J^{\mathsf T}F$ and the end-effector displacement is $\delta p \approx J\delta\theta = JK^{-1}J^{\mathsf T}F$. Applying a force of 1 N vertically downwards to the end-effector in the natural configuration $\theta_c$, Program 8.2.1 finds that the end-effector moves by ${{ex8_2.comp_down}}$ mm: while going down, the end-effector moves noticeably to the right, and the rightward component is even larger than the downward one. So to make the end-effector move mainly downwards and only slightly to the right, the force of the hand must have a component to the left. In this example the end-effector moves by {{ex8_2.disp_norm:.2f}} m and the configuration of the arm changes a great deal, so force and displacement no longer obey this linear relation, but it remains true that "the direction of the displacement is in general different from the direction of the force". This is a basic fact in the impedance control of Chapter 45.

Finally, check the second-order condition. The program finds the smallest eigenvalue of $\nabla_{xx}^2 L(\theta^*, \lambda^*)$ to be ${{ex8_2.lHmin:.3f}}$, so it is not positive definite; but the null space of J is one-dimensional, and the reduced Hessian matrix is a single number, {{ex8_2.red:.4f}} > 0, which satisfies Theorem 8.2.4: θ* is a strict local minimizer, that is, a stable equilibrium. Looking only at $\nabla_{xx}^2 L$ itself would lead to the wrong verdict. As an independent check, the trust-constr method of SciPy finds a solution that differs from the one above by ${{ex8_2.scipy_err:sci1}}$ rad, with multipliers ${{ex8_2.scipy_lam}}$ N (SciPy uses the same sign convention for multipliers as this book).

### 8.2.7 An everyday example

The two ends of a clothes line are tied to two hooks, and a hanger hung on the line can slide freely. Where does the hanger finally stop? It stops at the lowest point the line allows: under the constraint "the lengths of the two parts of the line add up to the length of the line", it minimizes the gravitational potential energy of the clothes.

**Example 8.2.3** Let the hooks A and B be at (0, 2.0) m and (2.0, 2.4) m, the length of the line ℓ = 2.6 m and the weight of the clothes G = 20 N. The decision variable is the position P of the hanger, the objective function is the potential energy $G\,P_y$, and the constraint is $h(P) = \|P - A\| + \|P - B\| - \ell = 0$. The gradient of the constraint is $\nabla h = u_A + u_B$, where $u_A$ and $u_B$ are the unit vectors pointing from A and B to P, that is, the directions of the two parts of the line. The Lagrange condition is

$$
G\,(0, 1)^{\mathsf T} + \lambda\,(u_A + u_B) = 0 .
$$

Its horizontal component says that the two parts of the line make equal angles with the vertical; its vertical component says that $2\lambda\cos\alpha = G$. This is exactly the balance of forces on the hanger: each part of the line pulls on the hanger with tension λ, balancing the weight. The multiplier is the tension of the line. Geometrically, $\sin\alpha$ equals the horizontal distance between the hooks divided by the length of the line, so α = {{ex8_2.alpha_deg:.1f}}°; Program 8.2.1, using the Lagrange–Newton method, finds that the hanger stops at ${{ex8_2.P_h}}$ m with multiplier λ = {{ex8_2.T_rope:.2f}} N, the same as $G/(2\cos\alpha)$. The shorter the line, the tighter it is: sin α = (2.0 m)/ℓ grows, α grows, and the tension grows. When the two hooks are at the same height, in the limit where the line is pulled straight α → 90° and the tension tends to infinity: a clothes line pulled too taut is easily broken by heavy wet clothes. (When the hooks are at different heights, ℓ cannot be less than the distance between the hooks, and sin α cannot reach 1; when the line is pulled as tight as possible, the hanger slides to the lower hook.)

So far all the constraints have been equalities. The joints of an arm have limits, speeds and torques have upper bounds, obstacles must not be hit: these are all inequality constraints. The next section begins the discussion with the most important class — quadratic objective functions with linear inequality constraints.

**Engineer's notes**

- Before writing down the Lagrangian, fix the sign convention of the multipliers ($f + \lambda^{\mathsf T}h$ or $f - \lambda^{\mathsf T}h$). When the multipliers are to be used as forces or as sensitivities, a wrong sign reverses the conclusion.
- Write the constraints in a "good" form as far as possible: independent of one another, with non-zero gradients. Writing the same constraint twice (for example, constraining both the end-effector position and twice the end-effector position) makes the rows of A linearly dependent, the multipliers non-unique and the KKT matrix singular.
- When solving the KKT system (8.2.8), do not invert; use an LU factorization or a specialized symmetric indefinite factorization (such as LDLᵀ). The KKT matrix is always indefinite, so the Cholesky factorization cannot be used.
- The units of the constraints and of the objective function together determine the unit of the multipliers. A position constraint (m) with an energy (J) gives a force (N); an angle constraint (rad) with an energy gives a torque (N·m). Using units to check the physical meaning of a multiplier is a good way to discover mistakes in a derivation.
- In a robot controller, a weighted minimum-norm solution like Eq. (8.2.9) has to be computed once every control cycle. W is often taken as the joint inertia or as a measure of how close the joint is to its limit: the closer to the limit, the larger the weight and the less that joint moves (Chapter 17).
