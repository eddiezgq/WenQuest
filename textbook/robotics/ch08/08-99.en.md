title: Chapter Summary

The main thread of this chapter is: **write "the best" as an objective function and the limits as constraints, then design algorithms starting from the conditions that an optimal point must satisfy.**

Without constraints, the gradient at an optimal point is zero and the Hessian matrix is positive semidefinite; a point where the gradient is zero and the Hessian matrix is positive definite is a strict local minimizer. Gradient descent moves in the steepest direction and uses only first-order information; it zigzags back and forth in long narrow valleys, its speed of convergence is decided by the condition number of the Hessian matrix, and it diverges once the step size exceeds $2/\lambda_{\max}$. Newton's method replaces the function by a quadratic model and converges quadratically near a minimizer, but it only looks for points where the gradient is zero; far from the solution, the Hessian matrix must be modified and combined with a line search.

With equality constraints, the gradient of the objective function at an optimal point lies in the subspace spanned by the gradients of the constraints, and the Lagrange multipliers are the coefficients of the combination. A multiplier is the rate of change of the optimal value with respect to the constraint: for the arm with torsion springs, the multiplier is the force applied by the hand; for the clothes line, it is the tension in the line; and the Lagrange conditions yield the static equilibrium $\tau + J^{\mathsf T}F = 0$ quite naturally. The second-order condition needs to be checked only on the tangent space of the constraints.

With inequality constraints, an optimal point satisfies the KKT conditions: stationarity, feasibility, non-negative multipliers and complementary slackness. The multiplier of an inactive constraint is zero, that of an active constraint is non-negative, and the negative gradient can only "push against" the boundary along its outward normal. Quadratic programs can be solved exactly in a finite number of steps by the active-set method; linearizing a nonlinear problem into a quadratic program at every step gives sequential quadratic programming. The multiplier of a joint limit is the torque exerted by the mechanical stop, and the multiplier of a speed limit tells us how much energy can be saved by relaxing the limit.

For a convex problem, a local optimum is a global optimum; the KKT conditions are always sufficient for a convex problem, and under Slater's condition they are also necessary. A convex set can be separated from a point outside it by a hyperplane, and from this we proved Farkas' lemma; weak duality gives a lower bound on the optimal value, and the duality gap measures the quality of a solution. The barrier method approaches the optimal solution along the central path, with a duality gap of at most m/t. Once a non-convex free region has been split into convex regions, path planning becomes a convex problem, and the very definition of a convex set guarantees that the segment between neighbouring waypoints does not hit an obstacle.

When the objective function is a sum of squares of residuals, the Gauss–Newton method uses $J^{\mathsf T}J$ in place of the Hessian matrix and solves one linear least squares problem per step; on zero-residual problems it is almost as fast as Newton's method. The LM method adds damping, which is equivalent to computing the step within a trust region; the damping parameter μ is precisely the multiplier of the trust-region constraint. It moves continuously between gradient descent and the Gauss–Newton method and stays robust near singular configurations. Kinematic calibration improved the absolute accuracy of the robot by about two orders of magnitude, and after calibration the residual should be comparable with the measurement noise.

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| Gradient | Points in the direction in which the function increases fastest, perpendicular to the level curves |
| Hessian matrix | The symmetric matrix of second partial derivatives; it describes how the function curves |
| Saddle point | The gradient is zero, but the point is lowest along some directions and highest along others |
| Condition number | The ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue of the Hessian matrix; it decides how fast gradient descent is |
| Quadratic convergence | The error is roughly squared at every step, so the number of correct digits doubles at every step |
| Lagrange multiplier | The rate of change of the optimal value with respect to the constraint; in mechanics, the constraint force |
| KKT conditions | Stationarity, feasibility, non-negative multipliers, complementary slackness |
| Complementary slackness | The multiplier of an inactive constraint is zero |
| Active-set method | Guess which constraints are active, then add or drop constraints according to the signs of the multipliers and the boundaries met |
| Sequential quadratic programming | Linearize a nonlinear constrained problem into a quadratic program at every step |
| Convex problem | Convex objective, convex inequality constraints, linear equality constraints; every local optimum is global |
| Duality gap | The difference between the objective value at a feasible point and the dual lower bound; it measures the quality of a solution |
| Central path | The curve traced by the solution of the barrier method as t varies |
| Zero-residual problem | The residual is zero at the solution, and the Gauss–Newton method converges quadratically |
| Trust region | Compute the step only within the range where the linearization can be trusted; the geometric interpretation of the LM method |
