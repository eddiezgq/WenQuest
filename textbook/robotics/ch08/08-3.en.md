### 8.3.1 The problem: how far one step may go is limited

Return to the planar 2R arm of Section 8.1. Let the arm be at $\theta = (0°, 90°)$ with target $p_d = (0.5, 0.4)$ m; the end-effector still falls short by $e = p_d - p(\theta)$. In the numerical inverse kinematics of Chapter 15 and the visual servoing of Chapter 50, the controller has to decide in every cycle on an increment Δ of the joint angles that brings the end-effector closer to the target. By the linearization of Chapter 2, the increment Δ moves the end-effector by about JΔ, so we want $\|J\Delta - e\|$ to be as small as possible. If J is invertible, $\Delta = J^{-1}e$ makes this quantity zero in a single step.

But how far each step may go is limited. First, joint velocities have upper bounds, so within one cycle a joint angle cannot change by more than a certain amount; second, the linearization is accurate only over a small range, and if the step is too large the actual end-effector position departs from the prediction JΔ. Suppose that each joint may change by at most 0.1 rad per step. The problem then becomes

$$
\min_{\Delta}\ \tfrac12\|J\Delta - e\|^2 \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad -0.1 \le \Delta_i \le 0.1\ \ (i = 1, 2) . \tag{8.3.1}
$$

**Example 8.3.1** From Section 8.1, $J = {{ex8_3.J}}$ m and $e = {{ex8_3.e}}$ m. The solution without the limits is $\Delta_{\rm free} = J^{-1}e = {{ex8_3.d_free}}$ rad: joint 2 would have to turn by 0.21 rad, which exceeds the limit.

There are two obvious remedies: clip the component that exceeds the limit to −0.1, which gives $\Delta_{\rm clip} = {{ex8_3.d_clip}}$; or scale the whole vector down so that its largest component equals 0.1, which gives $\Delta_{\rm scale} = {{ex8_3.d_scale}}$. Both satisfy the limits, but neither is the optimal solution of problem (8.3.1). This section will find the optimal solution $\Delta^* = {{ex8_3.d_qp}}$ rad: joint 2 uses the full 0.1 rad, while joint 1 turns a little in the opposite direction. After this step, the distance $\|J\Delta - e\|$ between the end-effector and the target is {{ex8_3.err_qp:.4f}} m for the optimal solution, {{ex8_3.err_clip:.4f}} m for clipping and {{ex8_3.err_scale:.4f}} m for scaling down. The reason joint 1 turns back is this: joint 2 is limited, so the end-effector cannot move far enough in the x direction; in the current configuration, turning joint 1 clockwise happens to move the end-effector to the right (the x component of the first column of J is negative), which makes up part of the shortfall.

The objective function of problem (8.3.1) is quadratic and its constraints are linear inequalities. Problems of this kind are extremely common and have a name of their own.

**Definition 8.3.1 (Quadratic programming)** A problem of the form

$$
\min_{x\in\mathbb R^n}\ \tfrac12 x^{\mathsf T}Qx + c^{\mathsf T}x \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad Ax \le b,\quad Ex = d \tag{8.3.2}
$$

is called a **quadratic programming** problem (QP), where Q is an n×n symmetric matrix, $Ax \le b$ stands for m **inequality constraints** $a_i^{\mathsf T}x \le b_i$ ($a_i^{\mathsf T}$ is row i of A), and $Ex = d$ are equality constraints. When Q is positive semidefinite the problem is called a convex QP; when Q is positive definite, a strictly convex QP.

In problem (8.3.1), $Q = J^{\mathsf T}J$, $c = -J^{\mathsf T}e$, and the four inequalities are $\Delta_1 \le 0.1$, $\Delta_2 \le 0.1$, $-\Delta_1 \le 0.1$ and $-\Delta_2 \le 0.1$ (the constant term $\tfrac12\|e\|^2$ does not affect the optimal solution). Quadratic programming has far more uses in robotics than this one: the whole-body control of a humanoid robot solves a QP in every control cycle, taking care of balance, the task of the hands and the limits on joint torques at the same time; model predictive control (Chapter 48) optimizes the controls over a stretch of future time together, and in every cycle this too is a QP. Today's dedicated solvers usually solve a QP with a few dozen variables in under a millisecond, and it is exactly this that allows them to be placed inside real-time control loops.

### 8.3.2 Inequality constraints and the KKT conditions

Inequality constraints differ from equality constraints in an essential way: at the optimum, an inequality constraint may "hit its boundary", or it may play no role at all.

**Definition 8.3.2 (Active constraint)** At a feasible point x, if $a_i^{\mathsf T}x = b_i$, the i-th inequality constraint is called an **active constraint** at x; if $a_i^{\mathsf T}x < b_i$, it is called inactive.

An inactive constraint has no influence near x: if x moves slightly, it is still satisfied. So at the optimum we may treat the inactive constraints as if they did not exist and the active constraints as equalities, and then use the Lagrange conditions of Section 8.2. But there is a new restriction here. Look at Figure 8.3.1: the optimum $\Delta^*$ lies on the bottom edge $\Delta_2 = -0.1$ of the square; this constraint is written $a_4^{\mathsf T}\Delta \le 0.1$, and $a_4 = (0, -1)^{\mathsf T}$ is its outward normal (pointing out of the feasible set). By the Lagrange condition, $\nabla q(\Delta^*) + \mu_4 a_4 = 0$, that is, $-\nabla q = \mu_4 a_4$. If $\mu_4 < 0$, then $-\nabla q$ points into the interior of the feasible set; moving inwards in this direction, q decreases while the constraint stays satisfied, and $\Delta^*$ is not the optimum. So the multiplier of an inequality constraint must be non-negative: the negative gradient can only "press" outwards against the boundary.

Putting these conditions together gives the basic theorem of optimization with inequality constraints.

**Theorem 8.3.1 (KKT conditions for quadratic programming)** Let Q be positive semidefinite. Then x* is an optimal solution of the QP (8.3.2) if and only if there exist multipliers $\lambda \in \mathbb R^{p}$ (p is the number of equality constraints) and $\mu \in \mathbb R^m$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
& Qx^* + c + E^{\mathsf T}\lambda + A^{\mathsf T}\mu = 0 && \text{(stationarity)} \\
& Ex^* = d,\quad Ax^* \le b && \text{(primal feasibility)} \\
& \mu \ge 0 && \text{(dual feasibility)} \\
& \mu_i\,(a_i^{\mathsf T}x^* - b_i) = 0,\quad i = 1, \ldots, m && \text{(complementary slackness)}
\end{aligned} \tag{8.3.3}
$$

These four groups of conditions are together called the **KKT conditions**. The last group is called the **complementary slackness condition**: for each inequality constraint, either it is active ($a_i^{\mathsf T}x^* = b_i$) or its multiplier is zero; at least one of the two holds. A zero multiplier for an inactive constraint is exactly what "treating it as if it did not exist" means.

The proof of sufficiency uses the expansion of a quadratic function; the proof of necessity needs the lemma below.

**Lemma 8.3.1 (Farkas' lemma)** Let g be an n-vector, and let $a_1, \ldots, a_k$ and $e_1, \ldots, e_p$ be n-vectors. If $g^{\mathsf T}d \ge 0$ for every d satisfying $e_j^{\mathsf T}d = 0$ (j = 1, …, p) and $a_i^{\mathsf T}d \le 0$ (i = 1, …, k), then there exist real numbers $\lambda_j$ and non-negative real numbers $\mu_i \ge 0$ such that

$$
g + \sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_j e_j + \sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_i a_i = 0 .
$$

The geometric meaning of the lemma is this: if moving along every direction d that "does not violate the constraints" gives no decrease according to g, then −g must lie in the cone formed by non-negative combinations of the outward normals $a_i$ of the constraints (plus the subspace spanned by the normals of the equality constraints). Gyula Farkas published this result in 1902. Its proof uses the separation theorem for convex sets and is given in Section 8.4.4.

*Proof of Theorem 8.3.1*　Sufficiency: let x be any feasible point and write z = x − x*; then Ez = 0. By the expansion of the quadratic function and the stationarity condition,

$$
q(x) - q(x^*) = (Qx^* + c)^{\mathsf T}z + \tfrac12 z^{\mathsf T}Qz \ \ge\ -\lambda^{\mathsf T}Ez - \mu^{\mathsf T}Az = -\sum_i\mu_i\,a_i^{\mathsf T}(x - x^*) .
$$

By complementary slackness, $\mu_i a_i^{\mathsf T}x^* = \mu_i b_i$, so the right-hand side equals $\sum_i\mu_i(b_i - a_i^{\mathsf T}x)$. Since $\mu_i \ge 0$ and $a_i^{\mathsf T}x \le b_i$, it is non-negative. Hence $q(x) \ge q(x^*)$.

Necessity: let x* be an optimal solution, and let I be the set of indices of the inequality constraints active at x*. Take any d satisfying Ed = 0 and $a_i^{\mathsf T}d \le 0$ (i ∈ I). For the inactive constraints, $a_i^{\mathsf T}x^* < b_i$, and $a_i^{\mathsf T}(x^* + sd) < b_i$ still holds for s small enough; so for s > 0 small enough $x^* + sd$ is feasible and $q(x^* + sd) \ge q(x^*)$. By Eq. (8.1.5), this requires $\nabla q(x^*)^{\mathsf T}d \ge 0$. Applying Farkas' lemma to $g = \nabla q(x^*)$ (with the rows $a_i$ for i ∈ I) gives λ and $\mu_i \ge 0$ (i ∈ I); setting the remaining $\mu_i = 0$, all four groups of conditions hold.　∎

For a general nonlinear problem $\min f(x)$ s.t. $g_i(x) \le 0$, $h_j(x) = 0$, under a suitable constraint qualification (for example, that the gradients of the active inequality constraints and of all equality constraints are linearly independent), there likewise exist at a local minimizer multipliers satisfying

$$
\nabla f + \sum_j\lambda_j\nabla h_j + \sum_i\mu_i\nabla g_i = 0, \qquad \mu_i \ge 0, \qquad \mu_i\,g_i = 0 \tag{8.3.4}
$$

This result first appeared in William Karush's 1939 master's thesis at the University of Chicago, which was not published at the time; Harold W. Kuhn and Albert W. Tucker gave it independently in their 1951 paper "Nonlinear Programming" and made it widely known; Fritz John had also obtained a similar form in 1948. That is why these conditions are known today as the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions, KKT conditions for short. The multiplier $\mu_i$ has the same meaning as in Section 8.2.4: if the i-th constraint is relaxed by one unit ($b_i$ increased by 1), the optimal value decreases by about $\mu_i$. That the multipliers are non-negative says precisely that relaxing a constraint cannot make the optimal value worse.

The constraint qualification cannot be dropped: the example of Section 8.2.3 (whose feasible set is the single point at the origin), rewritten as the inequality constraint $x_1^2 + x_2^2 \le 0$, has a minimizer but no multipliers. When all the constraints are linear functions, no constraint qualification is needed; this is exactly the case in the necessity proof of Theorem 8.3.1, which used only Farkas' lemma.

Return to Example 8.3.1. At $\Delta^*$ only the fourth constraint, $-\Delta_2 \le 0.1$, is active. Program 8.3.1 computes $\nabla q(\Delta^*) = {{ex8_3.grad_qp}}$, so the stationarity condition $\nabla q + \mu_4 a_4 = 0$ gives $\mu_4 = {{ex8_3.mu4:.5f}} > 0$; the other three multipliers are zero. The residuals of all four groups of conditions are below $10^{-12}$, so $\Delta^*$ is the optimal solution. The first component of $\nabla q$ is zero, which says that moving along the bottom edge of the square can bring no further improvement; the second component is positive, which says that if joint 2 were allowed to turn a little more (the constraint relaxed), the objective function could still decrease, and the rate of decrease is exactly $\mu_4$.

### 8.3.3 The active-set method

The KKT conditions tell us what the optimum looks like, but not how to find it. The difficulty is that we do not know in advance which constraints are active. If we did, treating them as equalities would give the problem of Section 8.2.5, and solving one system of linear equations (8.2.8) would be enough. The idea of the active-set method is exactly "guess first, then correct": maintain a working set W, the constraints currently believed to be active; solve the equality-constrained problem on W; add constraints to W or remove them from it according to the result, until the KKT conditions are all satisfied.

For a strictly convex QP, starting from a feasible point $x_0$ (for example Δ = 0), each step does the following.

First, treat the constraints in W as equalities, ignore the other constraints for the moment, and find the minimizer of q subject to these equalities. Written in terms of an increment p from the current point:

$$
\min_p\ q(x_k + p) \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad Ep = 0,\quad a_i^{\mathsf T}p = 0\ \ (i \in W) ,
$$

This is an equality-constrained quadratic problem; p and the multipliers are obtained from the KKT system (8.2.8).

Second, if p ≠ 0, move along p. On the way we may run into a constraint not in W; if so, stop there and add it to W. Specifically, for each constraint not in W with $a_i^{\mathsf T}p > 0$, compute the fraction $(b_i - a_i^{\mathsf T}x_k)/(a_i^{\mathsf T}p)$ of the way to its boundary; take the smallest of these, compare it with 1, and use the smaller as the step length.

Third, if p = 0, the current point is already the minimizer on W; check the multipliers of the constraints in W. If they are all non-negative, the KKT conditions (8.3.3) are all satisfied, and we stop; if some are negative, that constraint should not be active (moving away from it, q can still decrease), and the constraint with the most negative multiplier is removed from W.

It can be proved that for a strictly convex QP this procedure never increases q; if we further assume that each time p ≠ 0 the step taken is greater than zero (called non-degeneracy: we never hit a boundary right at the start), q decreases strictly, and the same working set cannot occur twice. There are only finitely many working sets, so the active-set method finds the optimal solution in a finite number of steps (for the proof see the book by Nocedal and Wright; in degenerate cases special rules are needed to prevent the working set from cycling). Martin Beale in 1955 and 1959 and Philip Wolfe in 1959 gave simplex-type algorithms for quadratic programming; these were the earliest active-set methods. The solvers commonly used in robotics today, such as the dual active-set method of Donald Goldfarb and Ashok Idnani (1983) and qpOASES, designed specifically for model predictive control (Ferreau et al., 2014), all belong to this family; another commonly used class of solvers, such as OSQP (Stellato et al., 2020), is based on the alternating direction method of multipliers, which this book does not cover.

For Example 8.3.1, the active-set method of Program 8.3.1 starts from Δ = 0, W = ∅. The first step takes no constraint into account and heads towards $\Delta_{\rm free}$; about 48% of the way there it hits $\Delta_2 = -0.1$ and adds it to W. The second step finds the minimum along this edge, giving $\Delta^*$; the multiplier is positive, and the method stops. It takes {{ex8_3.n_it1}} steps in all, and the solution differs from that of SciPy's SLSQP method by ${{ex8_3.sp_err1:sci1}}$.

### 8.3.4 Adding joint limits: sequential quadratic programming

At equilibrium, the arm with torsion springs of Section 8.2 has $\theta_3 = {{ex8_2.th3:.2f}}°$. If joint 3 has a range of motion from −80° to 80°, this configuration cannot be reached at all. Adding the joint limits $\theta_{\min} \le \theta \le \theta_{\max}$, the problem becomes

$$
\min_\theta\ \tfrac12(\theta - \theta_c)^{\mathsf T}K(\theta - \theta_c) \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad p(\theta) = p_d,\quad \theta_{\min} \le \theta \le \theta_{\max} . \tag{8.3.5}
$$

It has both nonlinear equality constraints and linear inequality constraints. The Lagrange–Newton method of Section 8.2.6 solves an equality-constrained quadratic problem (8.2.10) at each step; now this step is replaced by a QP with inequality constraints:

$$
\min_{\Delta}\ \tfrac12\Delta^{\mathsf T}\nabla_{xx}^2 L_k\,\Delta + \nabla f_k^{\mathsf T}\Delta \qquad \text{s.t.}\quad J_k\Delta = -h_k,\quad \theta_{\min} - \theta_k \le \Delta \le \theta_{\max} - \theta_k , \tag{8.3.6}
$$

where $J_k$ and $h_k = p(\theta_k) - p_d$ are the Jacobian matrix and the value of the constraints at $\theta_k$, and $\nabla_{xx}^2 L_k$ is the Hessian matrix of the Lagrangian. Having solved for Δ, set $\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + \Delta$, and take the multipliers of the equality and inequality constraints directly from the QP. This is **sequential quadratic programming** (SQP). It was proposed by Robert B. Wilson in his 1963 doctoral thesis and made into a practical algorithm by Shih-Ping Han in 1977 and Michael J. D. Powell in 1978; it is still the workhorse for nonlinear constrained optimization problems of small and medium size, and SciPy's SLSQP method is one variant of it.

The active-set method needs a feasible starting point. In Eq. (8.3.6), Δ = 0 generally does not satisfy the equality constraints, so at each step a linear program is solved first to find a Δ satisfying all the constraints; this is called Phase I. Moreover, Section 8.2 has already shown that $\nabla_{xx}^2 L$ is not necessarily positive definite; but as long as it is positive definite on the null space of $J_k$ (the reduced Hessian is positive), Eq. (8.3.6) is strictly convex within the equality constraints, and the active-set method still applies. Program 8.3.1 checks this at every step.

**Example 8.3.2** Take $\theta_{\min} = (-90°, 0°, -80°)$ and $\theta_{\max} = (90°, 150°, 80°)$, with the other data as in Example 8.2.2. Starting from $\theta_c$, the step lengths $\|\Delta\|$ of the successive steps of SQP are {{ex8_3.s1:.4f}}, {{ex8_3.s2:.4f}}, {{ex8_3.s3:.5f}} and ${{ex8_3.s4:sci2}}$ rad, and it converges in {{ex8_3.sqp_it}} steps. The result is

$$
\theta^* = {{ex8_3.th3_deg}}\ (°), \qquad \lambda^* = {{ex8_3.lam3}}\ \mathrm{N}, \qquad \mu_{\theta_3 \ge -80°} = {{ex8_3.mu3:.4f}}\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} ,
$$

and the multipliers of the other five limits are all zero. Joint 3 stops at its lower limit; the potential energy is {{ex8_3.f3:.4f}} J, slightly more than the {{ex8_3.f_nolim:.4f}} J obtained without the limits: with one more constraint, the optimal value can only get worse or stay the same. The residual of the stationarity condition is ${{ex8_3.stat3:sci1}}$, and the solution found by SciPy's trust-constr method differs from it by ${{ex8_3.rs_err:sci1}}$ rad. This multiplier, too, has a mechanical meaning. Written as a balance of torques, the stationarity condition is

$$
\tau_{\rm spring} + J^{\mathsf T}F + \mu\,e_3 = 0 , \qquad \tau_{\rm spring} = -K(\theta^* - \theta_c),\quad F = -\lambda^* , \tag{8.3.7}
$$

with $e_3 = (0, 0, 1)^{\mathsf T}$. The torque of the torsion springs, $\tau_{\rm spring} = {{ex8_3.tau_s}}$ N·m, the torque $J^{\mathsf T}F$ that the force of the hand produces at the joints, and the torque μ that the mechanical stop of joint 3 applies to the joint are in balance. μ > 0 means that the stop pushes joint 3 in the positive direction, preventing it from turning further in the negative direction. The complementary slackness condition states plain common sense: a stop that a joint does not touch exerts no force.

### 8.3.5 Minimum-energy trajectories

Another typical use of quadratic programming is trajectory optimization. Suppose one joint of a manipulator has to turn from 0 to $\theta_f = 2$ rad in T = 2 s, starting and ending at rest. The motor torque is proportional to the current, and the copper loss (heating) in the armature winding is proportional to the square of the current; if the joint mainly overcomes inertia, the torque is in turn proportional to the angular acceleration a. So the heat generated during the motion is proportional to

$$
\int_0^T a(t)^2\, dt . \tag{8.3.8}
$$

The question is: what motion makes Eq. (8.3.8) smallest? And what is the answer if the joint velocity may not exceed $v_{\max} = 1.2$ rad/s?

Divide the time into N = 100 intervals, each of length h = 0.02 s, and let the angular acceleration be a constant $a_k$ in interval k. By the formulas for uniformly accelerated motion,

$$
\omega_{k+1} = \omega_k + a_k h, \qquad \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + \omega_k h + \tfrac12 a_k h^2 , \tag{8.3.9}
$$

Recursing from $\theta_0 = \omega_0 = 0$, the final $\theta_N$ and $\omega_N$ and the $\omega_k$ at every instant are all linear combinations of $a_0, \ldots, a_{N-1}$. So the decision variables are the 100 values $a_k$; the objective function $\tfrac12\sum_k a_k^2 h$ (which approximates $\tfrac12\int a^2 dt$; the factor ½ does not change the optimal solution) is quadratic; the end conditions $\theta_N = \theta_f$, $\omega_N = 0$ are 2 linear equalities; and the speed limits $\omega_k \le v_{\max}$ (k = 1, …, 99) are 99 linear inequalities: a standard QP.

First leave out the speed limit. There are only the two equality constraints; by the KKT conditions, $a_k h$ plus a linear combination of the gradients of the two constraints equals zero, and the partial derivative of $\theta_N$ with respect to $a_k$ varies linearly with k, so the optimal $a_k$ varies linearly with time and the position is a cubic polynomial in time. The solution in the continuous case is $a(t) = \frac{6\theta_f}{T^2}(1 - \frac{2t}{T})$, with cost $\int a^2 dt = 12\theta_f^2/T^3 = 6$ rad²/s³, and the velocity reaches its peak $1.5\,\theta_f/T = 1.5$ rad/s at the midpoint. The discrete solution of Program 8.3.1 gives a cost of {{ex8_3.J_free:.4f}} and a peak velocity of {{ex8_3.v_free:.4f}} rad/s; the difference from the continuous solution comes from the discretization. The cubic polynomial trajectory is the basic form of point-to-point motion in Chapter 22, and here we see one of its origins: it is the motion that uses the least energy in this sense.

The peak of 1.5 rad/s exceeds the speed limit. Adding the 99 inequality constraints and solving with the active-set method, Program 8.3.1 takes {{ex8_3.n_it3}} iterations, and in the end {{ex8_3.n_act}} constraints are active: from t = {{ex8_3.act_from:.2f}} s to {{ex8_3.act_to:.2f}} s the velocity runs exactly along the limit. The cost is {{ex8_3.J_con:.4f}}, {{ex8_3.J_rise:.0f}}% more than without the limit. The four KKT residuals are all below ${{ex8_3.kkt3:sci1}}$, and the solution given by SciPy's SLSQP method differs from it by at most ${{ex8_3.st_err:sci0}}$ rad/s². **Example 8.3.3** The continuous form of this problem can be solved analytically and used to check the numerical result. From Figure 8.3.3 we can guess the structure of the solution: an acceleration phase, a constant-velocity phase and a deceleration phase. In the acceleration phase the constraint is inactive, and by the KKT conditions the acceleration still varies linearly with time; at the instant $t_1$ of entering the constant-velocity phase, the velocity is exactly $v_{\max}$ and the acceleration falls continuously to zero. The latter point is not a kinematic requirement (the acceleration could jump suddenly from a positive value to zero) but a requirement of optimality: if the acceleration jumped to zero at $t_1$, lengthening the acceleration phase slightly while lowering its acceleration would cover the same angle at a smaller cost. This can be proved rigorously by the calculus of variations, which we omit here; the discrete solution in Figure 8.3.3 also shows the acceleration falling continuously to zero at $t_1$. From $a = c\,(t_1 - t)$ and $\omega(t_1) = c\,t_1^2/2 = v_{\max}$, the angle covered in the acceleration phase is $c\,t_1^3/3 = \tfrac23 v_{\max}t_1$; the deceleration phase is symmetric to it, so the total angle is $\tfrac43 v_{\max}t_1 + v_{\max}(T - 2t_1) = \theta_f$, which gives

$$
t_1 = \frac{3\,(v_{\max}T - \theta_f)}{2\,v_{\max}} , \qquad \int_0^T a^2\,dt = \frac{8\,v_{\max}^2}{3\,t_1} . \tag{8.3.10}
$$

Substituting the data, $t_1 = {{ex8_3.t1:.2f}}$ s and the cost is {{ex8_3.J_an:.2f}}, which differs from the discrete QP solution {{ex8_3.J_con:.4f}} by less than 0.1%. Eq. (8.3.10) applies only when $\theta_f/T \le v_{\max} \le 1.5\,\theta_f/T$: if the limit is below the average speed, the task cannot be done; if it is above the peak without the limit, the limit is inactive.

Here, too, the multipliers have a clear meaning. If the speed limit is relaxed by δ, the cost decreases by about $\delta\sum_k\mu_k$ (the objective function is $\tfrac12\sum a_k^2 h$, so this is the decrease of $\tfrac12\int a^2 dt$). The program computes $\sum_k\mu_k = {{ex8_3.mu_sum:.4f}}$; differentiating Eq. (8.3.10), the analytic value of $-\tfrac12\,d(\int a^2 dt)/dv_{\max}$ is {{ex8_3.half_dJ:.1f}}, and the two agree. This tells the designer that under these operating conditions, raising the speed limit of the joint by 0.01 rad/s reduces $\int a^2 dt$ by about 0.2 rad²/s³, and the heating falls in the same proportion; this can be weighed against the cost of a higher speed.

### 8.3.6 Everyday examples

When you take a lift, it starts from rest and accelerates, runs at constant speed once it reaches its rated speed, and then decelerates to stop at the floor. The shape of its velocity profile is just like Figure 8.3.3: the rated speed is the speed limit, and how gently it accelerates and decelerates bears on the comfort of the passengers and the heating of the motor. Planning the velocity profile in a lift controller is the same kind of problem as that of Section 8.3.5.

The earliest famous application of quadratic programming was not in engineering but in investment. In 1952, in the paper "Portfolio Selection", Harry Markowitz proposed the following: money is divided among several assets, and the fractions held in each asset are the decision variables; the variance of the portfolio's return (its risk) is a quadratic function of the fractions; requiring the expected return to be no less than a given value, and the fractions to be non-negative and sum to 1, are all linear constraints. So the portfolio "with the least risk for a given return" is the solution of a QP. For this he received a share of the 1990 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences. When someone divides their savings into several parts to buy different funds, a QP of this kind lies behind it.

The QPs of this section are all convex, so a local optimum is a global optimum, and the algorithms are guaranteed to converge. The next section explains why the property of being "convex" matters so much, and how problems that look non-convex, such as obstacle avoidance, can be turned into convex problems and solved.

**Engineer's notes**

- Do not simply clip the components that exceed the limits. The clipped solution is feasible, but in general not optimal; in coordinated multi-joint motion, clipping also changes the direction of motion of the end-effector, so that it drifts off its intended path. Scaling down keeps the direction, but is not optimal either.
- In a QP for real-time control, the numbers of variables and constraints are the same in every cycle, and the optimal working set changes little. Using the working set of the previous cycle as the starting guess (a warm start), the active-set method often converges in one or two steps.
- An infeasible QP (constraints that contradict one another) is not rare in robotics, for example when the velocity a target demands is beyond what the joint limits can provide. The practical remedy is to turn some constraints into "soft constraints": add slack variables and penalize the slack in the objective function, so that the problem always has a solution.
- When checking a solver's output, look at three things at least: primal feasibility (whether the constraints are satisfied), whether the multipliers are non-negative, and whether complementary slackness holds. Clearly negative multipliers mean that the solver has not converged or the problem has been modelled wrongly.
- Keep the numerical scales of the constraints similar. When joint constraints in rad are combined with position constraints in mm, first make the units consistent or normalize; otherwise the solver's tolerances mean completely different things for different constraints.
