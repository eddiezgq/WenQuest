This chapter dealt with another standard way of writing forward kinematics: the DH parameter method. Its starting point is a geometric fact: the relative position of two joint axes is completely determined by their common normal and needs only two numbers, the length of the common normal and the angle between the two axes; the state of the joint is described by two more numbers, a distance along the axis and an angle about it. So, as long as a frame is placed on each link according to the rules, the transformation between two adjacent frames contains only four parameters, and the whole arm is the product of these transformations.

There are two sets of rules. Standard DH puts {i} at the far end of link i (on axis i+1), and the transformation goes first z, then x; Craig's modified DH puts {i} at the near end (on axis i), first x, then z. Both use the same set of geometric quantities, only shifted by one row in the parameter table, and the products along the whole chain differ by just one fixed transformation $X_n$. Use the wrong convention and what you compute is not this robot; given a table, the first thing is to find out its convention.

Setting up the frames for a particular robot follows fixed steps, with additional conventions for intersecting axes, parallel axes, coincident axes, prismatic joints, and the base and end-effector. The freedom these conventions leave does not affect the forward kinematics (Theorem 13.2.1), but it allows the same robot to have many different parameter tables. Manufacturers' manuals usually choose the form that makes the joint offsets zero, so negative link lengths appear; the official parameter table of the UR5e is an example. The end-effector computed from it differs from the parts-library model of Chapter 12 by about one millimetre; the difference comes entirely from the length parameters and can be written out exactly, term by term (Theorem 13.3.1).

DH parameters and the product of exponentials can be converted into each other exactly. From DH to the product of exponentials takes only one formula: at the zero position, the z-axis of {i−1} in standard DH ({i} in modified DH) is the screw axis of joint i. From the product of exponentials to DH, each screw axis must be turned back into a line, and the common normals found by the frame-assignment steps. This chapter verified both directions with the UR5e (standard DH) and the Panda (the Craig table published by Franka); the differences from the 3D models of the parts library are only rounding errors.

The main limitation of DH appears when adjacent axes are nearly parallel: if one axis tilts by ε within the plane of the two axes, the common normal degenerates to a distant intersection point, d is about −a/ε, and at ε = 0 it jumps from −∞ to +∞. This is why values of d of hundreds of metres appear in the factory calibration data of real robots. For calibration, Hayati parameters or methods based on the product of exponentials should be used instead. DH suits communication, and the product of exponentials suits computation and calibration: this is the division of labour in this book.

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| Common normal | The line segment perpendicular to and meeting both lines; unique when they are not parallel, and its length is the shortest distance between them |
| DH parameters | Link length a and link twist α (belonging to the link); link offset d and joint angle θ (belonging to the joint) |
| Standard DH | {i} is on axis i+1, and $z_i$ lies along the axis of the next joint; the transformation goes first z, then x |
| Modified DH | Craig's convention: {i} is on axis i, and $z_i$ lies along the axis of its own joint; the transformation goes first x, then z, and row i of the table holds $a_{i-1}$, $\alpha_{i-1}$, $d_i$, $\theta_i$ |
| Link frame | A frame fixed to each link according to the rules; its position is determined by the axes and need not be at the centre of the physical joint |
| Joint offset | The constant difference between the controller's joint variable and the DH joint angle (or offset) |
| Nominal parameters | The parameters on the design drawings; common to all robots of a model, and differing by a few tenths of a millimetre from the calibrated parameters of each robot |
| Manufacturer's-manual form | Makes the joint offsets zero wherever possible, and so allows negative link lengths |
| Hayati parameters | Replace d by an angle β about y to describe two nominally parallel axes; the parameters change continuously with the geometry |

The two frame notations compared (see Table 13.1.1 for details): Craig's ${}^{A}P$, ${}^{A}_{B}R$, ${}^{A}_{B}T$ and ${}^{i-1}_{\ \ i}T$ correspond to this book's $p_a$, $R_{ab}$, $T_{ab}$ and $T_{i-1,i}$ respectively; Craig's rule is that an adjacent superscript and subscript cancel, this book's rule is that two adjacent subscripts cancel.

### Try it in WenQuest

- Parts library B-ARM-WQCR5 (WenQuest WQ-CR5 six-axis collaborative robot): its geometry uses the nominal standard DH parameters of whole-robot calibration, and its URDF/MJCF models can be downloaded. Build the link frames yourself with the method of this chapter and check them against the body positions in the model. Its J2, J3 and J4 axes are parallel, exactly the weak spot of DH discussed in Section 13.5.
- Digital factory Lab 26 (robot plant): whole-robot calibration of one WQ-CR5. First compute the end position with the nominal DH parameters, then see why only 21 of the 24 parameter errors can be separated (Section 13.5), and how the measurement poses should be chosen.

### Historical notes

| Year | Who | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| 1955 | Denavit, Hartenberg | Described the relation between two adjacent axes of a lower-pair mechanism with four parameters and matrices, introducing the DH notation |
| 1981 | Paul | *Robot Manipulators: Mathematics, Programming, and Control* applied the DH method systematically to the kinematics of industrial arms |
| 1983 | Hayati | Pointed out that DH parameters are ill-conditioned in calibration when adjacent axes are parallel, and proposed replacing the offset by an angle about y |
| 1984 | Brockett | Proposed the product-of-exponentials formula (Chapter 12) |
| 1985 | Hayati, Mirmirani | Gave the modified parameters in the form of Eq. (13.5.3), used to improve the absolute positioning accuracy of arms |
| 1986 | Craig | *Introduction to Robotics: Mechanics and Control* adopted modified DH, which puts the frames at the near end of each link |
| 1986 | Khalil, Kleinfinger | Extended modified DH to tree-structured and closed-chain robots |
| 1987 | Everett, Driels, Mooring | Set out the requirements of completeness and minimality for calibration models, and showed that a complete and minimal kinematic model of a serial robot has 4R + 2P + 6 independent parameters |
| 1996 | Okamura, Park | Proposed a kinematic calibration method based on the product-of-exponentials formula |
