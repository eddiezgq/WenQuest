### 13.2.1 The problem

The previous section defined the DH parameters for three axes in general position. Faced with a particular robot, things are often not so "general": on the UR5e, the axes of joints 1 and 2 intersect, and the axes of joints 2, 3 and 4 are parallel to one another; the SCARA has three vertical parallel axes and also a prismatic joint whose axis coincides with the axis of joint 4. Phrases in Definition 13.1.3 such as "along the common normal" and "at the foot of the normal" need additional conventions, since for intersecting axes the common normal has zero length, and for parallel axes there are infinitely many common normals. In addition, the base frame {0} and the end-effector frame {n} each have a neighbouring axis on one side only, and these too need their own rules.

This section organizes the process of setting up link frames into a fixed sequence of steps, explains how to handle the special cases one by one, and then actually sets up the frames for the SCARA and the UR5e, writes down their parameter tables, and checks them against the product-of-exponentials results of Chapter 12. Applying these steps is also the most common type of question in robotics examinations.

### 13.2.2 Steps for setting up frames in standard DH

Suppose the arm has n joints, numbered outwards from the base. The link frames {0}, {1}, …, {n} of standard DH are set up as follows.

1. **Mark the joint axes.** On the drawing or model, draw axes 1 to n and mark the positive direction of each axis: for a revolute joint it agrees, by the right-hand rule, with the direction in which the joint angle increases; for a prismatic joint it agrees with the direction in which the joint variable increases. The positive directions should agree with the controller, otherwise the computed joint angles will differ by a sign.
2. **Find the common normals of adjacent axes.** For i = 1, …, n−1, find the common normal $N_i$ of axis i and axis i+1 by the method of Section 13.1.2.
3. **Set up the base frame {0}.** $z_0$ lies along axis 1; the origin and the direction of $x_0$ may be chosen freely, usually with the origin at the centre of the base mounting face and $x_0$ aligned with a mark on the base.
4. **Set up the intermediate frames {i} (i = 1, …, n−1).** $z_i$ lies along axis i+1, in the positive direction of axis i+1; the origin $o_i$ is where $N_i$ meets axis i+1; $x_i$ lies along $N_i$; $y_i = z_i \times x_i$.
5. **Set up the end-effector frame {n}.** Beyond the end-effector there is no "axis n+1", so there is considerable freedom in choosing {n}: usually $z_n$ is made parallel to $z_{n-1}$ ($\alpha_n = 0$), the origin is placed at the centre of the flange or at the tool centre point, and $x_n$ is perpendicular to $z_{n-1}$ and meets it.
6. **Read off the parameters.** For each i, read off the four parameters from the two adjacent frames according to their definitions.

Step 6 can be written in a form convenient for computation. By the fourth column of Eq. (13.1.5), the origin $o_i$ of {i} is reached from $o_{i-1}$ by going first $d_i$ along $z_{i-1}$ and then $a_i$ along $x_i$, that is, $o_i - o_{i-1} = d_i\,z_{i-1} + a_i\,x_i$. Since $x_i$ is perpendicular to $z_{i-1}$, taking the dot product of both sides with $x_i$ and with $z_{i-1}$ gives

$$
a_i = (o_i - o_{i-1})\cdot x_i, \qquad d_i = (o_i - o_{i-1})\cdot z_{i-1} . \tag{13.2.1}
$$

The two angles are given in the form of Eq. (13.1.3), with axes of rotation $x_i$ and $z_{i-1}$ respectively:

$$
\alpha_i = \operatorname{atan2}\big((z_{i-1} \times z_i)\cdot x_i,\ z_{i-1}\cdot z_i\big), \qquad
\theta_i = \operatorname{atan2}\big((x_{i-1} \times x_i)\cdot z_{i-1},\ x_{i-1}\cdot x_i\big) . \tag{13.2.2}
$$

Here atan2(y, x) is the two-argument arctangent function of program libraries, which uses the signs of its two arguments to give an angle in (−π, π]. All the vectors in Eqs. (13.2.1) and (13.2.2) are expressed in the same frame (for example {s}); this is exactly how frames are set up automatically in a program.

The $\theta_i$ in Eq. (13.2.2) is the value read off with the robot at the zero position. At the zero position the joint variables are zero, but the DH joint angles $\theta_i$ need not be, because the zero position is a convention of the controller, whereas the directions of $x_{i-1}$ and $x_i$ are determined by the rules for setting up the frames, and there is no reason for the two to agree.

**Definition 13.2.1 (Joint offset)** Denote the joint variable of the controller by $\theta_i^{\mathrm c}$ (an angle for a revolute joint, a displacement for a prismatic joint). The DH parameters are related to it by

$$
\theta_i = \theta_i^{\mathrm c} + \delta_i \quad (\text{revolute joint}), \qquad d_i = \theta_i^{\mathrm c} + \delta_i \quad (\text{prismatic joint}), \tag{13.2.3}
$$

and the constant $\delta_i$ is called the **joint offset**; it is just the $\theta_i$ or $d_i$ read off at the zero position by Eq. (13.2.2) or (13.2.1).

Many textbooks write the θ column of the parameter table in forms such as "$\theta_1$" and "$\theta_2 - \pi/2$"; what is subtracted is the joint offset. Manufacturers' manuals generally choose the directions of the x-axes so as to make the offsets zero wherever possible, so that the joint readings can be substituted directly into the formulas; Section 13.3 will show how UR does this.

### 13.2.3 Special cases

Steps 2 and 4 need additional rules in the following cases, illustrated in Figure 13.2.1.

**Adjacent axes intersect.** The common normal has zero length, and $a_i = 0$. Take $o_i$ at the point of intersection, and take $x_i$ in the direction perpendicular to both axes, that is, $\pm\, z_{i-1} \times z_i$, with either sign. Reversing $x_i$ changes the sign of $\alpha_i$ (the axis of rotation is reversed, so the angle of the same rotation changes sign), and changes the offset of $\theta_i$ and the offset of $\theta_{i+1}$ in the next row by π each, without affecting the forward kinematics (Theorem 13.2.1).

**Adjacent axes are parallel.** There are infinitely many common normals (Theorem 13.1.1), which can slide freely along the axes. Usually the one through $o_{i-1}$ is chosen, so that $d_i = 0$; then $a_i$ is the distance between the two axes, and $\alpha_i = 0$ or π (the two axes point the same way or opposite ways). Another one may also be chosen for engineering convenience, for example to put the origin of the frame at the height of the centre of the physical link, at the cost of a non-zero $d_i$.

**Adjacent axes coincide.** Even the direction of the common normal is undetermined, and $a_i = 0$. $x_i$ may be taken in any direction perpendicular to the axis; it is generally taken to be the same as $x_{i-1}$, so that the offset of $\theta_i$ is zero.

**Prismatic joints.** A prismatic joint has only a direction, not a position: a translation in a given direction has the same effect wherever its "axis" is drawn (as Chapter 12 explained, the screw axis of a prismatic joint contains only the direction v). So when setting up the frames, the axis of a prismatic joint can be shifted to wherever is most convenient, usually so that it passes through the next axis or the origin of the previous frame, making as many parameters zero as possible. Its joint variable appears in $d_i$, and $\theta_i$ is a constant.

**Base and end-effector.** If the base frame {s} of the robot does not meet the requirements of step 3 (for example, if the z-axis of {s} does not lie along axis 1), a fixed transformation $T_{s0}$ is added between {s} and {0}; likewise $T_{nb}$ is added at the end-effector, see Eq. (13.1.6). In general $T_{s0}$ and $T_{nb}$ are both arbitrary rigid-body transformations, each needing 6 numbers, and cannot be expressed with 4 DH parameters.

All these choices change the appearance of the parameter table. So if different people set up the frames with different choices, will the end-effector poses they compute differ?

**Theorem 13.2.1** Suppose {0} and {n} have been chosen. However the intermediate frames {1}, …, {n−1} are chosen (as long as each {i} is fixed on link i), the $T_{0n}(\theta)$ computed by Eq. (13.1.6) is the same.

*Proof* Replace {k} by another frame {k′} on the same link; the two are related by a fixed transformation G that does not change with the joint variables, $T_{k k'} = G$. By the subscript-cancellation rule (Theorem 5.3.1), the new adjacent transformations are $T_{k-1,k'} = T_{k-1,k}\,G$ and $T_{k',k+1} = G^{-1}\,T_{k,k+1}$; when the two are multiplied together, $G\,G^{-1} = I$ cancels and the product is unchanged. Doing this for each intermediate frame in turn proves the theorem.　∎

Note that Theorem 13.2.1 only guarantees that the product is unchanged; it does not guarantee that, after the change of frame, each adjacent transformation still has the DH form of Eq. (13.1.4). Only a few kinds of G preserve the DH form, for example a translation along $z_k$ at parallel axes, or a rotation through π about $z_k$ (that is, reversing $x_k$), and these correspond exactly to the free choices listed above. Program 13.2.1 gives an illustration for the UR5e: moving the origin of {2} by 0.05 m along $z_2$ and reversing $x_2$ changes a, d and the θ offsets in rows 2 and 3, while $T_{06}$ over a hundred random sets of joint angles is exactly the same as with the original table.

### 13.2.4 The link frames of the SCARA

**Example 13.2.1** Set up the standard DH link frames for the SCARA of the parts library, write down the parameter table, and check it with the joint variables of Example 12.3.2, $(40^\circ, -70^\circ, 0.12\ \mathrm m, 90^\circ)$.

From Table 12.3.2, at the zero position all four axes are vertical: axis 1 passes through the origin, axis 2 is at x = 0.35 m, and axis 3 (the lead screw, positive downwards) and axis 4 are at x = 0.6 m, where they coincide. The z-axis of {s} is axis 1 itself, so we take {0} = {s}.

Axes 1 and 2 are parallel, point the same way and are 0.35 m apart, so $a_1 = 0.35$ m and $\alpha_1 = 0$. By the rule "take $d = 0$ for parallel axes", $N_1$ is taken as the one through $o_0$, and the origin of {1} is at ${{ex13_2.oA1}}$ m, at ground level. Axes 2 and 3 are parallel but point opposite ways (axis 3 points downwards), so $a_2 = 0.25$ m and $\alpha_2 = \pi$, with $z_2$ pointing downwards so that the displacement of the lead screw is just $d_3$. Axes 3 and 4 coincide, so $a_3 = 0$, and $x_3$ is taken to be the same as $x_2$; $z_3$ points upwards along axis 4, so $\alpha_3 = \pi$. The end-effector is taken at the centre of the tool mounting face, on axis 4, and $d_4$ is determined by its height. This gives Table A in Table 13.2.1.

Table A follows the rules exactly, but all the frame origins are at ground level, and the origin of {3}, ${{ex13_2.oA3}}$ m, is not on the tool either, so the table is not easy to read. More common in engineering is Table B: $N_1$ is taken at the height of the upper arm, 0.4 m ($d_1 = 0.4$ m), and the origin of {3} is placed on the tool mounting face. At the zero position the tool mounting face is 0.158 m below the upper arm, so the DH parameter of the lead screw is $d_3 = \theta_3^{\mathrm c} + 0.158$ m, with joint offset $\delta_3 = 0.158$ m; {4} coincides with {3}, and $d_4 = 0$.

### 13.2.5 The link frames of the UR5e

**Example 13.2.2** Following the steps of Section 13.2.2, set up the link frames {0}…{6} for the UR5e, using the axes from Table 12.1.1.

{0} is taken to be the base frame {s} of Chapter 12: its origin is at ${{ex13_2.o0}}$, and $z_0$ points upwards along axis 1. The intermediate frames are set up one by one below, recording the coordinates in {s} of each origin at the zero position (in m).

**{1}.** Axis 1 is vertical, and axis 2 points along −y at a height of 0.163 m; the two intersect at (0, 0, 0.163). So $a_1 = 0$, the origin of {1} is at the point of intersection ${{ex13_2.o1}}$, and $d_1 = 0.163$ m. $z_1$ points in the positive direction of axis 2, −y; $x_1$ is chosen as +x out of $\pm z_0 \times z_1 = \pm x$, the same direction as $x_0$, so that the offset of $\theta_1$ is zero. Turning from $z_0$ (+z) about $x_1$ to $z_1$ (−y) is +90°, so $\alpha_1 = \pi/2$.

**{2}, {3}.** Axes 2, 3 and 4 are parallel to one another. By the rule, $d_2 = d_3 = 0$, the common normals all lie in the vertical plane y = 0, and the origins of {2} and {3} are at ${{ex13_2.o2}}$ and ${{ex13_2.o3}}$ respectively. $x_2$ and $x_3$ point the same way as $x_1$ (+x), while the origins advance in the −x direction, so by Eq. (13.2.1), $a_2 = -0.425$ m and $a_3 = -0.392$ m.

**{4}.** Axis 4 (along −y) and axis 5 (pointing vertically downwards) intersect at (−0.817, −0.134, 0.163), so $a_4 = 0$ and the origin of {4} is at ${{ex13_2.o4}}$. The distance along $z_3$ from $o_3$ to this point is $d_4 = 0.134$ m, which is exactly the algebraic sum $W_1 - W_2 + W_3$ of the three sideways offsets of Chapter 12. Turning from $z_3$ (−y) about $x_4$ (+x) to $z_4$ (−z) is +90°, so $\alpha_4 = \pi/2$.

**{5}.** Axes 5 and 6 intersect; the origin of {5} is at ${{ex13_2.o5}}$, $d_5 = 0.1$ m and $\alpha_5 = -\pi/2$.

**{6}.** The centre of the flange is on axis 6 and is taken as the origin of {6}, ${{ex13_2.o6}}$; $z_6$ points the same way as $z_5$ (outwards along the normal of the flange), $d_6 = 0.1$ m, and $a_6 = \alpha_6 = 0$.

Arranging the six rows of parameters into a table gives the parts-library counterpart of the manufacturer's Table 13.3.1 (its lengths are the "Model" column of Table 13.3.3; the full table reappears as Table 13.4.2). Figure 13.2.3 shows all seven frames. Notice that the origins of {1}, {2} and {3} all lie in the plane y = 0, whereas the physical joints 2, 3 and 4 are at y = −0.138, −0.007 and −0.007 m respectively. The positions of the frames are determined by the axis lines and need not be at the centres of the physical joints, which beginners often find surprising.

The end-effector frame {b} chosen in Chapter 12 is the frame of the last link of the model, whose y-axis lies along the normal of the flange, whereas the z-axis of the DH frame {6} lies along the normal of the flange. Program 13.2.1 finds the fixed transformation between the two,

$$
T_{6b} = {{ex13_2.T6b}} = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, 90^\circ) ,
$$

in agreement with the conclusion of Section 12.3.3. After multiplying by it, the $T_{06}T_{6b}$ computed by DH differs from the product of exponentials by at most ${{ex13_2.err_ur_poe:sci1}}$ over ten thousand random sets of joint angles, and from the parts-library model by at most ${{ex13_2.err_ur_model:sci1}}$.

### 13.2.6 Steps for setting up frames in modified DH

The steps of modified DH differ from those of standard DH in only one respect: {i} is placed on axis i rather than on axis i+1. Specifically, step 4 becomes: $z_i$ lies along axis i; the origin is where $N_i$ meets axis i; $x_i$ lies along $N_i$. {0} is usually chosen to coincide with {1} at the zero position, so that the first row has $a_0 = \alpha_0 = 0$ and $d_1 = 0$; alternatively, as Franka does, {0} may be placed on the underside of the base, and $d_1$ in the first row is then the shoulder height. The end-effector frame {n} is on axis n, and the tool is reached by multiplying by a further fixed transformation.

The way the parameters are read off changes accordingly: the $a_{i-1}$ and $\alpha_{i-1}$ of row i are read off from the x- and z-axes of {i−1} and {i} by Eqs. (13.2.1) and (13.2.2), with the subscripts shifted forward by one. By Theorem 13.1.2, if a standard DH table is already available, moving the a and α columns down one row gives the modified DH table for the same set of common normals. Section 13.4 will take the Panda as an example.

### 13.2.7 An everyday example

Drawing frames on an unfamiliar mechanism has something in common with settling the generations in a family tree: the rules must be agreed beforehand, such as "the eldest son goes on the left" and "count down from the grandparents", otherwise two people may each draw a correct family tree and still find that the two do not match. The steps for setting up DH frames are just such a set of rules agreed in advance. The freedom the rules leave, such as where to take the common normal of parallel axes, is like the order in which brothers of the same generation are arranged in the family tree: it does not change who is whose father; but once it has been chosen, the whole table must be read with that same choice.

We can now set up the frames of a robot and write down its parameter table. The next section goes the other way and reads a parameter table written by a manufacturer: the official DH parameters of the UR5e.

**Engineer's notes**

- Before setting up the frames, draw a sketch of the zero position, drawing each joint axis as a straight line and marking its positive direction. Most errors in setting up frames come from taking the position of the physical joint for the position of the axis line.
- Taking $d_i = 0$ for parallel axes is the default rule of textbooks; industrial tables often choose differently for convenience, and then have non-zero $d_i$ or joint offsets. Both kinds of table are correct; when comparing them, look only at whether the end-effector poses agree.
- As soon as the frames are set up, make two checks: whether the origin of {n} at the zero position is the centre of the flange; and, when only one joint turns, which frames stay still and which turn with it. The latter reveals whether some {i} has been put on the wrong link.
- The variable of a prismatic joint is in the d column, and that of a revolute joint in the θ column. In a program, distinguish them with an array of "joint types", rather than guessing from "whether the d in this row is a variable".
