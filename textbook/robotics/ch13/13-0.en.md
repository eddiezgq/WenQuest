Chapter 12 solved forward kinematics with the product-of-exponentials formula: write down the zero-position pose M and the screw axis of each joint, and multiply them in turn. Yet if you open a robot maker's technical manual, or the parameter page of an industrial controller, what you usually see is not screw axes but a table with four columns: one row per joint, with the columns headed θ, a, d, α. This table is the DH parameter table. It was proposed by Jacques Denavit and Richard S. Hartenberg in 1955, and for seventy years it has been the common language in industry for describing the geometry of serial arms; robotics courses and examinations, in China and elsewhere, also generally require students to be able to set up link frames and write a DH parameter table. This book takes the product of exponentials as its main line, but the reader must be able to read, write and convert DH parameters in order to use manufacturers' documentation and to talk with engineers and colleagues. This chapter is that bridge.

The idea of the DH method is this: fix a frame on each link according to rules, so that the transformation between two adjacent frames can be written with only four numbers. Of these four numbers, two describe the shape of the link itself (the distance and the angle between two joint axes), and two describe the joint (a distance along the axis and an angle about it). The heart of the rules is the common normal of two joint axes. There are two versions of the DH method in common use: standard DH, which keeps the original meaning of Denavit–Hartenberg and was popularized in robotics by Richard P. Paul, and modified DH, adopted by John J. Craig in his 1986 textbook. In the two versions the four parameters mean the same thing, but the frames are placed at different ends of the link, and the factors in the formula are multiplied in a different order. Not knowing which convention a table was written in is one of the most common mistakes in practice.

The chapter is organized as follows:

- Section 13.1 starts from the common normal of two lines, introduces the four DH parameters, derives the transformation matrices of standard DH and modified DH, and proves that the two conventions differ only by one fixed transformation;
- Section 13.2 gives the steps for setting up link frames and the treatment of special cases such as parallel axes, intersecting axes and prismatic joints, and sets up the frames of the SCARA and the UR5e;
- Section 13.3 reads the UR5e parameter table published by Universal Robots, explains the intent behind entries in manufacturers' manuals such as negative values of a and zero offsets, and analyses quantitatively where its difference of about one millimetre from the parts-library model comes from;
- Section 13.4 proves that DH parameters and the product of exponentials can be converted into each other, gives algorithms for both directions, and verifies them with the UR5e (standard DH) and the Panda (modified DH);
- Section 13.5 analyses the limitations of the DH method, in particular the jump in the parameters when adjacent joint axes are nearly parallel, and the modification proposed for this by Samad Hayati.

This chapter uses from Chapter 12 the screw axes, the product-of-exponentials formula (12.2.3), the similarity transformation (12.2.4) and the adjoint matrix (12.2.6); from Chapter 5 the homogeneous transformation, the subscript-cancellation rule (Theorem 5.3.1) and "motion relative to the body's own frame postmultiplies" (Theorem 5.3.3); and from Chapter 4 the elementary rotations about coordinate axes. This chapter uses the same UR5e and the same parts-library model as Chapter 12, so all the numbers in the book can be checked against Chapter 12. The notation of Chapter 13 is mainly this book's subscript notation; where a comparison with Craig's textbook is needed, his left-superscript notation is given together with a table for converting between the two.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. find the common normal of two joint axes, state the geometric meaning of the four DH parameters, and write the link transformation matrices of standard DH and modified DH;
2. set up the link frames of a serial arm step by step and write its DH parameter table, handling parallel axes, intersecting axes and prismatic joints correctly;
3. read a DH parameter table published by a manufacturer, decide which convention it uses, and use it to compute forward kinematics;
4. convert between DH parameters and the screw axes of the product of exponentials, and compute the same robot by both methods so that each checks the other;
5. explain the jump in the DH parameters at nearly parallel axes and its effect on kinematic calibration, and know the role of the Hayati parameters and of the product of exponentials in calibration.
