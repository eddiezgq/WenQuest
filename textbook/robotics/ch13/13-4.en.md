### 13.4.1 The problem

In an engineering team the two descriptions often exist side by side. The controller, the manufacturer's manual and the calibration report give a DH parameter table, while the programs of this book, many simulation packages and modern calibration methods use screw axes. So there is a need in both directions: given the manufacturer's DH table, we want to use the product of exponentials for velocity analysis or trajectory planning (the Jacobian matrix of Chapter 16 follows directly from the screw axes); or, given screw axes read from a CAD model, we need to write out a DH table for the controller, or for an examination.

Section 12.4.3 of Chapter 12 has already compared the two methods geometrically. This section gives exact formulas for converting between them, proves that the forward kinematics before and after conversion is exactly the same, and verifies this on the UR5e (standard DH) and the Panda (modified DH).

### 13.4.2 From DH to the product of exponentials

Look first at one joint. By Eq. (13.1.4), the link transformation of standard DH is $T_{i-1,i} = Z_i X_i$, where $Z_i = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \theta_i)\,\mathrm{Trans}(\hat z, d_i)$ contains the joint variable and $X_i$ is constant. Let joint i be a revolute joint; by Eq. (13.2.3), $\theta_i = \theta_i^{\mathrm c} + \delta_i$. Two rotations about the same axis can be combined, so $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \theta_i^{\mathrm c} + \delta_i) = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \theta_i^{\mathrm c})\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \delta_i)$. And a rotation through $\theta$ about the frame's own z-axis (through the origin) is just the matrix exponential of the screw axis $\hat{\mathcal Z} = (0, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0)$ (Eq. (12.1.5) with ω = (0, 0, 1), q = 0, v = 0). Hence

$$
Z_i(\theta_i^{\mathrm c}) = e^{[\hat{\mathcal Z}]\theta_i^{\mathrm c}}\, Z_i(0) , \tag{13.4.1}
$$

where $Z_i(0)$ is $Z_i$ with the joint variable at zero. For a prismatic joint, $d_i = \theta_i^{\mathrm c} + \delta_i$; a translation along z commutes with a rotation about z, and Eq. (13.4.1) follows in the same way, only with $\hat{\mathcal Z}$ replaced by $\hat{\mathcal Z}_{\mathrm P} = (0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1)$.

**Theorem 13.4.1 (Standard DH → product of exponentials)** Suppose the standard DH parameter table of an arm (including the joint offsets) is known, with {0} as the space frame and {n} as the end-effector frame. Write $T_{0,i-1}(0)$ for the poses of the link frames at the zero position. Then its product-of-exponentials formula is $T_{0n}(\theta) = e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}\cdots e^{[\mathcal S_n]\theta_n} M$, where

$$
\mathcal S_i = [\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{0,i-1}(0)}]\,\hat{\mathcal Z} \ \ (\text{revolute joint; for a prismatic joint use}\ \hat{\mathcal Z}_{\mathrm P}), \qquad M = T_{0n}(0) . \tag{13.4.2}
$$

Here $\theta_i$ is the controller's joint variable $\theta_i^{\mathrm c}$; the superscript is dropped for brevity.

*Proof* Write $C_i = Z_i(0)\,X_i = T_{i-1,i}(0)$, which is a constant matrix. By Eq. (13.4.1),

$$
T_{0n}(\theta) = e^{[\hat{\mathcal Z}]\theta_1} C_1\, e^{[\hat{\mathcal Z}]\theta_2} C_2 \cdots e^{[\hat{\mathcal Z}]\theta_n} C_n .
$$

Write $P_i = C_1 C_2 \cdots C_i = T_{0i}(0)$, $P_0 = I$. Insert $P_{i-1}$ and $P_{i-1}^{-1}$ on the two sides of each exponential:

$$
T_{0n}(\theta) = \big(P_0\, e^{[\hat{\mathcal Z}]\theta_1} P_0^{-1}\big)\big(P_1\, e^{[\hat{\mathcal Z}]\theta_2} P_1^{-1}\big) \cdots \big(P_{n-1}\, e^{[\hat{\mathcal Z}]\theta_n} P_{n-1}^{-1}\big)\, P_n .
$$

This step is only an identity: the product $P_{i-1}^{-1} P_i$ between two adjacent brackets is exactly $C_i$. By the property of similarity transformations (12.2.4) (with P replaced by $P^{-1}$), $P\,e^{A}P^{-1} = e^{PAP^{-1}}$; and by Eq. (12.2.7), $P[\hat{\mathcal Z}]P^{-1} = \big[[\mathrm{Ad}_P]\hat{\mathcal Z}\big]$. So the i-th bracket equals $e^{[\mathcal S_i]\theta_i}$, with $\mathcal S_i$ as in Eq. (13.4.2); and the final $P_n = T_{0n}(0) = M$. ∎

The geometric meaning of Eq. (13.4.2) is very clear. By the adjoint matrix of Eq. (12.2.6), if $T_{0,i-1}(0) = (R, p)$, then $[\mathrm{Ad}_{T}]\hat{\mathcal Z} = (R\hat z,\ [p]R\hat z)$. $R\hat z$ is the direction of $z_{i-1}$ at the zero position, p is the origin $o_{i-1}$ of {i−1} at the zero position, and $[p]R\hat z = p \times z_{i-1} = -z_{i-1} \times o_{i-1}$. Therefore

$$
\mathcal S_i = \begin{pmatrix} z_{i-1}(0) \\ -z_{i-1}(0) \times o_{i-1}(0) \end{pmatrix}\ \text{(revolute)}, \quad \mathcal S_i = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ z_{i-1}(0) \end{pmatrix}\ \text{(prismatic)}. \tag{13.4.3}
$$

These are exactly Eqs. (12.1.3) and (12.1.4): in standard DH, $z_{i-1}$ lies along the axis of joint i, and $o_{i-1}$ lies on that axis. All that Theorem 13.4.1 says is that, at the zero position, the DH link frames have already "marked" every joint axis in space; reading them off gives the screw axes.

For modified DH, the axis of joint i is $z_i$, and the origin of $\{i\}$ lies on that axis. The same derivation gives:

**Corollary 13.4.1 (Modified DH → product of exponentials)** For a modified DH parameter table,

$$
\mathcal S_i = [\mathrm{Ad}_{T_{0i}(0)}]\,\hat{\mathcal Z} \ \ (\text{for a prismatic joint use}\ \hat{\mathcal Z}_{\mathrm P}), \qquad M = T_{0n}(0)\,T_{nb} . \tag{13.4.4}
$$

*Proof* The link transformation of modified DH is $T_{i-1,i} = X_{i-1} Z_i$, with the variable part $Z_i$ on the far right. Let $C_i = X_{i-1} Z_i(0)$; since $Z_i(\theta) = e^{[\hat{\mathcal Z}]\theta} Z_i(0) = Z_i(0)\,e^{[\hat{\mathcal Z}]\theta}$ (motions along the same z-axis commute), $T_{0n} = C_1 e^{[\hat{\mathcal Z}]\theta_1} C_2 e^{[\hat{\mathcal Z}]\theta_2} \cdots$. Inserting $P_i = C_1\cdots C_i = T_{0i}(0)$ as in Theorem 13.4.1 gives the result. ∎

### 13.4.3 From the product of exponentials to DH

Conversely, given the screw axes $\mathcal S_i = (\hat\omega_i, v_i)$ and M, we want the DH parameter table. By Section 13.2, setting up the frames needs only the direction of each joint axis and a point on it. The direction is $\hat\omega_i$; a point on the axis can be computed directly from $v_i$. Since $v = -\hat\omega \times q$, taking the cross product of both sides with $\hat\omega$ and using the vector triple product formula $\hat\omega \times (\hat\omega \times q) = (\hat\omega \cdot q)\hat\omega - q$ gives

$$
q^{*} = \hat\omega \times v = q - (\hat\omega \cdot q)\,\hat\omega . \tag{13.4.5}
$$

The right-hand side is q minus its component along the axis, so $q^{*}$ is the point on the axis closest to the origin. Eq. (13.4.5) turns a screw axis back into a line.

So the steps for "product of exponentials → DH" are:

1. For each revolute joint, find $q_i^{*}$ by Eq. (13.4.5), giving the line $(\hat\omega_i, q_i^{*})$; a prismatic joint has only the direction $v_i$, and by Section 13.2.3 it is placed wherever is most convenient;
2. Check whether the z-axis of {s} lies along axis 1. If it does, take {0} = {s}; otherwise choose {0} separately and record the fixed transformation $T_{s0}$;
3. Following steps 2–6 of Section 13.2.2, find the common normals of adjacent axes with Eqs. (13.1.1)–(13.1.3), and read off the parameters with Eqs. (13.2.1) and (13.2.2);
4. Put the origin of {n} at the origin of M (if the origin of M lies on axis n), with $z_n$ parallel to $z_{n-1}$, and find the fixed transformation of the end-effector from $T_{nb} = T_{0n}(0)^{-1} M$.

### 13.4.5 The two descriptions compared

The conversion formulas also reveal how the two descriptions compare in their number of parameters. The screw axis of a revolute joint has 6 numbers, but they satisfy two constraints, $\|\hat\omega\| = 1$ and $\hat\omega \cdot v = 0$, so only 4 are independent, exactly the number needed to fix a line in space, matching the 4 parameters of DH. The screw axis (0, v) of a prismatic joint has only 2 independent quantities (a direction), yet DH still writes 4 numbers, two of which describe "the position of the axis", and that position can be chosen at will. Adding the 6 numbers of the end-effector pose, the total number of independent parameters of the product of exponentials is 4R + 2P + 6, where R and P are the numbers of revolute and prismatic joints. This number is no coincidence. In 1987, studying kinematic models for calibration, Louis J. Everett, Morris Driels and Bruce W. Mooring put forward two requirements: a model should be "complete", that is, able to represent any possible deviation of the robot's geometry; and it should be "minimal", that is, contain no redundant parameters that cannot be determined from measurements. They showed that a model of a serial robot that meets both requirements has 4R + 2P + 6 independent parameters. The product-of-exponentials model (counting its independent parameters) reaches exactly this number.

| | DH parameter method | Product of exponentials |
|---|---|---|
| One revolute joint | 4: a, α, d, θ offset | 6 numbers, 2 constraints, 4 independent |
| One prismatic joint | 4, of which 2 can be chosen at will | 3 numbers, 1 constraint, 2 independent |
| Base frame | The z-axis of {0} must lie along axis 1; otherwise add $T_{s0}$ | {s} chosen at will |
| End-effector frame | Add $T_{nb}$ (6) | Included in M (6) |
| Converted from the other | By the steps of Section 13.4.3; special cases need choices | Eqs. (13.4.2)–(13.4.4), written in one step |

The last row of the table shows the asymmetry of the conversion: DH to the product of exponentials is a single formula, with no choices at all; the product of exponentials to DH requires the common normals, conventions must be made when axes intersect, are parallel or coincide, and the result is not unique (Theorem 13.2.1). The next section will show that "a convention must be made for parallel axes" is not just a nuisance: in calibration it causes serious problems.

### 13.4.6 An everyday example

There are two ways of telling someone a route. One is step by step: "Turn right out of the door, walk two hundred metres to the second crossing and turn left, then walk another hundred metres…"; each sentence is relative to the position and heading reached by the one before. This is the DH product. The other is to mark the coordinates of every crossing on a map. This is the product of exponentials: all the joint axes are marked on the same "map", {s}. The two describe the same route and can be converted into each other: walk the route once following the step-by-step directions, and you can mark every crossing on the map (Theorem 13.4.1); conversely, to write step-by-step directions from the crossings on the map, you must decide where each leg is measured from and which way counts as positive. These are the steps of Section 13.4.3.

---

**Engineer's notes**

- When you have a manufacturer's DH table and want to use the methods of this book for velocity analysis or planning, convert it to screw axes once with Eq. (13.4.2) or (13.4.4), and from then on compute only with the screw axes, so as not to mix two sets of parameters.
- After converting, always do two things: compare the zero-position end-effector with the $T_{0n}(0)$ of the original table; and compare the end-effector given by the two methods at a few random sets of joint angles. These two checks take only a few lines of code, and catch the great majority of mistakes in convention, units and the sign of the x-axes.
- When generating a DH table from a CAD or simulation model (URDF, MJCF), first turn each joint axis back into a line with Eq. (13.4.5), then set up the frames as in Section 13.2. Do not copy the offsets between links in the URDF straight into a and d: the link frames of a URDF generally do not obey the DH rules.
- The end-effector frames of the two descriptions are often different (the {6} and {b} of the UR5e differ by Rot(x, 90°), and the flange and hand frames of the Panda by Rot(z, −45°)). Before comparing two sets of data, first make sure that the end-effector means the same frame in both.
