### 13.5.1 The problem: link offsets hundreds of metres long

Among the robot drivers that UR provides for ROS 2 there is a tool that reads the factory calibration parameters from the controller. Its documentation shows the DH parameters read from a real robot, in which the link offsets of joints 2, 3 and 4 are

$$
d_2 = {{ex13_5.cal_d2:.2f}}\ \mathrm{m}, \qquad d_3 = {{ex13_5.cal_d3:.2f}}\ \mathrm{m}, \qquad d_4 = {{ex13_5.cal_d4:.2f}}\ \mathrm{m} .
$$

The documentation describes what this means in one sentence: the upper arm is placed 439 m away from the base, and the forearm 7 m away on the other side. An arm with a reach of a little over one metre, yet calibrated parameters of hundreds of metres: clearly this is not a measurement gone wrong. The documentation then gives a correction algorithm that replaces these parameters with reasonable-looking values while leaving the pose of the end-effector unchanged.

This section explains the phenomenon. It comes from an inherent flaw of the DH method: when two adjacent joint axes are nearly parallel, the DH parameters are extremely sensitive to small changes in the geometry, and even discontinuous. This is the last problem discussed in this chapter, and one of the important reasons why this book takes the product of exponentials as its main line.

### 13.5.2 Two nearly parallel axes

Take joints 2 and 3 of the UR5e as an example. Seen in {1}, axis 2 is the $z_1$ axis; axis 3 is nominally parallel to it, at a distance a = {{ex13_5.A:.3f}} m (the length of the upper arm), passing through the point (a, 0, 0). By the rule of Section 13.2.3, for parallel axes the common normal through the origin is taken, so $a_2 = a$ and $d_2 = 0$.

The axes of a real robot are never exactly parallel. Suppose axis 3 is tilted by a small angle ε within the plane of the two axes, so that its direction becomes $u = (\sin\varepsilon,\ 0,\ \cos\varepsilon)$, still passing through (a, 0, 0). Two lines in the same plane that are not parallel must intersect. Setting the x-coordinate of the point $(a + s\sin\varepsilon,\ 0,\ s\cos\varepsilon)$ of axis 3 to zero gives $s = -a/\sin\varepsilon$, and the height of the intersection is $s\cos\varepsilon$. So by the DH rules the common normal degenerates to this intersection point (Theorem 13.1.1), and

$$
a_2 = 0, \qquad \alpha_2 = \varepsilon, \qquad d_2 = -a\cot\varepsilon \approx -\frac{a}{\varepsilon}\quad (\varepsilon \to 0) . \tag{13.5.1}
$$

ε is in radians. With a tilt of only 0.01° (about $1.7 \times 10^{-4}$ rad), $d_2 = {{ex13_5.d01:.0f}}$ m. If the tilt is to the other side, ε = −0.01°, then $d_2 = {{ex13_5.d_neg:.0f}}$ m. Figure 13.5.1(a) shows this case (with the tilt exaggerated).

**Theorem 13.5.1** Near a geometry in which two adjacent axes are parallel, the DH parameters are not continuous functions of the geometry of the axes: when one axis is tilted by ε within the plane of the two axes, $d_i \to -\infty$ as $\varepsilon \to 0^{+}$ and $d_i \to +\infty$ as $\varepsilon \to 0^{-}$, while at ε = 0 the rule gives $d_i = 0$; the link length $a_i$ jumps from a to 0 at ε = 0.

*Proof* By Eq. (13.5.1), $\cot\varepsilon$ tends to ±∞ as ε → 0, with the same sign as ε. For ε ≠ 0 the two axes intersect and $a_i = 0$; for ε = 0 they are parallel and $a_i = a$. ∎

The direction of the tilt matters too. If axis 3 is tilted perpendicular to the plane of the two axes, that is, $u = (0,\ -\sin\varepsilon,\ \cos\varepsilon)$, the two lines become skew lines; the common normal still runs in the x direction and is still at z = 0, so $a_2 = a$ and $d_2 = 0$, only $\alpha_2 = \varepsilon$, and everything is continuous. The jump comes only from the "in-plane" component of the tilt; and manufacturing errors always have an in-plane component, so real calibration results almost always run into it.

We can also look at the sensitivity of d to ε. By Eq. (13.5.1),

$$
\frac{\mathrm d d_2}{\mathrm d\varepsilon} = \frac{a}{\sin^2\varepsilon} . \tag{13.5.2}
$$

Near ε = 0.01°, a further change of 0.001° in ε changes $d_2$ by {{ex13_5.dd_sens:.0f}} m. The actual shape of the robot has hardly changed, yet the parameter has changed by more than two hundred metres.

### 13.5.3 The effect on calibration: Hayati parameters

Why is a jump in the parameters a practical problem? As Section 13.3.4 said, kinematic calibration is a nonlinear least-squares problem: the model parameters are adjusted with measured data so that the end-effector positions computed by the model agree as closely as possible with the measured ones. Solving it needs the derivatives of the end-effector position with respect to each parameter. At two axes that are nominally parallel, the real geometry differs from the nominal one only by a tiny angle, yet the DH parameter may be anywhere between "d = 0" and "d = hundreds of metres". The iteration starts from the nominal value d = 0, and every correction to d is huge and unstable; Eq. (13.5.2) shows that the parameter change corresponding to a tiny change in the geometry can be meaninglessly large. The result is that the iteration does not converge, or converges to a physically absurd set of parameters, just like the example of Section 13.5.1.

Samad Hayati pointed out this problem in a paper of 1983 and proposed the following: for two adjacent axes that are nominally parallel, do not use the common normal; instead fix the frame by the point where axis i+1 meets the xy plane of {i−1}, and describe the tilt of the axis by an angle β about the y-axis in place of d. Then, in a paper of 1985, Hayati and M. Mirmirani wrote it as the following link transformation:

$$
T_{i-1,i} = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \theta_i)\,\mathrm{Trans}(\hat x, a_i)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, \alpha_i)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat y, \beta_i) . \tag{13.5.3}
$$

These parameters are called the **Hayati parameters**. In the example above, the point where axis 3 meets the plane z = 0 is always at (a, 0, 0), so $a_2 = a$ does not change, and the tilt is represented entirely by $\beta_2 = \varepsilon$ (Figure 13.5.1(b)). The parameters change continuously with the geometry, and the least-squares problem becomes well conditioned.

The Hayati parameters have their own singular case: if axis i+1 is parallel to the xy plane of {i−1} (that is, the two axes are perpendicular), there is no intersection point. So practical calibration models are usually mixed: Hayati parameters for links whose two adjacent axes are nearly parallel, and DH parameters for the other links. A word of warning: in the calibration literature the Hayati parameters are sometimes also called "modified DH", the same name as Craig's modified DH but with a completely different meaning. In this book "modified DH" refers only to Craig's convention.

Back to the data of Section 13.5.1. The documentation does not state the model of the robot; the identification of the model below is this book's inference from the values, not a statement of the documentation. Its $d_1 = {{ex13_5.cal_d1:.4f}}$ m, $d_5 = {{ex13_5.cal_d5:.4f}}$ m and $d_6 = {{ex13_5.cal_d6:.4f}}$ m each differ by less than 1 mm from the nominal values {{ex13_5.n_d1:.4f}} m, {{ex13_5.n_d5:.5f}} m and {{ex13_5.n_d6:.5f}} m of the UR10e (which shares a column with the UR12e) on the UR web page. On that page only two columns have $d_1$ = {{ex13_5.n_d1:.4f}} m, the UR10e/UR12e and the UR16e; the two have the same offsets and differ in the upper arm: that of the UR16e is {{ex13_5.ur16e_a2:.4f}} m, while the length of the upper arm found from these data in Example 13.5.2 below is about 0.612 m, which matches the UR10e/UR12e. So this is very probably a UR10e, or a UR12e, whose geometry is the same. The artificial example that the documentation goes on to use in explaining the correction algorithm is built from the nominal parameters of the UR10e, which also agrees with this inference. ### 13.5.4 Other limitations of the DH method

The jump in the parameters is the most striking problem, but not the only one. Gathering together the situations met throughout this chapter, the DH method has the following further limitations.

**Too many conventions and choices.** Two conventions, standard DH and modified DH, exist side by side, and within one convention the sign of the x-axis, the position of the common normal of parallel axes and the x direction for coincident axes can all be chosen freely (Section 13.2.3). The same robot therefore has many parameter tables, all correct yet all different. This is a burden for communication and for finding mistakes; the mistakes of Section 13.1.7 arise from this.

**Redundant parameters for prismatic joints.** A prismatic joint has only a direction, and the position of its axis is meaningless, yet DH still writes 4 parameters for it, two of which can be chosen at will (Section 13.4.5). In calibration these parameters cannot be determined from measurements and must be removed from the model beforehand; otherwise the least-squares problem is singular.

**The base and the tool need extra transformations.** DH requires the z-axis of {0} to lie along axis 1 and the origin of {n} to lie on axis n. The actual base frame of a robot mounted on a workbench, and the tool centre point, generally do not meet these requirements, so a general 6-parameter transformation, no longer of DH form, has to be added at each end.

**Tree-shaped and closed-chain structures.** DH describes a single serial chain: each link has only one "next" joint. The trunk of the Go2 quadruped robot is connected to four legs, the trunk of the G1 humanoid robot to two arms and two legs, and the three branches of a Delta parallel mechanism meet at the end-effector; none of these is a single serial chain. For such structures, Khalil and Kleinfinger in 1986 extended modified DH to a notation with two extra parameters per link; the product of exponentials just writes the screw axes of each branch separately, with the form unchanged (Chapters 18 and 20).

**Frames detached from the hardware.** DH frames are determined by the axes and often do not lie on the physical link (in Figure 13.2.3 the origins of {2} and {3} are not at the joint centres, and in the example of Section 13.5.1 they are hundreds of metres away). The mass, centre of mass and collision shape of a link are all given in CAD frames, so using DH needs one more layer of transformations. Modern model formats such as URDF and MJCF all allow link frames to be placed anywhere, and do not require the DH rules to be followed.

In contrast with these limitations, the screw axes of the product of exponentials are given directly by the direction of the axis and a point on it, and change continuously with the geometry (the last column of Table 13.5.1); they need no intermediate frames, use the same formula for revolute and prismatic joints, and have exactly 4R + 2P + 6 independent parameters (Section 13.4.5). Koichiro Okamura and Frank C. Park proposed a kinematic calibration method based on the product-of-exponentials formula in 1996, and since then calibration methods based on the product of exponentials (for example the local POE model of I-Ming Chen and colleagues of 2001, mentioned in Chapter 12) have been widely studied and applied.

### 13.5.5 How to treat DH

The DH method has its limitations, but it is not obsolete. Its parameter table is compact: four columns of numbers write out a whole robot; and it has been the common language of industry for seventy years, used by manufacturers' manuals, controllers, calibration reports, a large literature and examinations. A sensible approach is a division of labour:

- use DH for communication: when reading and writing manufacturers' manuals, configuring controllers or taking examinations, write the DH table according to the convention, and state whether it is standard DH or modified DH;
- use the product of exponentials for computation: forward kinematics, the Jacobian matrix and dynamics are all based on screw axes (Chapters 16 and 25 of this book), and the conversion is done once by Section 13.4;
- avoid the singularity of DH in calibration: handle parallel axes with Hayati parameters, or use a method based on the product of exponentials directly.

### 13.5.6 An everyday example

Lay two chopsticks side by side on a table, 3 cm apart, looking perfectly parallel. If you really try to find "the place where the two chopsticks are closest", a problem appears: if they are exactly parallel, they are equally close everywhere; if they are even slightly out of parallel, their extensions meet somewhere, and "the closest place" is that intersection. With a tilt of 1.7° the intersection is about 1 m away; with a tilt of 0.17°, 10 m away; nudge one chopstick gently to the other side, and the intersection jumps from beyond this end of the table to beyond the other. Describing the relative position of two chopsticks by "the closest place" is exactly what DH does when it describes two axes by their common normal. Such a description works well when the chopsticks are clearly not parallel, but very badly when they are nearly parallel.

This brings the chapter to an end. The next chapter takes up the inverse problem of forward kinematics: given the pose of the end-effector, find the joint angles. There, too, screw axes will be used, and we shall also meet classical analytic solutions written with DH parameters.

**Engineer's notes**

- If you see a d of tens or hundreds of metres among calibration parameters, first check whether the two adjacent axes are nominally parallel. If they are, this is usually not an error, just a matter of how DH writes things; do not let it make you doubt the robot or the measuring equipment.
- Before handing such calibration parameters to software that accepts only "reasonable" DH tables (for example some analytic inverse kinematics programs), first convert them into an equivalent, reasonable-looking table that describes the same geometry; the UR ROS driver does exactly this.
- When doing calibration yourself, parameterize parallel axes with Hayati parameters or with the product of exponentials, and remove beforehand any redundant parameters that cannot be identified, such as those of prismatic joints.
- When writing reports or exchanging parameters, state the convention (standard DH, Craig's modified DH, or Hayati parameters), the unit of angle, and the definitions of the base and tool frames.
