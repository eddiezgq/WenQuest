### 13.1.1 The problem: four columns of numbers in a manual

On the technical-support website of Universal Robots one can find a parameter table for the UR5e. The table has six rows, one for each joint, and lists three constants a, d and α; in the first row, for example, $d = {{ex13_3.v_d1}}$ m and $\alpha = \pi/2$. Each joint also has a fourth quantity, the joint angle θ, which is simply the joint variable θ₁, …, θ₆, so the table need not list values for it. The parameter table that Franka publishes for the Panda has all four columns, has more rows, and states that it follows "Craig's convention". Industrial controllers, offline programming software and calibration software mostly take a table of this kind as the input describing the geometry of the robot.

Chapter 12 described the same UR5e with six screw axes and a zero-position pose M. Each screw axis has 6 numbers and M has 12, which looks like far more than a DH table. Can a joint really be described with only four numbers? And how are these four numbers measured?

The key is where the frames are placed. Chapter 12 used only two frames, {s} on the base and {b} at the end-effector, and every joint axis had to be written out in full in {s}. The DH method instead fixes a frame on every link and places it according to a set of rules, so that in the transformation between two adjacent frames many of the numbers are automatically zero and only four remain. The starting point of these rules is the common normal of two joint axes.

### 13.1.2 The common normal of two lines

Every revolute joint of an arm has an axis of rotation, which is a straight line in space. Between two adjacent joints there is a link; the link is a rigid body, so the relative position of the two joint axes at its ends is fixed. To describe the shape of a link is to describe the relative position of two lines.

Two lines in space can be in one of three situations: they intersect, they are parallel, or they neither intersect nor are parallel. Lines of the third kind are called **skew lines**, and they are the most general case in an arm. For two skew lines, the most natural geometric quantities are the shortest distance between them and the angle by which their directions differ. Where is the shortest distance attained?

Let the first line pass through the point $p_1$ with direction given by the unit vector $u_1$, and the second line pass through the point $p_2$ with direction given by the unit vector $u_2$. Points on the two lines are written $f_1 = p_1 + t\,u_1$ and $f_2 = p_2 + s\,u_2$, where t and s are distances measured along the lines (in m). Write $w = p_2 - p_1$. The squared length of the vector $f_2 - f_1 = w + s\,u_2 - t\,u_1$ joining the two points is a quadratic function of t and s, and it is a minimum where its partial derivatives with respect to t and s are zero. Differentiating and tidying up, these two conditions say exactly that $f_2 - f_1$ is perpendicular to both lines: $(f_2 - f_1)\cdot u_1 = 0$, $(f_2 - f_1)\cdot u_2 = 0$. Expanding them gives a system of linear equations in t and s:

$$
\begin{aligned}
t - (u_1 \cdot u_2)\,s &= w \cdot u_1, \\
(u_1 \cdot u_2)\,t - s &= w \cdot u_2 .
\end{aligned} \tag{13.1.1}
$$

Its determinant of coefficients is $(u_1 \cdot u_2)^2 - 1$. Since $u_1$ and $u_2$ are unit vectors, $|u_1 \times u_2|^2 = 1 - (u_1 \cdot u_2)^2$, so the determinant equals $-|u_1 \times u_2|^2$. As long as the two lines are not parallel, the cross product is non-zero and the system (13.1.1) has a unique solution.

**Definition 13.1.1 (Common normal)** The line segment that is perpendicular to two lines and meets both of them is called the **common normal** of the two lines.

**Theorem 13.1.1** Two non-parallel lines have a unique common normal, and its length is the shortest distance between the two lines. Let n be a unit vector perpendicular to both $u_1$ and $u_2$; then the common normal lies along n and its length is

$$
a = w \cdot n , \tag{13.1.2}
$$

with a ≥ 0 when n is taken to point from the first line towards the second. The angle α through which the direction of the first line is turned about n into the direction of the second line satisfies

$$
\cos\alpha = u_1 \cdot u_2, \qquad \sin\alpha = (u_1 \times u_2)\cdot n . \tag{13.1.3}
$$

When the two lines intersect, the common normal has zero length and degenerates into a line through the point of intersection along $u_1 \times u_2$; when the two lines are parallel, there are infinitely many common normals, all of the same length.

*Proof* When the lines are not parallel, the system (13.1.1) has a unique solution (t, s), and the $f_1$ and $f_2$ obtained from it make $f_2 - f_1$ perpendicular to both lines, so $f_1 f_2$ is a common normal; conversely, the two end points of any common normal satisfy (13.1.1), so the common normal is unique. The squared distance is a quadratic function of t and s whose quadratic part $|s\,u_2 - t\,u_1|^2$ is always positive for (t, s) ≠ 0, so the stationary point is the minimum. $f_2 - f_1$ is perpendicular to both $u_1$ and $u_2$ and is therefore parallel to n; write $f_2 - f_1 = a\,n$. Taking the dot product of both sides with n and noting that $u_1 \cdot n = u_2 \cdot n = 0$ gives $a = (w + s\,u_2 - t\,u_1)\cdot n = w \cdot n$, which is Eq. (13.1.2). The first part of Eq. (13.1.3) is the definition of the dot product; in the second part, $u_1 \times u_2$ is parallel to n with length equal to the absolute value of sin α, and α is positive when it points the same way as n. The intersecting and parallel cases can be checked directly: when the lines intersect, the point of intersection can serve as both $p_1$ and $p_2$, so that $w = 0$ and $w \cdot n = 0$ (using other points on the lines only adds components along $u_1$ and $u_2$ to w, and does not change $w \cdot n$); when they are parallel, the two equations of (13.1.1) are linearly dependent and t can be chosen freely.　∎

The sign of α in Eq. (13.1.3) depends on the choice of direction of n: reversing n changes the sign of α, and also the sign of a. We shall meet this again in Section 13.3 when reading a manufacturer's parameter table.

**Example 13.1.1** Find the common normal of the axes of joints 3 and 4 of the Panda.

From Table 12.3.1, at the zero position the axis of joint 3 points vertically upwards, $u_1 = {{ex13_1.w3}}$, and passes through the point $p_1 = (0,\ 0,\ 0.649)$ m; the axis of joint 4 is horizontal, $u_2 = {{ex13_1.w4}}$, and passes through the point $p_2 = (0.0825,\ 0,\ 0.649)$ m. The directions of the two axes are neither the same nor opposite, and the axes do not intersect, so they are skew lines. $u_1 \times u_2 = (0, 0, 1) \times (0, -1, 0) = (1, 0, 0)$; take $n = {{ex13_1.n}}$, which happens to point from axis 3 to axis 4. By Eq. (13.1.2),

$$
a = w \cdot n = (0.0825,\ 0,\ 0) \cdot (1,\ 0,\ 0) = {{ex13_1.dist:.4f}}\ \mathrm{m} .
$$

Solving the system (13.1.1) gives t = s = 0, so the two feet of the common normal are $p_1$ and $p_2$ themselves. By Eq. (13.1.3), cos α = 0 and sin α = 1, so α = {{ex13_1.alpha35:.0f}}°: turning the vertical axis 3 through 90° about the x-axis brings it exactly onto axis 4, which points along −y. Program 13.1.1 also computes the result independently by a numerical method, "alternately finding the closest point on each of the two lines", and obtains the same feet and the same distance. Section 13.4 will show that these two numbers are exactly the a and α of row 4 of Franka's parameter table.

### 13.1.3 One link, one joint, four parameters

Now let us apply the common normal to an arm. Following the convention of Chapter 12, the joints are numbered 1, 2, …, n outwards from the base. Link i connects joint i and joint i+1, and the two joint axes at its ends are called axis i and axis i+1. The common normal of axis i and axis i+1 is written $N_i$.

By Theorem 13.1.1, the shape of link i is fixed by two numbers: the length of the common normal $N_i$, and the angle about $N_i$ from axis i to axis i+1. Once the link has been made, they no longer change.

Next consider joint i. Axis i carries the feet of two common normals: the foot of $N_{i-1}$ (from link i−1) and the foot of $N_i$ (belonging to link i). When joint i turns, link i turns about axis i and $N_i$ turns with it about axis i, while $N_{i-1}$ stays still. So the state of joint i is also fixed by two numbers: the distance along axis i between the two feet, and the angle about axis i from $N_{i-1}$ to $N_i$.

**Definition 13.1.2 (DH parameters)** For link i and joint i, the four **Denavit–Hartenberg parameters** (DH parameters) are defined as follows:

- the **link length** $a_i$: the length of the common normal $N_i$, that is, the distance between axis i and axis i+1, in m;
- the **link twist** $\alpha_i$: the angle about $N_i$ from the direction of axis i to the direction of axis i+1, in rad;
- the **link offset** $d_i$: the distance along axis i from the foot of $N_{i-1}$ to the foot of $N_i$, in m;
- the **joint angle** $\theta_i$: the angle about axis i from the direction of $N_{i-1}$ to the direction of $N_i$, in rad.

For a revolute joint $\theta_i$ is the joint variable and $d_i$ is a constant; for a prismatic joint $d_i$ is the joint variable and $\theta_i$ is a constant. $a_i$ and $\alpha_i$ are always constants.

Figure 13.1.1 shows three adjacent joint axes and two common normals. Of the four parameters, $a_i$ and $\alpha_i$ are measured at the common normal $N_i$, and $d_i$ and $\theta_i$ are measured on axis i. One point about the "names" needs a word of warning: the link length $a_i$ is the distance between two axes, and is not always the length of the physical link. On the UR5e, the two axes at the ends of the upper arm are parallel, and $a_i$ is the length of the upper arm; but the axes of shoulder joints 1 and 2 intersect, so $a_1 = 0$ for link 1, even though the physical link 1 is more than a hundred millimetres tall.

So far: one link plus one joint, four numbers. What remains is to turn these four numbers into the 4×4 homogeneous transformation between two adjacent frames. This needs a convention for where the frames are placed, and there are two such conventions.

### 13.1.4 Standard DH

**Definition 13.1.3 (Link frames of standard DH)** On link i fix a frame {i} (i = 1, …, n−1) according to these rules:

1. the $z_i$ axis lies along axis i+1;
2. the $x_i$ axis lies along the common normal $N_i$, pointing from axis i to axis i+1;
3. the origin $o_i$ is at the point where $N_i$ meets axis i+1 (the foot of the normal);
4. $y_i = z_i \times x_i$, making a right-handed frame.

The $z_0$ axis of the base frame {0} lies along axis 1, and the end-effector frame {n} is chosen by the rules of Section 13.2. Frames fixed on the links in this way are called **link frames**, and the DH method based on these rules is called **standard DH**.

Note that by these rules {i} is fixed on link i, but its z-axis is the axis of the next joint (joint i+1). This is the most confusing feature of standard DH, and one of the reasons why modified DH appeared later.

With frames placed in this way, the four parameters of Definition 13.1.2 can be read directly from the coordinate axes: $a_i$ is the distance along $x_i$ from $z_{i-1}$ to $z_i$; $\alpha_i$ is the angle about $x_i$ from $z_{i-1}$ to $z_i$; $d_i$ is the distance along $z_{i-1}$ from $x_{i-1}$ to $x_i$; $\theta_i$ is the angle about $z_{i-1}$ from $x_{i-1}$ to $x_i$.

**From {i−1} to {i}.** Imagine a frame that starts at {i−1} and reaches {i} through four elementary motions (Figure 13.1.1(a)):

1. rotate through $\theta_i$ about $z_{i-1}$, so that the x-axis becomes parallel to $N_i$;
2. translate by $d_i$ along the z-axis, so that the origin reaches the foot of $N_i$ on axis i;
3. translate by $a_i$ along the new x-axis (that is, along $N_i$), so that the origin reaches axis i+1;
4. rotate through $\alpha_i$ about the x-axis, so that the z-axis comes to lie along axis i+1.

Each step is made about (or along) the current axes of the moving frame itself. By Theorem 5.3.3, motions relative to the body's own frame postmultiply in turn (Theorem 4.2.2 of Chapter 4 is the special case of pure rotation). Hence

$$
T_{i-1,i} = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \theta_i)\,\mathrm{Trans}(\hat z, d_i)\,\mathrm{Trans}(\hat x, a_i)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, \alpha_i) , \tag{13.1.4}
$$

where $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \theta)$ denotes the 4×4 homogeneous transformation that rotates through θ about the z-axis, $\mathrm{Trans}(\hat z, d)$ denotes a translation by d along the z-axis, and so on. By the subscript rules of this book, $T_{i-1,i}$ is the pose of {i} relative to {i−1}.

Multiplying out the first two factors and the last two factors separately gives

$$
\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \theta)\,\mathrm{Trans}(\hat z, d) = \begin{pmatrix} c_\theta & -s_\theta & 0 & 0 \\ s_\theta & c_\theta & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & d \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
\mathrm{Trans}(\hat x, a)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, \alpha) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & a \\ 0 & c_\alpha & -s_\alpha & 0 \\ 0 & s_\alpha & c_\alpha & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix},
$$

where $c_\theta = \cos\theta$, $s_\theta = \sin\theta$, and so on. Multiplying these together, the rotation part is the product of $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z,\theta)$ and $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x,\alpha)$, and the translation part is $(a, 0, 0)$ rotated by $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z,\theta)$ plus $(0, 0, d)$. The result is

$$
T_{i-1,i} = \begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta_i & -\sin\theta_i\cos\alpha_i & \sin\theta_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\cos\theta_i \\
\sin\theta_i & \cos\theta_i\cos\alpha_i & -\cos\theta_i\sin\alpha_i & a_i\sin\theta_i \\
0 & \sin\alpha_i & \cos\alpha_i & d_i \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix} . \tag{13.1.5}
$$

This is the standard DH transformation matrix most often seen in textbooks and examinations. Read its fourth column: the origin of {i} lies in {i−1} at $(a_i\cos\theta_i,\ a_i\sin\theta_i,\ d_i)$, that is, first rise by $d_i$ along $z_{i-1}$, then go out $a_i$ in the horizontal plane in the direction $\theta_i$, in agreement with Figure 13.1.1(a).

Eq. (13.1.4) has another structural feature worth noticing. A rotation and a translation along the same axis can be performed in either order, so the first two factors together form a screw motion along the z-axis, and the last two factors together form a screw motion along the x-axis. As Chapter 12 explained, the motion of each joint in the product of exponentials is also a screw motion; Section 13.4 will use this to convert between the two methods.

**From the links to the whole arm.** Multiplying the transformations between adjacent frames in turn gives the pose of the end-effector frame {n} relative to the base frame {0}; if the base frame {s} and the end-effector frame {b} differ from {0} and {n}, a fixed transformation is added at each end:

$$
T_{0n}(\theta) = T_{01}(\theta_1)\,T_{12}(\theta_2) \cdots T_{n-1,n}(\theta_n), \qquad T_{sb} = T_{s0}\,T_{0n}(\theta)\,T_{nb} . \tag{13.1.6}
$$

The DH method itself was proposed in 1955 by Jacques Denavit and Richard S. Hartenberg in the *Journal of Applied Mechanics*. They were studying mechanisms made up of lower pairs (kinematic pairs with surface contact, such as revolute and prismatic pairs), and their aim was to give a unified notation that would replace geometric derivations made mechanism by mechanism with matrix computation. More than twenty years later, Richard P. Paul, in his 1981 book *Robot Manipulators: Mathematics, Programming, and Control*, applied the method systematically to the forward and inverse kinematics of industrial arms, and the form of Eq. (13.1.4), which places each frame at the far end of its link, thereby became the standard form in robotics.

### 13.1.5 Modified DH (Craig)

In his textbook *Introduction to Robotics: Mechanics and Control*, published in 1986, John J. Craig adopted another way of placing the frames, which later became generally known as **modified DH**, or the Craig convention.

**Definition 13.1.4 (Link frames of modified DH)** On link i fix a frame {i} according to these rules: the $z_i$ axis lies along axis i (the joint belonging to link i itself); the $x_i$ axis lies along the common normal $N_i$, pointing from axis i to axis i+1; the origin is at the point where $N_i$ meets axis i; $y_i = z_i \times x_i$.

Compared with standard DH, the geometric meaning of the four parameters is unchanged; what changes is the end of the link at which the frame is placed: standard DH places {i} at the far end of link i (on axis i+1), and modified DH places it at the near end (on axis i). The four motions from {i−1} to {i} therefore come in a different order: {i−1} is on axis i−1 and its x-axis already lies along $N_{i-1}$, so we first rotate through $\alpha_{i-1}$ about x and translate by $a_{i-1}$ along x to reach axis i, and then rotate through $\theta_i$ about z and translate by $d_i$ along z (Figure 13.1.1(b)):

$$
T_{i-1,i} = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, \alpha_{i-1})\,\mathrm{Trans}(\hat x, a_{i-1})\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \theta_i)\,\mathrm{Trans}(\hat z, d_i) . \tag{13.1.7}
$$

Multiplying out in the same way as for Eq. (13.1.5) gives

$$
T_{i-1,i} = \begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta_i & -\sin\theta_i & 0 & a_{i-1} \\
\sin\theta_i\cos\alpha_{i-1} & \cos\theta_i\cos\alpha_{i-1} & -\sin\alpha_{i-1} & -d_i\sin\alpha_{i-1} \\
\sin\theta_i\sin\alpha_{i-1} & \cos\theta_i\sin\alpha_{i-1} & \cos\alpha_{i-1} & d_i\cos\alpha_{i-1} \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix} . \tag{13.1.8}
$$

So row i of a Craig parameter table gives $(a_{i-1}, \alpha_{i-1}, d_i, \theta_i)$: the first two numbers belong to the previous link and the last two to this joint.

The advantage of modified DH is that the z-axis of {i} is the axis of joint i itself, the rotation of joint i is a rotation about $z_i$, and the origin also lies on the joint. As a result, when velocities, forces and torques are computed recursively link by link in dynamics, the axis of every joint is written as (0, 0, 1) in the frame of its own link, which keeps the writing tidy. In the same year Wisama Khalil and Jean-François Kleinfinger extended this convention to tree-structured and closed-chain mechanisms, and this form is widely used in the literature on dynamic modelling and parameter identification.

Craig's textbook also uses left superscripts and left subscripts to indicate frames; for example, ${}^{A}P$ denotes the coordinates in {A} of the position vector of a point P (Craig uses a capital P for a position vector). This book uses subscript notation (see the notation conventions), and the two notations are compared in Table 13.1.1. When reading Craig's textbook or manuals based on it, convert using this table.

### 13.1.6 The relation between the two conventions

Since the four parameters mean the same thing in the two conventions, the two parameter tables of the same robot ought to be merely different arrangements of the same set of numbers. Let us make this precise.

Split Eq. (13.1.4) into two screw motions and write

$$
Z_i = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, \theta_i)\,\mathrm{Trans}(\hat z, d_i), \qquad X_i = \mathrm{Trans}(\hat x, a_i)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, \alpha_i) ,
$$

where $a_i$ and $\alpha_i$ are the length and twist of the common normal $N_i$ (Definition 13.1.2), the same numbers whichever convention is used. Then the link transformation of standard DH is $Z_i X_i$, and the link transformation of modified DH is $X_{i-1} Z_i$ (the first two factors in Eq. (13.1.7) are just $X_{i-1}$, because a rotation and a translation along the same axis commute).

**Theorem 13.1.2** Suppose the two conventions use the same base frame {0}, with $z_0$ along axis 1 and $x_0$ along $N_0$ (so that in the first row of modified DH $a_0 = \alpha_0 = 0$ and $X_0 = I$). Then

$$
T^{\mathrm S}_{0n} = T^{\mathrm M}_{0n}\,X_n , \tag{13.1.9}
$$

where the superscripts S and M denote computation by standard DH and modified DH respectively. Moreover, for each i, the {i} of standard DH equals the {i} of modified DH multiplied by $X_i$.

*Proof* By Eqs. (13.1.4) and (13.1.7),

$$
T^{\mathrm S}_{0n} = (Z_1 X_1)(Z_2 X_2)\cdots(Z_n X_n), \qquad T^{\mathrm M}_{0n} = (X_0 Z_1)(X_1 Z_2)\cdots(X_{n-1} Z_n) .
$$

Both are the alternating product of $Z_1, X_1, Z_2, X_2, \ldots$; only the brackets are placed differently. Matrix multiplication is associative, so on removing the brackets and substituting $X_0 = I$, the former has one more factor $X_n$ at the far right than the latter. Taking the first i pairs of factors gives the second statement in the same way.　∎

Theorem 13.1.2 shows that the two DH tables of the same robot hold the same set of numbers: in the modified DH table the a and α columns are moved down one row as a whole, while the d and θ columns stay where they are; the end-effector frames of the two conventions differ by a fixed transformation $X_n$. The positions of the frames follow as well: the {i} of standard DH is at the far end of $N_i$ (on axis i+1), and the {i} of modified DH is at the near end of $N_i$ (on axis i), with the x-axis along $N_i$ in both. The two conventions have long existed side by side, each with its own variants in the literature, and Harvey Lipkin devoted a short paper in 2005 to comparing them.

**Example 13.1.2** Write down the two DH tables of the planar 3R arm (the same as in Example 12.1.1, with $L_1 = 0.425$ m, $L_2 = 0.392$ m, $L_3 = 0.1$ m), and compute the pose of the end-effector at θ = (30°, 45°, −90°).

The three joint axes are all perpendicular to the page and parallel to one another; the common normal of two adjacent axes is simply the line joining the centres of the two joints, so $a_1 = L_1$, $a_2 = L_2$, all twists are zero, and all offsets are taken as zero. Placing {3} at the end-effector amounts to imagining a further parallel "axis 4" at the end-effector, with $a_3 = L_3$. |---|---|---|

| 2 | $(L_2,\ 0,\ 0,\ \theta_2)$ | $(L_1,\ 0,\ 0,\ \theta_2)$ |
| 3 | $(L_3,\ 0,\ 0,\ \theta_3)$ | $(L_2,\ 0,\ 0,\ \theta_3)$ |

The a column has moved down one row, just as Theorem 13.1.2 says. Computing with standard DH by Eq. (13.1.6),

$$

so the end-effector is at $({{ex13_1.tipx:.4f}},\ {{ex13_1.tipy:.4f}})$ m, pointing at −15°. ### 13.1.7 What happens if the wrong convention is used

The numbers in the two tables are the same, merely shifted by one row, and this makes "using the wrong convention" an error that is very easy to make and hard to notice: the table can still be substituted into the formulas, and what comes out is still a perfectly valid homogeneous transformation matrix.

**Example 13.1.3** (1) Substitute the modified DH table of Example 13.1.2 into the standard DH formula (13.1.4); (2) substitute the standard DH table of the UR5e published by Universal Robots (Section 13.3) into the modified DH formula (13.1.7), using the joint angles of Example 12.3.1. How large is the error in each case?

(1) For the planar 3R arm, the end-effector comes out at $({{ex13_1.wx:.4f}},\ {{ex13_1.wy:.4f}})$ m, {{ex13_1.err3:.3f}} m from the correct position. The reason is easy to see: the standard DH formula places the a of row i after joint i, so the lengths of the three links become 0, $L_1$, $L_2$ in turn; that is, each length is applied one joint too late, and the $L_3$ of the last segment is lost.

(2) With the correct convention, the centre of the flange of the UR5e is at ${{ex13_1.ur_right}}$ m; with the wrong convention it comes out at ${{ex13_1.ur_wrong}}$ m, {{ex13_1.err_ur:.3f}} m away. Over ten thousand random sets of joint angles, the error is {{ex13_1.err_mean:.2f}} m on average and {{ex13_1.err_max:.2f}} m at most. This is not a question of accuracy: what is being computed is simply not this robot.

So, on receiving a DH table, the first thing to do is to establish which convention it uses. The simplest test is a zero-position check: set all the joint variables to zero, compute the end-effector position from the table, and compare it with the drawings or the real robot (Engineer's notes of Chapter 12).

### 13.1.8 An everyday example

If you describe a folding desk lamp with a tape measure, the most natural way to do it is exactly the DH way: measure the distance between the pins of two adjacent hinges, which is the link length; see whether the two pins are parallel, and if they are not, note the angle between them, which is the link twist; then note the angle through which each hinge is turned, which is the joint angle. As for which side of the hinge the "zero mark" is drawn on, the side nearer the base or the side nearer the shade, that is the difference between standard DH and modified DH. Either way of marking describes the lamp completely, but a given record sheet must be read by the convention in which it was drawn up.

**Engineer's notes**

- On receiving a DH table, first look for the convention in the notes on its source: tables marked Craig, modified or proximal are modified DH; tables marked standard, classic or distal, or following the textbooks of Paul, Spong or Siciliano, are standard DH. If there is no such note, decide by a zero-position check.
- Another way to tell the conventions apart: in a modified DH table the a and α of the first row are almost always 0, while those of the last row are often non-zero; in standard DH it is just the reverse, and the a and α of the last row are often 0.
- When implementing DH in a program, it is best to write it as a product of four elementary transformations, as in Eqs. (13.1.4) and (13.1.7), and then use the expanded forms (13.1.5) and (13.1.8) as unit tests. Copying the expanded forms by hand makes it very easy to confuse the subscripts i and i−1.
- Always use radians for angles. In manufacturers' tables α is often written as π/2; some software wants the value in radians as a decimal, other software wants 90, so check the unit before entering it.
