### 13.3.1 The problem: an official parameter table

In the technical-support section of its website, Universal Robots has published an article entitled "DH parameters for calculations of kinematics and dynamics", which lists the DH parameters of each model of arm, together with the mass, centre-of-mass position and inertia of each link. As of October 2026 the article is still on the UR website, with the date 4 October 2018 shown on the page; the UR5e and UR7e share one column, and the UR10e and UR12e share another. Table 13.3.1 reproduces the kinematic part of the UR5e column. The original lists only the three columns a, d and α, with α written as π/2, −π/2 and 0; the joint angle θ is a variable and is not listed in the original, so Table 13.3.1 adds a θ column at the front, for comparison with the other parameter tables of this chapter. The original also contains a note: the a in the table is the r of the Wikipedia article "Denavit–Hartenberg parameters", that is, the link length.

Given this table, several questions need answering. Which convention is it written in? Where are the frames placed? Why are a₂ and a₃ negative? ### 13.3.2 Reading the table

**Convention.** The last row of the table has $a_6 = \alpha_6 = 0$, and the first row has $\alpha_1 = \pi/2$ with $d_1$ equal to the shoulder height; these are typical features of standard DH (Engineer's notes of Section 13.1). Substituting the table into Eqs. (13.1.5) and (13.1.6), the result at the zero position can be checked against the real robot.

**Example 13.3.1** Using Table 13.3.1, compute the pose of the end-effector of the UR5e at the zero position.

With all θ set to zero, multiplying the six link transformations in turn gives

$$
T_{06}(0) = {{ex13_3.T0}} .
$$

The centre of the flange is at ${{ex13_3.p0}}$ m, which differs by {{ex13_3.gap0:.2f}} mm from the value (−0.817, −0.234, 0.063) m obtained in Chapter 12 from the parts-library model, mainly in the y direction (the manufacturer's $d_4 + d_6$ is 1.1 mm smaller than the model's 0.234 m). The difference is only about one millimetre, which shows that the base frame {0} of Table 13.3.1 and the {s} of Chapter 12 are the same frame (as Section 12.3.3 already pointed out). The third column of the rotation matrix, (0, −1, 0), is the direction of $z_6$: at the zero position the flange faces the −y direction, and $z_6$ points outwards along the normal of the flange. The first column, (1, 0, 0), shows that $x_6$ points the same way as $x_0$.

**Frames.** The frames of Table 13.3.1 are exactly the same as those set up for the UR5e in Section 13.2.5 (Figure 13.2.3); only the values are taken from the manufacturer. $d_1$ is the height of the point where the axes of joints 1 and 2 intersect; $a_2$ and $a_3$ are the distances between the two pairs of parallel axes on the upper arm and the forearm; $d_4$ is the sideways distance from the vertical plane containing the forearm to the axis of joint 5; $d_5$ and $d_6$ are the distances along the axes in the two segments of the wrist.

**Negative a.** By Definition 13.1.2 the link length is a distance and should be non-negative. In the table, $a_2 = {{ex13_3.v_a2}}$ m and $a_3 = {{ex13_3.v_a3}}$ m are negative, and the reason lies in the choice of direction of the x-axes. When the frames were set up in Section 13.2.5, the x-axes of {1}, {2} and {3} were all chosen as +x, the same direction as $x_0$, so that every $\theta_i$ is zero at the zero position and all the joint offsets are zero. The price is that going from {1} to {2} and from {2} to {3} means moving in the −x direction, so Eq. (13.2.1) gives negative values of a. To insist on a ≥ 0, $x_2$ and $x_3$ must be taken as −x. By the analysis of Section 13.2.3, reversing $x_i$ changes the sign of a and α in row i, adds π to the θ offset of that row, and also adds π to the θ offset of the next row; here $\alpha_2 = \alpha_3 = 0$, which is unchanged by the change of sign. Adding up the two reversals, the θ offset of row 3 has had π added twice, which is the same as adding nothing, and the result is the "strict" form of Table 13.3.2.

Program 13.3.1 verifies that the two tables give exactly the same $T_{06}$ at any joint angles. Textbooks and examinations usually require the form with a ≥ 0, as the definition says; manufacturers prefer to let a be negative so that the θ column contains no offsets, and the joint angles read by the controller can then be substituted into the formulas as they are, without remembering which joints need π added. This is what "manufacturer's-manual form" in the title of this section means: zero offsets come first. When reading a manufacturer's manual, a negative a is no cause for surprise; when rewriting a table from a manual in the form an examination requires, remember to change the θ column at the same time.

**Checking one joint at a time.** If only joint 1 turns, only the θ of the first row of the table changes; the flange turns about $z_0$ and its height does not change. If only joint 6 turns, the centre of the flange does not move, because the origin of {6} lies on $z_5$ (axis 6). This agrees with the observations of Lab 12.1 in Chapter 12.

### 13.3.3 The same joint angles, two sets of parameters

**Example 13.3.2** Take the joint angles of Example 12.3.1, θ = (30°, −60°, 90°, −120°, −90°, 45°), compute the pose of the end-effector with Table 13.3.1, and compare it with the product-of-exponentials result of Chapter 12.

$$
T_{06} = {{ex13_3.Tv}} .
$$

The centre of the flange is at ${{ex13_3.pv}}$ m; Chapter 12, using the parts-library dimensions and the product of exponentials, found ${{ex13_3.pp}}$ m, and the two differ by {{ex13_3.gap_ex:.2f}} mm. As for the orientation, the end-effector frame {b} of Chapter 12 and the DH frame {6} differ by a fixed $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, 90^\circ)$ (Example 13.2.2); after multiplying by it, the rotation matrices of the two differ by only ${{ex13_3.rot_err:sci1}}$, which is rounding error. This is because the orientation is determined only by the twist angles α and the joint angles θ, and has nothing to do with the lengths, and the two sets of parameters have the same twist angles. The whole difference between the two sets of parameters lies in the lengths.

How do differences in the length parameters carry through to the end-effector? The following theorem gives the exact answer.

**Theorem 13.3.1** Suppose two standard DH tables have the same twist angles and joint offsets and differ only in the length parameters: $d_i$ differs by $\Delta d_i$ and $a_i$ by $\Delta a_i$. Then at any joint angles the two give the same orientation of the end-effector, and the difference in the position of the end-effector is

$$
\Delta p = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(\Delta d_i\,z_{i-1}(\theta) + \Delta a_i\,x_i(\theta)\big) , \tag{13.3.1}
$$

where $z_{i-1}(\theta)$ and $x_i(\theta)$ are the directions in {0} of the axes of the link frames in the current configuration (computed from either of the two tables). Hence

$$
|\Delta p| \le \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(|\Delta d_i| + |\Delta a_i|\big) . \tag{13.3.2}
$$

*Proof* Replacing $d_i$ in row i by $d_i + \Delta d_i$ amounts to inserting a $\mathrm{Trans}(\hat z, \Delta d_i)$ after the $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z,\theta_i)$ in Eq. (13.1.4). This translation is along the z-axis of the frame $T_{0,i-1}\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z,\theta_i)$, and a rotation about z does not change the z-axis, so it is along $z_{i-1}(\theta)$. A translation along a given direction, wherever it is inserted in the product, can be moved to the far left, where it becomes a translation along the same direction in {0}, while the other factors are unchanged. So the whole chain is translated by $\Delta d_i\,z_{i-1}$ in {0}, and its orientation is unchanged. A change in $a_i$ works in the same way, with a translation along $x_i$ inserted instead. None of these translations changes the direction of any axis, so they can be added term by term, giving Eq. (13.3.1). Eq. (13.3.2) follows from the triangle inequality.　∎

### 13.3.4 Nominal, model and calibrated parameters

Table 13.3.1 gives the **nominal parameters** of the UR5e, that is, the dimensions on the design drawings. The dimensions of the parts-library model are given to the millimetre and are an approximation of the nominal parameters. Each real UR5e, because of errors in machining and assembling its parts, differs from the nominal parameters by a few tenths of a millimetre, and its axes are not exactly parallel or perpendicular either.

Every UR robot undergoes kinematic calibration before it leaves the factory, and the calibrated parameters are stored in the controller of that robot in the form of DH parameters; the driver that UR provides for ROS includes a dedicated tool that reads these data out and generates a kinematic model of that particular robot. The calibrated parameters generally differ very little from the nominal ones, but Section 13.5 will show that on axes that are nominally parallel, such as those of joints 2, 3 and 4, a calibrated d can be as large as several hundred metres. This does not mean anything is wrong with the robot; it is a defect of the DH parameterization itself.

Kinematic calibration is itself a nonlinear least-squares problem: the parameters are adjusted so as to minimize the sum of squares of the differences between the end-effector positions computed by the model and those measured with instruments such as a laser tracker (Section 8.5 of Chapter 8). The mass and centre-of-mass data in the UR article, which have nothing to do with kinematics, will be used for dynamic parameter identification in Chapter 26.

### 13.3.6 An everyday example

When a room is being fitted out, a set of built-in bookcases is made to order from a design drawing. The drawing gives the width of the wall as 2,400 mm: this is the nominal dimension. The carpenter measures it on site with a tape measure and finds it is actually 2,398 mm: this is the "calibration". If the bookcases are made to the drawing, they will be 2 mm out when fitted. The same holds for industrial robots doing precision work: the DH parameters in the manual are the drawing, and the calibrated parameters are the on-site measurements. To send the end-effector precisely to a given point on a CAD drawing, the robot's own parameters must be used. As for which side of the wall the drawing puts the dimension on, and whether it is measured from left to right or from right to left, that corresponds to the choice of direction of the x-axis and the sign of a; all that matters is that the person measuring and the person reading agree on the convention.

The next section returns to the method itself: since the DH parameters and the product of exponentials describe the same robot, it should be possible to convert between them.

**Engineer's notes**

- After entering a manufacturer's DH table into your own program, first compute the zero position and a few cases in which only one joint turns, and compare them with the workspace drawings in the manual or with the readings of the teach pendant. Negative values of a and an α of π/2 are common sources of input errors.
- The end-effector position shown on the UR teach pendant is computed by the controller from the calibrated parameters of that robot and the current tool centre point (TCP) setting; if you compute it yourself with the nominal parameters, a difference of a few tenths of a millimetre to one or two millimetres from the pendant reading is normal.
- Where absolute accuracy is needed, as in offline programming and vision-guided positioning, the calibrated parameters of that particular robot should be used; they cannot be carried over to another robot of the same model.
- On the UR web page α is written as π/2, but when it is copied into data files and software it is often truncated to a decimal; for example, the entry in this book's parts library stores {{ex13_3.alpha_raw:.7f}}. Computing with this number leaves an extra residual of ${{ex13_3.raw_err:sci1}}$ in the orientation of Example 13.3.2. In programs it is best to use π/2 itself; when making numerical comparisons, the tolerance for deciding "equal or not" should be larger than this order of magnitude.
