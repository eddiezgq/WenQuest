The main thread of this chapter is: **the motion of a robot is described by differential equations, and a computer finds approximate solutions to them step by step; every approximation has an error, and both the size and the character of the error can be analysed and measured.**

We defined the derivative starting from the rate of change, and used Taylor's formula with remainder to show that the error of "approximating along the tangent" is proportional to the square of the step; this fact runs through the whole chapter. A physical law that relates a rate of change to the quantity itself is a differential equation. The first-order model of a motor has an analytic solution, and its time constant characterizes how fast it responds; a link obeys a second-order equation, which becomes a first-order state equation when the angle and the angular velocity are taken as the state, and the same is true of a general robot arm. The phase plane draws a motion as a trajectory; linearization at an equilibrium point and the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix decide stability; without friction, energy is conserved and the trajectories are the level curves of the energy.

The starting point of numerical integration is Euler's method. Its local truncation error is proportional to h² and its global error to h, so it is a first-order method; the order of a method can be measured by "halving the step and seeing by what factor the error shrinks". When the step is too large, another kind of failure, unrelated to accuracy, appears: numerical instability, which for Euler's method sets the condition h ≤ 2/|λ|. Runge–Kutta methods take a weighted average of several slopes within one step; the classical RK4 is fourth-order. In long simulations of conservative systems, what matters more is to keep the energy from drifting: Euler's method magnifies areas in phase space, so the energy keeps growing; symplectic methods preserve area, and their energy error stays bounded. Orientation is not an ordinary vector: when integrating gyroscope readings, the update must be made on SO(3) with the exponential map; such Lie group integration always yields a rotation matrix, and the Magnus expansion gives a fourth-order version.

Nonlinear equations are solved by Newton's method: linearize at the current point, solve the linear equations, iterate. It converges quadratically near a root, but it depends on the starting guess, cannot take a single step where the Jacobian matrix is singular, and jumps about when the target has no solution; line search and damped least squares are the two most common remedies. Numerical differentiation is the reverse of numerical integration: the truncation error decreases with h, while errors in the readings (round-off, quantization, noise) are magnified by 1/h, and the best difference step lies where the two balance; speed measurement with an encoder is exactly this problem. Finally, when the fast and slow modes of a system differ widely, the equation is stiff: the step of explicit methods is held down by the fastest mode, while implicit methods solve an equation at every step and in exchange are stable at any step size.

These methods will be used again and again in later chapters: optimization and the Gauss–Newton method in Chapter 8, numerical inverse kinematics in Chapter 15, dynamic simulation in Chapters 23–29, and the discretization of controllers and state estimation in Chapters 42–46.

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| State | Knowing it and the future inputs determines the future motion uniquely |
| Phase plane | The plane whose coordinates are the two components of the state; a motion is a trajectory in it |
| Equilibrium point | A state with f(x) = 0; the eigenvalues of the linearized matrix decide whether it is stable |
| Local truncation error and global error | The deviation produced by one step; the total deviation after accumulation and propagation |
| Order of a method | Global error ∝ $h^p$; halving the step shrinks the error by a factor of $2^p$ |
| Numerical stability | The modulus of the amplification factor is at most 1, so errors are not magnified step after step |
| Symplectic method | A method that preserves area in phase space; the energy error is bounded and does not drift |
| Lie group integration | Advancing on a Lie group with the exponential map, so the numerical solution always stays on the Lie group |
| Quadratic convergence | The error is proportional to the square of the previous error; the number of correct digits roughly doubles at each step |
| Basin of attraction | The set of starting guesses from which the iteration converges to a given solution |
| Stiff equation | The fast and slow modes differ widely, and the step of explicit methods is held down by the fast mode |
| A-stability | The stability region contains the whole left half-plane, so every step size is stable |
