### 7.4.1 The problem

Velocity control of a joint needs the angular velocity, but often the only sensor on the joint is an encoder, which reports the angle, and in whole counts at that (Chapter 38). Suppose the incremental encoder on the motor shaft has 4,096 lines, giving {{ex7_4.counts:,}} counts per revolution after quadrature decoding; one count corresponds to the angle q = 2π/{{ex7_4.counts:,}} rad = {{ex7_4.q_urad:.1f}} µrad. The controller reads the count every 1 ms. How can the angular velocity be found from this sequence of readings? The most direct way is "this reading minus the last one, divided by 1 ms". How large is the error of this approach? Can we do better?

This is the problem of **numerical differentiation**, that is, finding the derivative of a function whose values are known only at some discrete points. It looks like the inverse of numerical integration, but its character is very different: integration averages errors out, while differentiation amplifies them. The first half of this section studies the errors of numerical differentiation; the second half turns to another difficulty in numerical integration. Section 7.2 noted that the step size of Euler's method is limited by the fastest mode. If the inductance of the motor and its current loop are also written into the model, the fastest mode becomes hundreds of times faster than the speed changes we care about, and explicit methods are forced to use extremely small steps. Equations of this kind are called stiff equations.

### 7.4.2 Difference quotients and truncation error

By the definition (7.1.1) of the derivative, taking a small h lets us approximate the derivative by a difference quotient. There are three common choices of difference quotient:

$$
\dot x(t) \approx \frac{x(t+h) - x(t)}{h} , \tag{7.4.1}
$$

$$
\dot x(t) \approx \frac{x(t) - x(t-h)}{h} , \tag{7.4.2}
$$

$$
\dot x(t) \approx \frac{x(t+h) - x(t-h)}{2h} . \tag{7.4.3}
$$

They are called the **forward difference**, the **backward difference** and the **central difference** respectively; h is called the difference step; in measurement problems, where h is the time between the two readings that are differenced, it is called the difference interval. Real-time control can use only data already measured, so only the backward difference can be used there; when processing data offline, all three can be used.

**Theorem 7.4.1** If $|\ddot x| \le M_2$, the truncation error of the forward and backward differences is at most $M_2h/2$; if $|\dddot x| \le M_3$, the truncation error of the central difference is at most $M_3h^2/6$.

*Proof*　Forward difference: by Theorem 7.1.1, $|x(t+h) - x(t) - h\dot x(t)| \le M_2h^2/2$; dividing both sides by h gives the result. The backward difference is handled in the same way (replace h by −h). Central difference: following the proof of Theorem 7.1.1, apply "a function whose derivative is not positive does not increase" three times in succession to $g(s) = x(t+s) - x(t) - s\dot x(t) - \frac{s^2}{2}\ddot x(t) - \frac{M_3}{6}s^3$; this gives the second-order Taylor formula

$$
x(t \pm h) = x(t) \pm h\dot x(t) + \frac{h^2}{2}\ddot x(t) \pm r_\pm , \qquad |r_\pm| \le \frac{M_3}{6}h^3 .
$$

Subtracting the two, $x(t+h) - x(t-h) = 2h\dot x(t) + r_+ + r_-$: the second-derivative terms cancel. Dividing by 2h, the error is at most $\frac{1}{2h}\cdot\frac{2M_3h^3}{6} = M_3h^2/6$.　∎

The central difference is second-order accurate, the forward and backward differences first-order accurate. The advantage of the central difference comes from symmetry: taking points symmetrically on either side of t makes the even-order terms of the Taylor expansion cancel. This is the same reason as for the symmetry of the midpoint method and the Störmer–Verlet method in Section 7.2.

### 7.4.3 Round-off error: smaller h is not always better

By Theorem 7.4.1, the smaller h is, the smaller the truncation error. But when h is very small, x(t + h) and x(t) are almost equal, and subtracting two nearly equal numbers loses many significant digits.

**Example 7.4.1** Use the forward and central differences to find the derivative of sin t at t = 1 (exact value cos 1), with h ranging from $10^{-1}$ to $10^{-15}$.

Computers use double-precision floating-point numbers, with machine epsilon $\varepsilon$ = $ {{ex7_4.eps:sci2}} $; that is, a number has only about 16 significant digits. The results are plotted in Figure 7.4.1. As h decreases from $10^{-1}$, the error of the forward difference at first decreases in proportion to h: it is $ {{ex7_4.fw1:sci2}} $ at h = 0.1 and $ {{ex7_4.fw2:sci2}} $ at h = 0.01; it reaches its minimum $ {{ex7_4.fw_best:sci1}} $ at $h = 10^{-8}$; after that, as h decreases further, the error grows again, reaching $ {{ex7_4.fw15:sci2}} $ at $h = 10^{-15}$. The error of the central difference first decreases like h² (at h = 0.1 and 0.01 it is $ {{ex7_4.ce1:sci2}} $ and $ {{ex7_4.ce2:sci2}} $, a hundredfold reduction), reaches its minimum $ {{ex7_4.ce_best:sci1}} $ at $h = 10^{-5}$, and then likewise grows.

::: Figure 7.4.1
src: fig7_4_1
caption: Finding the derivative of sin t at t = 1 by difference quotients: error against step size h (log–log axes). On the right is the truncation error (forward ∝ h, central ∝ h²), on the left the round-off error (∝ ε/h)
:::

The reason is this: sin(1 + h) and sin 1 each carry a round-off error of about $\varepsilon|\sin 1|$; after subtraction the error can be as large as $2\varepsilon|\sin 1|$, and dividing by h magnifies it to $2\varepsilon|\sin 1|/h$. The total error is about

$$
E(h) \approx \frac{M_2}{2}h + \frac{2\varepsilon|x|}{h} , \tag{7.4.4}
$$

where the first term shrinks as h shrinks, while the second grows. Setting the derivative of E with respect to h to zero gives the optimal step size

$$
h^* = 2\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon|x|}{M_2}} . \tag{7.4.5}
$$

For sin at t = 1, $|x| = M_2 = \sin 1$ and $h^* = 2\sqrt\varepsilon$ = $ {{ex7_4.h_fw_th:sci1}} $; the smallest error is of the order of $\sqrt\varepsilon$, only about 8 significant digits. In the same way the central difference gives $h^* = (3\varepsilon|x|/M_3)^{1/3}$ = $ {{ex7_4.h_ce_th:sci1}} $, with a smallest error of about $\varepsilon^{2/3}$, about 11 significant digits. The measured optimal values of h are at $10^{-{{ex7_4.k_fw}}}$ and $10^{-{{ex7_4.k_ce}}}$ respectively, in agreement with the estimates.

This conclusion has a direct use: if the Jacobian matrix in Newton's method of Section 7.3 is to be computed by difference quotients, the difference step h should be $10^{-8}$ (forward) or $10^{-5}$–$10^{-6}$ (central) times the typical size of the variable, rather than "the smaller the better".

### 7.4.4 Speed measurement with an encoder

The case of the encoder is similar to round-off error, except that the "rounding" is much coarser: a reading can only be a whole number of counts.

**Example 7.4.2** A joint moves as θ(t) = A sin Ωt, with A = 2 rad and Ω = 2π rad/s, so the amplitude of the angular velocity is AΩ = {{ex7_4.w_amp:.2f}} rad/s. The encoder is read every 1 ms, and the reading is $\theta_q = q\lfloor\theta/q\rfloor$ (rounded down to a whole number of counts). The angular velocity is estimated by a backward difference with difference interval h = n × 1 ms. Find the h that minimizes the root-mean-square error.

First consider the error due to quantization. Each reading is smaller than the true value by some amount between 0 and q; subtracting two readings gives an error between −q and q, and after dividing by h the error of the angular velocity can be as large as q/h. At h = 1 ms, q/h = {{ex7_4.q_over_T:.3f}} rad/s: as soon as the shaft moves one count more or one count less between two readings, the speed jumps by this much (Figure 7.4.2(a), (c)). Following Eq. (7.4.4), the total error of the backward difference is the sum of the truncation error and the quantization error,

$$
E(h) \approx \frac{M_2}{2}h + \frac{q}{h} , \qquad M_2 = A\Omega^2 . \tag{7.4.6}
$$

If h is too small, the quantization error is large; if h is too large, the truncation error is large (in practice this means the estimate lags behind the true value).

Measuring with the root-mean-square error (the square root of the mean of the squared errors) is closer to practice. In the truncation error $\frac h2\ddot\theta$, $\ddot\theta = -A\Omega^2\sin\Omega t$ has root-mean-square value $A\Omega^2/\sqrt2$; if the quantization error is approximately uniformly distributed on [0, q) and independent from one reading to the next, the difference of two readings has root-mean-square value $q/\sqrt6$ (the variance of a uniform distribution is $q^2/12$, and when two independent errors are subtracted their variances add). Hence

$$
E_{\mathrm{rms}}(h) \approx \sqrt{\Big(\frac{hA\Omega^2}{2\sqrt2}\Big)^2 + \Big(\frac{q}{\sqrt6\,h}\Big)^2} . \tag{7.4.7}
$$

Its minimum is at $h^* = \big(8q^2/(6A^2\Omega^4)\big)^{1/4}$ = {{ex7_4.h_th_ms:.2f}} ms, with minimum value {{ex7_4.rms_th:.4f}} rad/s. Program 7.4.1 tries n = 1, 2, …, 60 in turn on 2 s of simulated data: at h = 1 ms the root-mean-square error is {{ex7_4.rms_1:.4f}} rad/s ({{ex7_4.rms_1_pct:.2f}}% of the amplitude); the minimum occurs at h = {{ex7_4.h_best_ms:.0f}} ms and is {{ex7_4.rms_best:.4f}} rad/s ({{ex7_4.rms_best_pct:.2f}}% of the amplitude), in agreement with the estimate of Eq. (7.4.7) (Figure 7.4.2(b)).

The central difference is much better: its best difference interval is {{ex7_4.n_best_c}} ms, and its root-mean-square error is only {{ex7_4.rms_best_c:.4f}} rad/s, an order of magnitude smaller than that of the backward difference. But it uses the reading at time t + h, so the result is available only h later; in a control loop this 10 ms delay is often more harmful than the error itself (Chapter 42). So the best practice for real-time speed measurement differs from the best practice for offline analysis.

Several other improvements are common in engineering. First, at low speed, instead of counting "how many counts in 1 ms", measure "how long one count takes"; the former is called the M method, the latter the T method, and their combination, the M/T method, has good resolution at both high and low speeds. Second, fit several points by least squares and then differentiate; Abraham Savitzky and Marcel Golay published a ready-made set of coefficients in 1964. Third, do not differentiate directly at all, but let a model "track" the position and read the velocity off the model; this is the state estimation of Chapter 46.

### 7.4.5 Stiff equations

We now turn to another kind of difficulty in numerical integration. The motor model of Section 7.1 neglected inductance. In a real motor drive, the current is controlled by a "current loop", and outside it there is a "speed loop" (Chapter 43). Taking the inductance L into account, the voltage balance becomes $L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt = u - Ri - K_e\omega$. Let the current loop be a proportional controller, $u = K_p(i_{\mathrm{ref}} - i)$, and let the speed loop give the current command $i_{\mathrm{ref}} = K_v(\omega_{\mathrm{ref}} - \omega)$. Taking the state x = (i, ω) and rearranging gives the linear equation

$$
\dot x = A x + B , \qquad
A = \begin{pmatrix} -(R + K_p)/L & -(K_e + K_pK_v)/L \\ K_t/J_m & -b/J_m \end{pmatrix} , \qquad
B = \begin{pmatrix} K_pK_v\,\omega_{\mathrm{ref}}/L \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} . \tag{7.4.8}
$$

**Example 7.4.3** The motor parameters are as in Example 7.1.2, with L = 0.6 mH, $K_p$ = {{ex7_4.Kp:.0f}} V/A, $K_v$ = {{ex7_4.Kv:.2f}} A/(rad/s) and speed command $\omega_{\mathrm{ref}}$ = {{ex7_4.w_ref:.0f}} rad/s, starting from rest. Analyse the time scales of this system, and compare the explicit and implicit Euler methods.

Substituting the values, the four entries of A are {{ex7_4.a11:,.0f}}, {{ex7_4.a12:,.0f}}, {{ex7_4.a21:,.0f}} and {{ex7_4.a22:,.1f}} (in s⁻¹ or the corresponding combined units). The two eigenvalues are

$$
\lambda_{\text{slow}} = {{ex7_4.lam_slow:.2f}}\ \mathrm s^{-1}, \qquad \lambda_{\text{fast}} = {{ex7_4.lam_fast:.0f}}\ \mathrm s^{-1} .
$$

The corresponding time constants are {{ex7_4.tau_slow_ms:.1f}} ms and {{ex7_4.tau_fast_us:.1f}} µs, a factor of {{ex7_4.ratio:.0f}} apart. The fast mode is the current: about {{ex7_4.t_peak_ms:.1f}} ms after start-up the current has already shot up to its peak of {{ex7_4.i_peak:.2f}} A. The slow mode is the speed: its time constant is about 20 ms, it takes about {{ex7_4.t95_ms:.0f}} ms to reach 95% of its steady-state value, and at 0.1 s it is {{ex7_4.w_exact:.2f}} rad/s (Figure 7.4.4(a); proportional control leaves a steady-state error, so the steady-state value is not 100 rad/s).

**Definition 7.4.1 (Stiff equation)** If the eigenvalues of a state equation (or of its linearization) all have negative real parts, while the largest and smallest |Re λ| differ so widely that the step size of explicit methods is limited by the stability of the fast modes and is far smaller than the step needed to describe how the solution changes, the equation is called a **stiff equation**. The ratio of the largest to the smallest |Re λ| is called the **stiffness ratio**.

The word "stiff" comes from a 1952 paper by Charles F. Curtiss and Joseph O. Hirschfelder on the computation of chemical reactions: there, fast and slow reactions occur together, like a system containing both soft springs and extremely stiff ones. Robotics has many such cases: the current loop together with mechanical motion, very stiff springs in flexible joints, and hard impacts during contact (Chapters 27 and 29).

For Example 7.4.3 the stiffness ratio is about {{ex7_4.ratio:.0f}}. By Theorem 7.2.2, the explicit Euler method requires $h \le 2/|\lambda_{\text{fast}}|$ = {{ex7_4.h_exp_max_us:.1f}} µs, and RK4 only relaxes this to {{ex7_4.h_rk4_max_us:.1f}} µs. Simulating 0.1 s takes at least {{ex7_4.n_exp:,}} steps, while the speed changes we care about could be described well enough with a step of 1 ms. At h = 50 µs the explicit Euler method is stable, and its speed at 0.1 s, {{ex7_4.ok_exp_w:.2f}} rad/s, is close to the exact value; at h = 60 µs the solution oscillates with growing amplitude, amplified at every step, and diverges (Figure 7.4.4(b)). With h = 1 ms the fast mode is amplified {{ex7_4.gain_exp_1ms:.1f}}-fold at each step and overflows within a few steps.

### 7.4.6 The implicit Euler method

Euler's method uses the slope at the start of the interval. Suppose we use the slope at the end of the interval instead:

$$
x_{k+1} = x_k + h\,f(t_{k+1}, x_{k+1}) . \tag{7.4.9}
$$

The unknown $x_{k+1}$ appears on both sides of the equation and cannot be computed directly; at every step an equation must be solved. Such methods are called implicit methods, and Eq. (7.4.9) is called the **implicit Euler method**, also the backward Euler method; correspondingly, the methods of Section 7.2 are called explicit methods. For the linear equation (7.4.8), Eq. (7.4.9) becomes the linear system

$$
(I - hA)\,x_{k+1} = x_k + hB , \tag{7.4.10}
$$

so each step only needs to solve a 2×2 system; when h does not change, $I - hA$ needs to be factorized only once.

**Theorem 7.4.2** The amplification factor of the implicit Euler method is G(z) = 1/(1 − z), and its region of absolute stability is the whole complex plane outside the circle of radius 1 centred at 1, which includes the whole left half-plane. So for any test equation with Re λ < 0, every step size is stable.

*Proof*　Substitute f = λx: $x_{k+1} = x_k + h\lambda x_{k+1}$, so $x_{k+1} = x_k/(1 - h\lambda)$ and G(z) = 1/(1 − z). |G| ≤ 1 is equivalent to |1 − z| ≥ 1, that is, the distance from z to 1 is at least 1. When Re z < 0, |1 − z| > 1, so z is always in the region.　∎

A method whose stability region contains the whole left half-plane is called A-stable; this concept was introduced by the Swedish mathematician Germund Dahlquist in 1963. At the same time he proved a famous restriction: no explicit linear multistep method can be A-stable, and an A-stable linear multistep method can be at most of order two (Dahlquist's second barrier). For stiff equations one must pay the price of "solving an equation at every step" in exchange for stability at any step size. Figure 7.4.3 compares the regions of absolute stability of several methods.

Back to Example 7.4.3. The implicit Euler method with h = 1 ms uses a step about 18 times the stability limit of the explicit method. The amplification factor of the fast mode is $1/(1 - h\lambda_{\text{fast}})$ = {{ex7_4.gain_imp:.4f}}, so the fast transient of the current is "flattened" within a step or two. At 0.1 s the speed is {{ex7_4.w_imp1:.2f}} rad/s, which differs from the exact value {{ex7_4.w_exact:.2f}} rad/s (found with the matrix exponential of Eq. (7.1.15)) by {{ex7_4.err_imp1:.3f}} rad/s; the largest deviation of the speed over the whole 0.1 s is {{ex7_4.max_dw:.2f}} rad/s (Figure 7.4.4(c)). It takes only {{ex7_4.n_imp}} steps, whereas the explicit method needs at least {{ex7_4.n_exp:,}}. With step sizes of 2 ms, 1 ms and 0.5 ms the errors at 0.1 s are {{ex7_4.err_imp2:.4f}}, {{ex7_4.err_imp1:.4f}} and {{ex7_4.err_imp05:.4f}} rad/s, and the ratio of the first two is {{ex7_4.r_imp:.2f}}: the implicit Euler method is a first-order method.

Note that A-stability only guarantees that the solution does not blow up; it does not guarantee that the fast modes are damped correctly. The trapezoidal rule $x_{k+1} = x_k + \frac h2[f(x_k) + f(x_{k+1})]$ is also A-stable (and second-order), but its amplification factor $(1 + z/2)/(1 - z/2)$ tends to −1 as z → −∞: the fast modes do not blow up, but they oscillate in sign with a factor close to −1 and take a long time to die out. In this example, at h = 1 ms the absolute value of this factor is {{ex7_4.gain_trap:.3f}}, and the current would oscillate back and forth for dozens of steps. The factor of the implicit Euler method tends to 0 as z → −∞, so the fast modes are removed in a single step. For strongly stiff problems, the implicit Euler method and its generalization, the backward differentiation formulas (BDF, systematized by C. William Gear in his 1971 monograph), are preferred.

**Implicit integration of nonlinear equations.** When the equation is nonlinear, Eq. (7.4.9) requires solving a nonlinear equation at each step, which is just what Newton's method of Section 7.3 is for, with the prediction of the explicit Euler method as the starting guess. Take the link of Section 7.1 as an example and let $y = \theta_{k+1}$. Eliminating $\dot\theta_{k+1}$ from $\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + h\dot\theta_{k+1}$ and $\dot\theta_{k+1} = \dot\theta_k - h\omega_n^2\sin\theta_{k+1}$ gives

$$
g(y) = y - \theta_k - h\dot\theta_k + h^2\omega_n^2\sin y = 0 . \tag{7.4.11}
$$

Program 7.4.1 simulates 10 s with h = 0.02 s; at every step Newton's method converges to $10^{-13}$ in at most {{ex7_4.its_max}} iterations ({{ex7_4.its_mean:.2f}} on average). But the result reminds us that stable does not mean accurate: on the imaginary axis the modulus of the amplification factor of the implicit Euler method, $1/\sqrt{1 + h^2\omega_n^2}$, is less than 1, so the frictionless link loses a little energy at every step; after 10 s it has lost {{ex7_4.loss_imp:.1f}}% of its energy, and the link has almost come to rest. This is exactly the opposite of Euler's method: one creates energy artificially, the other dissipates it artificially. Conservative systems call for the symplectic methods of Section 7.2.6, and only stiff systems call for implicit methods; physics engines often combine the two, treating the very stiff parts (such as contact and joint damping) implicitly and the rest with the symplectic Euler method (Chapter 29).

### 7.4.7 Everyday examples

A running watch that finds speed from satellite positioning faces exactly the encoder's problem. The position is updated once a second, with an error of a few metres. If the speed were computed from the difference between the positions in two consecutive seconds, a positioning error of 3 m would make the displayed pace jump about between "3 minutes per kilometre" and "8 minutes per kilometre"; so watches usually smooth the data of the last few tens of seconds before displaying it, at the cost that, when you speed up or slow down, the displayed pace takes a while to catch up. A long difference interval is steady but slow, a short one quick but jumpy: exactly the trade-off of Eq. (7.4.6).

Stiffness has examples in the kitchen too. When a rice cooker cooks rice, the temperature of the heating element changes considerably within a few seconds, while the rice in the pot takes tens of minutes to cook. Simulating the whole cooking process with an explicit method would need a step size set by the time scale of the heating element, a fraction of a second, so tens of minutes would take tens of thousands of steps; with an implicit method, the step size can be chosen on the time scale of the rice.

This chapter has now covered how to write the motion of a robot as a state equation, integrate it step by step, solve nonlinear equations, and obtain derivatives from data. These are all tools; from Chapter 8 onwards they will be applied to concrete problems of optimization, estimation, kinematics and dynamics.

**Engineer's notes**

- For encoder speed measurement, first work out two numbers: the speed corresponding to one count in one sampling period, q/T ({{ex7_4.q_over_T:.2f}} rad/s in this example), and the required speed resolution. If the former is larger than the latter, increase the difference interval, switch to the M/T method, or use an encoder of higher resolution.
- Before differentiating measured data, be clear about where the result will be used: a control loop needs low delay, so use a backward difference with moderate filtering or a state observer; offline analysis needs accuracy, so a central difference or a Savitzky–Golay fit can be used.
- Numerically estimating the second derivative (acceleration) is much harder than the first: in the trade-off between truncation error and quantization error, the quantization error becomes of order $q/h^2$. Estimating acceleration from an encoder usually has to rely on a model and filtering.
- A simple test for stiffness: find the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix of the linearization and look at the ratio of the largest to the smallest |Re λ|. If the ratio is several tens or more and the simulation must also cover the whole course of the slow modes, it is time to consider an implicit method. `method="BDF"` or `"Radau"` in SciPy's `solve_ivp` and MATLAB's `ode15s` are designed for stiff equations.
- An implicit method being stable does not mean it is accurate. When the step size is far larger than the time constant of the fast modes, fast processes (such as the current spike in this example) are smeared out in the simulation result; if the quantity of interest is precisely the current peak (for example, when checking whether the drive is over-current), a small step must be used to resolve it.
