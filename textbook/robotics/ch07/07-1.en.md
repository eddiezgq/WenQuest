### 7.1.1 The problem

A joint is driven by a DC servo motor through a gearbox. At time t = 0 the controller applies a voltage of 12 V to the motor. The speed of the motor does not reach its final value at once; it rises gradually: quickly at first, and more and more slowly the closer it comes to the final value. What the control engineer wants to know is: what is the speed at 50 ms? How long does it take to reach 95% of the final value? And what is the final value?

What is characteristic of such problems is that what we know directly is not "what the speed is" but "how fast the speed is changing". Newton's second law tells us that the angular acceleration (how fast the speed changes) equals the torque divided by the moment of inertia, and the torque in turn depends on the current and the speed at this instant. In other words, the laws of physics give a relation between how fast a quantity changes and the quantity itself. Working back from such a relation to how the quantity varies with time is the subject of this section and the next.

To make "how fast it changes" precise, we need the derivative; writing down "the relation between how fast it changes and the quantity itself" gives a differential equation. This section first presents the derivative, then differential equations, and then writes robot models such as motors and links in the unified form of the "state space". This form is the common starting point for the numerical integration, control and estimation that follow.

### 7.1.2 The derivative: the instantaneous rate of change

Let x(t) be a quantity that varies with time t, for example the speed of a motor. Over the time from t to t + h, x changes by x(t + h) − x(t); dividing the change by the time taken,

$$
\frac{x(t+h) - x(t)}{h},
$$

gives the average rate of change over this time. This ratio is called the **difference quotient**. On the x–t graph it is the slope of the straight line (the secant) joining the two points (t, x(t)) and (t + h, x(t + h)) (Figure 7.1.1). The smaller h is, the closer the average rate of change comes to how fast x is changing at the instant t itself.

**Example 7.1.1** Let x(t) = t². Find the difference quotients at t = 1 for h = 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001.

Direct calculation: for h = 0.1 the difference quotient is (1.21 − 1)/0.1 = {{ex7_1.dq1:.1f}}; for h = 0.01 it is {{ex7_1.dq2:.2f}}; for h = 0.001 it is {{ex7_1.dq3:.3f}}. In general $[(1+h)^2 - 1]/h = 2 + h$, and the smaller h is, the closer the difference quotient comes to 2. The number 2 is the "instantaneous rate of change" of t² at t = 1.

"Coming closer and closer" needs a precise statement. Let a quantity g(h) vary with h. If for every given positive number ε a positive number δ can be found such that |g(h) − a| < ε whenever 0 < |h| < δ, we say that the **limit** of g(h) as h tends to 0 is a, and write $\lim_{h\to 0} g(h) = a$. Put simply: however close to a we want to be, we can get there by making h small enough. In the example above g(h) = 2 + h, and taking δ = ε will do, so the limit is 2. This way of stating limits with ε and δ was founded by Augustin-Louis Cauchy in his *Cours d'analyse* (Course of analysis) of 1821 and put into its final form by Karl Weierstrass in the middle of the 19th century; it freed calculus from the vague language of "infinitesimals".

**Definition 7.1.1 (Derivative)** If the limit

$$
\dot x(t) = \frac{\mathrm d x}{\mathrm d t}(t) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x(t+h) - x(t)}{h} \tag{7.1.1}
$$

exists, it is called the **derivative** of x at t. Geometrically, it is the slope of the tangent to the curve at that point; physically, it is the instantaneous rate of change, with the unit of x divided by the unit of time.

Both notations are in common use. A dot over the letter, $\dot x$, is Newton's notation; he called it the "fluxion". The d notation, from which $\mathrm dx/\mathrm dt$ derives, was published by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in 1684. Throughout the book, derivatives with respect to time are written with a dot, and two dots, $\ddot x$, denote the derivative of the derivative, called the second derivative. The derivative of position is velocity, and the derivative of velocity is acceleration; the derivative $\dot\theta$ of an angle θ is the angular velocity, in rad/s, as already used in Eq. (3.6.1) of Chapter 3.

In computing derivatives, the following rules are used constantly. All of them can be derived directly from the definition (7.1.1); here we only explain what they mean.

- The derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives; when a function is multiplied by a constant, its derivative is multiplied by the same constant.
- The product rule: $(uv)^{\boldsymbol\cdot} = \dot u\,v + u\,\dot v$. Chapter 6 used it to differentiate $RR^{\mathsf T} = I$.
- The chain rule: if x depends on θ and θ varies with time, then $\dot x = \dfrac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm d\theta}\,\dot\theta$. For example, $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\cos\theta(t) = -\sin\theta\;\dot\theta$.
- A few frequently used results: $(t^n)^{\boldsymbol\cdot} = n t^{n-1}$; $(\sin t)^{\boldsymbol\cdot} = \cos t$, $(\cos t)^{\boldsymbol\cdot} = -\sin t$; $(e^{at})^{\boldsymbol\cdot} = a\,e^{at}$. The last is the special case, for 1×1 matrices, of the result of Section 4.4.4 obtained by differentiating the matrix exponential term by term.

The derivative describes what a function looks like near a point: over a very short time h, x changes approximately along the tangent, $x(t+h) \approx x(t) + h\,\dot x(t)$. How accurate is this approximation? The following theorem answers this question, and it is used again and again in this chapter: the errors of numerical integration (Section 7.2) and of numerical differentiation (Section 7.4) are both estimated with it.

**Theorem 7.1.1 (First-order Taylor formula with remainder)** Let x(t) have a second derivative on the interval [t, t + h] (h > 0), with $|\ddot x| \le M$. Then

$$
\big|\,x(t+h) - x(t) - h\,\dot x(t)\,\big| \le \frac{M}{2}\,h^2 . \tag{7.1.2}
$$

*Proof*　Let $g(s) = x(t+s) - x(t) - s\,\dot x(t) - \frac{M}{2}s^2$, 0 ≤ s ≤ h. Then g(0) = 0, $\dot g(s) = \dot x(t+s) - \dot x(t) - Ms$, $\dot g(0) = 0$, and $\ddot g(s) = \ddot x(t+s) - M \le 0$. A function whose rate of change is nowhere greater than zero cannot increase, so $\dot g$, starting from $\dot g(0) = 0$, can only decrease: $\dot g(s) \le 0$. In the same way g, starting from 0, can only decrease, so g(h) ≤ 0, that is, $x(t+h) - x(t) - h\dot x(t) \le \frac{M}{2}h^2$. Replacing M by −M and repeating the argument (all the inequalities are now reversed) gives $x(t+h) - x(t) - h\dot x(t) \ge -\frac{M}{2}h^2$. Together the two inequalities are Eq. (7.1.2).　∎

Eq. (7.1.2) shows that the error of the tangent approximation is proportional to h²: halving h makes the error about a quarter of what it was. For example, for $e^h \approx 1 + h$ the error is {{ex7_1.te1:.6f}} at h = 0.1 and {{ex7_1.te2:.6f}} at h = 0.05, a ratio of {{ex7_1.ratio_taylor:.2f}}. This result is the simplest case of the expansion published by the English mathematician Brook Taylor in 1715. The general **Taylor's formula** writes x(t + h) as a polynomial in h plus a remainder; when terms up to $h^n$ are kept, the error is proportional to $h^{n+1}$. Section 7.2 uses the cases n = 2, 3, 4 to construct higher-order numerical methods.

### 7.1.3 Differential equations: from the law of change to the course of change

Back to the motor. Let the armature resistance be R, the current i, the speed of the motor ω, and the voltage applied across the motor u. When the motor turns, its coils cut the magnetic field and produce a back EMF $K_e\omega$, opposed to the applied voltage; the effect of the inductance is neglected here for the time being (it is taken into account in Section 7.4). The voltage balance and the torque balance are then

$$
u = R\,i + K_e\,\omega , \qquad J_m\,\dot\omega = K_t\,i - b\,\omega . \tag{7.1.3}
$$

Here $K_e$ is the back-EMF constant (V·s/rad), $K_t$ the torque constant (N·m/A), $J_m$ the total moment of inertia referred to the motor shaft (kg·m²), and b the viscous friction coefficient (N·m·s/rad). The second equation is just Newton's second law for rotation: the electromagnetic torque $K_t i$ minus the friction torque $b\omega$ equals the moment of inertia times the angular acceleration $\dot\omega$. The principles of motors are discussed in detail in Chapter 36; only these two relations are needed here. In this section and in Section 7.4, R denotes the resistance and has nothing to do with rotation matrices.

Solving the first equation for $i = (u - K_e\omega)/R$, substituting into the second, multiplying both sides by $R/(Rb + K_tK_e)$ and rearranging gives

$$
\tau\,\dot\omega + \omega = K\,u , \qquad \tau = \frac{J_m R}{Rb + K_tK_e} , \qquad K = \frac{K_t}{Rb + K_tK_e} . \tag{7.1.4}
$$

This is an equation containing the unknown function ω(t) and its derivative.

**Definition 7.1.2 (Ordinary differential equation and initial value problem)** An equation containing an unknown function of one independent variable (here the time t) and its derivatives is called an **ordinary differential equation**. The order of the highest derivative appearing in the equation is called the order of the equation. If the value of the unknown function at the starting time $t_0$ is given as well, we have an **initial value problem**. A first-order equation (or system) is written in the standard form

$$
\dot x = f(t, x) , \qquad x(t_0) = x_0 , \tag{7.1.5}
$$

where f gives "the rate of change at this instant", which depends on the time and on x at this instant. A function x(t) that satisfies the equation and the initial condition is called a solution of the initial value problem.

"Ordinary" is in contrast with partial differential equations: in the latter the unknown function depends on several independent variables (for example time and position in space), and partial derivatives appear. This book uses only ordinary differential equations.

Eq. (7.1.4) is a first-order ordinary differential equation; in standard form it reads $\dot\omega = (Ku - \omega)/\tau$. When the voltage is held at u from t = 0 on and the initial speed is zero, its solution is

$$
\omega(t) = K u\,\big(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\big) . \tag{7.1.6}
$$

Checking it is straightforward: by $(e^{at})^{\boldsymbol\cdot} = a e^{at}$, $\dot\omega = \frac{Ku}{\tau}e^{-t/\tau}$, so $\tau\dot\omega + \omega = Ku\,e^{-t/\tau} + Ku - Ku\,e^{-t/\tau} = Ku$, and ω(0) = 0. How one "thinks of" this solution is discussed systematically with the Laplace transform in Chapter 42; for this chapter, what matters is that it can be substituted back into the equation and checked.

**Example 7.1.2** The motor data are R = 1.2 Ω, $K_t = K_e$ = 0.05 (N·m/A and V·s/rad are numerically the same), $J_m = 4.0\times10^{-5}$ kg·m², b = 2.0×10⁻⁵ N·m·s/rad, and the applied voltage is u = 12 V. Find τ, K, the final speed and the speed at 50 ms.

First compute the common denominator: $Rb + K_tK_e = 1.2\times 2.0\times10^{-5} + 0.05^2 = {{ex7_1.den:.6f}}$. Then

$$
\tau = \frac{4.0\times10^{-5}\times 1.2}{ {{ex7_1.den:.6f}} } = {{ex7_1.tau_ms:.2f}}\ \mathrm{ms}, \qquad
K = \frac{0.05}{ {{ex7_1.den:.6f}} } = {{ex7_1.K:.2f}}\ \frac{\mathrm{rad/s}}{\mathrm V} .
$$

The final speed is $\omega_\infty = Ku = {{ex7_1.w_inf:.1f}}$ rad/s, about {{ex7_1.rpm:,.0f}} r/min. By Eq. (7.1.6), at t = 50 ms, ω = {{ex7_1.w_chk:.1f}} rad/s. Program 7.1.1 also integrates Eq. (7.1.3) directly with the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method of Section 7.2 at a step of 10 µs; the result differs from Eq. (7.1.6) by less than $10^{-8}$ of the final speed.

**The meaning of the time constant.** By Eq. (7.1.6), at t = τ we have $\omega = (1 - e^{-1})\,\omega_\infty$, that is, {{ex7_1.frac1:.3f}} of the final value, about 63.2%; at t = 3τ it reaches {{ex7_1.frac3:.3f}}, about 95%. This is why τ is called the **time constant**; it is the measure of "how quickly" a first-order system responds. The motor of this example essentially reaches its final speed after about 3τ ≈ 57 ms (Figure 7.1.2). Another useful observation: at the instant of starting ω = 0 and $\dot\omega(0) = Ku/\tau$ = {{ex7_1.dw0:,.0f}} rad/s²; if this acceleration were kept up, the final value would be reached exactly at t = τ. This is the point where the tangent at the start meets the final value in the figure. At the instant of starting the current is $i = u/R$ = {{ex7_1.i0:.0f}} A; at steady state only {{ex7_1.i_inf:.3f}} A remains, just enough to overcome friction. The starting current is more than a hundred times the steady-state current, which is exactly why drives have to limit the current (Chapter 36).

**Existence and uniqueness of solutions.** Not every initial value problem has a solution, and when it has one, it need not be the only one. For example, for $\dot x = \sqrt{|x|}$, x(0) = 0, x(t) ≡ 0 is a solution, and so is $x(t) = t^2/4$ (t ≥ 0): according to this equation, a body at rest can "decide for itself" to start moving at any moment. For simulation this is disastrous, because the computer has no way of knowing which solution to follow. Fortunately, almost all the equations of robotics satisfy the conditions of the following theorem.

**Theorem 7.1.2 (Existence and uniqueness of solutions)** Let f(t, x) be continuous near $t_0$, and let there be a constant L such that, for any x and y in the range under consideration,

$$
|f(t, x) - f(t, y)| \le L\,|x - y| . \tag{7.1.7}
$$

Then the initial value problem (7.1.5) has a solution near $t_0$, and only one. When x is a vector, |·| in the formula denotes the length of the vector.

Eq. (7.1.7) is called the **Lipschitz condition**: f may not change with x more steeply than a straight line. If the derivative of f with respect to x is bounded, the condition holds with L taken as a bound on the absolute value of the derivative; in the counterexample above, the slope of $\sqrt{|x|}$ tends to infinity at x = 0, and the condition fails. The proof of the theorem needs integrals and successive approximation and is omitted; see the book by Hairer et al. in the chapter references (Section I.7). The existence result was first given by Cauchy in his lecture notes of the 1820s, and his proof consisted precisely in letting the step of Euler's method of Section 7.2 tend to zero and proving that the polygonal line converges to the solution; Rudolf Lipschitz independently obtained a similar result in 1868 (published again in French in 1876) and stated the condition above explicitly; Émile Picard in 1890 and Ernst Lindelöf in 1894 gave the proof by successive approximation that is now standard. For this chapter, the theorem guarantees one thing: as long as the model is smooth, the initial state determines the future uniquely, and the target that a numerical method has to approximate is well defined.

### 7.1.4 Second-order equations and the state

Now consider a link. When an arm is switched off and a joint brake is released, the link swings under gravity. Treat the link as a uniform slender rod of length $L_k$ = 0.5 m and mass m = 2 kg, turning about a horizontal axis through one end; θ is the angle between the link and the downward vertical, positive anticlockwise (Figure 7.1.3(a)). Gravity acts at the centre of mass, at distance $l_c = L_k/2$ from the axis, and its moment about the axis is $-m\mathfrak g\,l_c\sin\theta$: the minus sign means that it always pulls the link back towards the downward vertical. The moment of inertia of the link about the axis is $J_o = mL_k^2/3$ (Chapter 23), and the axis also carries a viscous friction torque $-b\dot\theta$ and a motor torque $\tau_m$. Newton's second law for rotation gives

$$
J_o\,\ddot\theta = -m\mathfrak g\,l_c\sin\theta - b\,\dot\theta + \tau_m . \tag{7.1.8}
$$

This is a second-order equation: the second derivative of θ appears in it. With the brake released, the motor exerting no torque ($\tau_m = 0$) and friction negligible, dividing both sides by $J_o$ gives

$$
\ddot\theta = -\omega_n^2 \sin\theta , \qquad \omega_n^2 = \frac{m\mathfrak g\,l_c}{J_o} = \frac{3\mathfrak g}{2L_k} . \tag{7.1.9}
$$

In this example $J_o$ = {{ex7_1.Jo:.4f}} kg·m², $\omega_n^2$ = {{ex7_1.wn2:.2f}} s⁻², and $\omega_n$ = {{ex7_1.wn:.3f}} rad/s.

A second-order equation cannot be written directly in the form (7.1.5), because f may use only x itself, whereas the law of change here needs two quantities, θ and $\dot\theta$. The way out is to take the two together as the unknown. There is a physical reason for this: to predict how the link will move from now on, knowing the angle at this instant is not enough; we must also know the angular velocity at this instant. Once both are known, the future is uniquely determined.

**Definition 7.1.3 (State)** The **state** of a system at an instant is a set of numbers such that, knowing them and the inputs from then on, the subsequent motion of the system is uniquely determined. The quantities that make up the state are called **state variables**, and the set of all possible states is called the **state space**. An equation written as $\dot x = f(t, x, u)$ is called the **state equation** of the system, where u is the input (voltage, torque and so on).

For the link, take

$$
x = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \theta \\ \dot\theta \end{pmatrix} , \tag{7.1.10}
$$

then $\dot x_1 = x_2$ is simply the definition "the derivative of the angle is the angular velocity", and $\dot x_2$ is given by Eq. (7.1.8):

$$
\dot x = f(x) = \begin{pmatrix} x_2 \\ -\omega_n^2\sin x_1 - (b/J_o)\,x_2 + \tau_m/J_o \end{pmatrix} . \tag{7.1.11}
$$

One second-order equation has become a system of two first-order equations, of exactly the same form as Eq. (7.1.5), except that x has changed from a number into a column vector. The same is true of a general arm with n joints: Chapter 24 will derive its equations of motion $M(\theta)\ddot\theta + c(\theta, \dot\theta) + g(\theta) = \tau$, in which the mass matrix M(θ) is invertible. Taking the state $x = (\theta, \dot\theta) \in \mathbb R^{2n}$ gives

$$
\dot x = \begin{pmatrix} \dot\theta \\ M(\theta)^{-1}\big(\tau - c(\theta, \dot\theta) - g(\theta)\big) \end{pmatrix} . \tag{7.1.12}
$$

So however complicated the robot, once it is written as a state equation it can be solved with the same set of numerical methods. This is precisely the value of the state-space approach. It was introduced into control theory by Rudolf E. Kalman and others in the 1960s and has since become the common language of modern control and estimation (Chapters 42 and 46). The motor equation (7.1.4) is first order to begin with, and its state is simply the speed ω; when the inductance is taken into account in Section 7.4, the current also becomes a state variable.

### 7.1.5 The phase plane and equilibrium points

When the state has two components, it can be drawn as a point in a plane, with θ as the abscissa and $\dot\theta$ as the ordinate. This plane is called the **phase plane**. As the system moves, the state point traces a curve in the phase plane, called a trajectory. At every point, Eq. (7.1.11) gives $(\dot\theta, \ddot\theta)$, that is, the direction and the speed with which the state point moves there; drawing such a small arrow at every point of the phase plane gives Figure 7.1.3(b). The trajectories are tangent to the arrows everywhere. The geometric meaning of a differential equation becomes plain at a glance: the equation prescribes the "direction of flow" at every point, and a solution is the curve traced by following the flow.

The figure shows several different kinds of trajectory. When the energy is small, the link swings back and forth and the trajectory is a closed curve around the origin; going once round it takes one period. When the energy is large enough, the link goes over the highest point and keeps turning, and the trajectory runs on indefinitely, to the right above the θ-axis or to the left below it. The boundary between the two kinds of trajectory is called the **separatrix**; it corresponds to the motion in which the link just reaches the upright position, and takes an infinitely long time to get there.

**Equilibrium points.** A state at which f(x) = 0 is called an **equilibrium point**: a system that is there stays there for ever. For Eq. (7.1.11) (with $b = \tau_m = 0$), the equilibrium points satisfy $\dot\theta = 0$ and $\sin\theta = 0$, that is, θ = 0 (link hanging straight down) and θ = π (link pointing straight up). The two are completely different in nature: disturbed slightly at θ = 0, the link swings about nearby; disturbed slightly at θ = π, the link falls over. The former is called stable, the latter unstable. The rigorous definition of stability is given in Chapters 42 and 44; here we first see the difference between the two by linearization.

**Linearization.** Near an equilibrium point $x_e$, let $x = x_e + \delta x$ and apply Taylor's formula to each component of f, keeping only the first-order terms; this gives $\dot{\delta x} \approx A\,\delta x$. The (i, j) entry of A is the rate of change of $f_i$ with respect to $x_j$: the derivative obtained when only $x_j$ varies and the other components are held fixed, called the **partial derivative** and written $\partial f_i/\partial x_j$. The matrix made up of the partial derivatives,

$$
A = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x_e) = \begin{pmatrix} \partial f_1/\partial x_1 & \partial f_1/\partial x_2 \\ \partial f_2/\partial x_1 & \partial f_2/\partial x_2 \end{pmatrix}_{x = x_e} \tag{7.1.13}
$$

is called the **Jacobian matrix** of f at $x_e$. The process is called **linearization**. The Jacobian matrix will be used again in Section 7.3 for solving systems of equations, and in Chapter 16 it becomes the central tool of the velocity analysis of manipulators.

**Example 7.1.3** Find the Jacobian matrices of the link at its two equilibrium points, and their eigenvalues.

By Eq. (7.1.11), $\partial f_1/\partial x_1 = 0$, $\partial f_1/\partial x_2 = 1$, $\partial f_2/\partial x_1 = -\omega_n^2\cos x_1$, $\partial f_2/\partial x_2 = 0$. Hence

$$
A_0 = {{ex7_1.A0}} , \qquad A_\pi = {{ex7_1.Api}} . \tag{7.1.14}
$$

The characteristic polynomial of $A_0$ is $\lambda^2 + \omega_n^2$, with eigenvalues $\lambda = \pm i\,\omega_n = \pm i\,{{ex7_1.ev_wn:.3f}}$ s⁻¹, a pair of purely imaginary numbers; the eigenvalues of $A_\pi$ are $\lambda = \pm\omega_n$, one positive and one negative. Program 7.1.1 recomputes the two matrices by numerical differentiation and finds the eigenvalues by the methods of Chapter 2, with the same results.

The significance of the eigenvalues lies in the solution of the linear equation $\dot x = Ax$. By Section 4.4.4,

$$
x(t) = e^{At}\,x(0) . \tag{7.1.15}
$$

If A has an eigenvector v with eigenvalue λ, take x(0) = v; then $A^k v = \lambda^k v$, and substituting into the series of the matrix exponential gives $x(t) = e^{\lambda t}v$. So a disturbance decays along directions whose eigenvalue has a negative real part, grows exponentially along directions whose eigenvalue has a positive real part, and a purely imaginary eigenvalue corresponds to an oscillation of constant amplitude ($e^{i\omega t} = \cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t$, Euler's formula). Thus a small disturbance at θ = 0 oscillates with angular frequency $\omega_n$, while a small disturbance at θ = π grows like $e^{\omega_n t}$: the latter is unstable. This is why $\omega_n$ is called the **natural angular frequency**; the period for small swings is $T_0 = 2\pi/\omega_n$ = {{ex7_1.T0:.4f}} s. If friction is included, with $b/J_o$ = 1 s⁻¹, the lower-right entry of $A_0$ becomes −1 and the eigenvalues become {{ex7_1.evd_re:.1f}} ± {{ex7_1.evd_im:.3f}}i s⁻¹: the real part is negative, the swing gradually dies away, and the trajectory in the phase plane becomes a spiral winding into the origin.

Linearization is reliable only near an equilibrium point. Released from 5°, after 1 s the linear equation (7.1.15) gives θ = {{ex7_1.x_lin_deg:.3f}}°, while the numerical solution of the nonlinear equation gives {{ex7_1.x_non_deg:.3f}}°, a difference of only {{ex7_1.lin_gap_deg:.4f}}°. Released from 90°, things are quite different.

**Example 7.1.4** The link is released from rest in the horizontal position (θ = 90°). Find the period of the swing.

Now sin θ ≈ θ no longer holds, and there is no formula for the period in terms of elementary functions. Classical mechanics gives the result in terms of the complete elliptic integral of the first kind K, $T = 4K(\sin(\theta_0/2))/\omega_n$; the calculation gives T = {{ex7_1.T_ell:.4f}} s, {{ex7_1.ratio_T:.3f}} times the small-swing period. Program 7.1.1 also finds, by high-accuracy numerical integration, the time from release until the velocity of the link becomes zero for the second time (that is, one period), and obtains {{ex7_1.T_sim:.4f}} s; the two agree. The larger the amplitude, the longer the period; as the amplitude tends to 180°, the period tends to infinity, and this is exactly the motion on the separatrix.

### 7.1.6 Energy: a quantity that stays constant along trajectories

Without friction and external torques, the mechanical energy of the link stays constant. Multiply both sides of Eq. (7.1.8) by $\dot\theta$ and use the chain rule: $\ddot\theta\dot\theta = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\tfrac12\dot\theta^2\big)$, $\sin\theta\,\dot\theta = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(1 - \cos\theta)$. This gives

$$
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\Big[\frac12\dot\theta^2 + \omega_n^2(1 - \cos\theta)\Big] = 0 , \qquad
E = J_o\Big[\frac12\dot\theta^2 + \omega_n^2(1 - \cos\theta)\Big] . \tag{7.1.16}
$$

E is the sum of the kinetic energy $\frac12 J_o\dot\theta^2$ and the gravitational potential energy $m\mathfrak g\,l_c(1 - \cos\theta)$, measured from the lowest position. A zero derivative means that E stays constant along the true motion. So the trajectories in the phase plane are the level curves of E: each curve in Figure 7.1.3(b) is the set of points at which "the energy equals a certain value". When the link is released from horizontal, $E = J_o\omega_n^2$; at the bottom of the swing all the potential energy has become kinetic energy, and $\dot\theta = \sqrt{2}\,\omega_n$ = {{ex7_1.v_bottom:.3f}} rad/s. Program 7.1.1 takes 400 points along a high-accuracy numerical solution over three periods; the energy varies by no more than the order of $10^{-9}$.

Conservation of energy is a good yardstick for checking a simulation: for a real frictionless link the energy is strictly constant; if the energy of a simulation grows or shrinks swing after swing, that is error introduced by the numerical method. Section 7.2 will show that different methods behave completely differently in this respect, and that "whether the energy drifts" is precisely what decides whether a method can be used for long simulations. Chapter 24 will also use conservation of energy to check the derivation of the equations of motion.

### 7.1.7 Everyday examples

A cup of tea at 90 °C is left in a room at 20 °C. How fast its temperature falls is proportional to the difference between the tea and the room temperature: this is Newton's law of cooling, put forward in 1701. Written as an equation, $\tau\dot T + T = T_{\text{room}}$, it has exactly the same form as the motor equation (7.1.4), and the same solution: the temperature difference decays like $e^{-t/\tau}$, falling to 36.8% of its initial value after one time constant. First-order equations all describe processes that "approach a final value, more and more slowly the closer they get": a motor speeding up, a capacitor charging, hot tea cooling, a water tank draining.

A playground swing obeys the same equation as the link (7.1.9). For small swings the period hardly depends on the amplitude, as Galileo noticed at the beginning of the 17th century; for very high swings the period becomes noticeably longer. To get a swing going, you crouch and stand up at the right moments, which amounts to changing the "length of the pendulum" and feeding energy into the system; in the phase plane, the trajectory moves outwards from the inner loops, loop by loop.

This section has written robot models as state equations. For the first-order model of the motor we happened to find an analytic solution; for the link swinging with a large amplitude the period can still be found with an elliptic integral, but the angle at an arbitrary instant can no longer be written as a simple formula; as for the general equations of an arm (7.1.12), an analytic solution is out of the question. The next section discusses how to compute the solution of a state equation step by step on a computer, and how to know whether the result is accurate.

**Engineer's notes**

- Choose the state before modelling: to predict the future, which present quantities do you need to know? For the mechanical part they are usually positions and velocities; for the electrical part, usually currents (the current in an inductor cannot change abruptly) and voltages (the voltage across a capacitor cannot change abruptly). If the state is incomplete, the simulation will run into the contradiction of "the same state, different futures".
- The time constant is the first guide in choosing the sampling period and the simulation step. As a rule of thumb, the sampling period is taken as 1/10 to 1/20 of the fastest time constant; Sections 7.2 and 7.4 will explain why a simulation diverges when the step is too large.
- The starting current is far larger than the steady-state current. In this example the current at the instant the motor starts is {{ex7_1.i0:.0f}} A; a real drive limits it to within 2 to 3 times the rated current, in which case u in the model (7.1.3) is no longer constant and the acceleration is slower.
- Linearizing about an equilibrium point is the most common first step in analysing stability and designing controllers; but a linear model can be trusted only over a small range. With the link released from 5°, the error of linearization is less than 0.01°; released from 90°, the period is already off by about 18%.
- To judge whether a frictionless simulation can be trusted, first check whether energy is conserved. This check costs almost no extra computation, yet it catches most errors in the integration method, the step size and the derivation of the model.
