title: Chapter Overview
---

The previous chapters studied what a robot looks like "at a given instant": an orientation, a pose, a twist. When a robot actually does its work, all these quantities change with time, and the laws of change are almost always written as differential equations: how the speed of a motor rises with the voltage, how a link swings under gravity, how the orientation changes with the angular velocity read by a gyroscope. Most of these equations have no solution that can be written as a formula, so controllers, simulation software and digital twins can only compute them step by step by numerical methods. This chapter answers four questions: how to write the motion of a robot as differential equations and bring them into the unified form of the state space; how to solve them step by step on a computer, how large the error is and how to choose the step size; how to solve nonlinear equations such as those of inverse kinematics; and how to obtain derivatives from discrete measured data, and how to deal with "stiff" equations whose time scales differ enormously.

The mathematics of the chapter starts from secondary-school mathematics. Section 7.1 begins with the rate of change, gives the definition of the derivative and Taylor's formula, and then introduces ordinary differential equations, the state and the phase plane. Section 7.2 presents four kinds of numerical integration: Euler's method, Runge–Kutta methods, symplectic integration, which preserves the geometric structure of Hamiltonian mechanics, and Lie group integration, which keeps the orientation on SO(3) at all times; the order of the error of every method is measured by experiment, by "halving the step and seeing by what factor the error shrinks". Section 7.3 presents Newton's method and uses it to solve the inverse kinematics of a 2R arm. Section 7.4 deals with numerical differentiation and stiff problems; the former corresponds to measuring speed with an encoder, the latter to simulating a motor with a current loop.

The chapter uses the matrices, eigenvalues and systems of linear equations of Chapter 2, the cross product and skew-symmetric matrices of Chapter 3, the matrix exponential of Section 4.4 in Chapter 4, and the rule "gyroscope readings multiply on the right" of Section 6.2 in Chapter 6. The methods of this chapter run through the whole book: the Gauss–Newton method of Chapter 8 is the extension of Newton's method to least-squares problems; the numerical inverse kinematics of Chapter 15 and the Jacobian of Chapter 16 use Section 7.3 directly; the dynamics simulation of Chapters 23–29 (especially the time step and stability of Chapter 29) and the controller design and simulation of Chapters 42 and 43 all have to choose an integration method and a step size; the motor model of Chapter 36 and the encoder and inertial measurement unit of Chapter 38 first appear here as examples; the state estimation of Chapter 46 uses state equations and orientation integration.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. explain clearly what derivatives, differential equations, states and the phase plane mean, and write robot models such as motors and links as state equations $\dot x = f(t, x)$;
2. write down Euler's method, the midpoint method, the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method, the symplectic Euler method and the Störmer–Verlet method, measure the order of a method by halving the step, and decide whether Euler's method is stable at a given step size;
3. explain the cause of energy drift in long simulations, and say when symplectic integration should be chosen;
4. integrate the orientation from gyroscope readings, and explain why it must be updated on SO(3) with the exponential map rather than by integrating the matrix entries directly;
5. solve nonlinear equations and systems of equations by Newton's method, and explain quadratic convergence, the influence of the initial guess and the cases in which it fails;
6. choose the form of a difference quotient and its difference step and estimate the error of encoder speed measurement; recognize stiff equations, and simulate them stably with the implicit Euler method.
