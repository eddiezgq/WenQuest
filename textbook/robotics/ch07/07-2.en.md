### 7.2.1 The problem

Section 7.1 wrote down the state equation (7.1.11) of the link. After the link is released from horizontal, there is no formula in elementary functions for its angle at an arbitrary instant; still less for the equations (7.1.12) of a six-axis arm. Yet simulation software, digital twins and controllers have to answer "what is the state at the next instant" all the time. Their way of doing it is to cut time into short intervals, each of length h, and, starting from the known initial state, to push forward one interval after another. What this gives is approximate values $x_k \approx x(t_k)$ of the solution at a sequence of instants

$$
t_k = t_0 + k\,h , \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots \tag{7.2.1}
$$

This procedure is called **numerical integration**, and h is called the **step size**. The word "integration" comes from the fact that finding the course of change from the rate of change is exactly what integration does.

Numerical integration has to answer three questions: how to advance each step; how far the result is from the true solution, and how this depends on the step size; and whether absurd results can appear when the step is too large. This section first makes the three questions clear with Euler's method, then introduces the more accurate Runge–Kutta methods, symplectic integration for long simulations and Lie group integration for orientations, and in every case measures how the error scales with h by "halving the step and seeing by what factor the error shrinks".

### 7.2.2 Euler's method

By Taylor's formula of Section 7.1, over a very short time h, $x(t + h) \approx x(t) + h\,\dot x(t)$, and $\dot x(t) = f(t, x(t))$ is given by the equation. Use this as a recurrence:

$$
x_{k+1} = x_k + h\,f(t_k, x_k) . \tag{7.2.2}
$$

This is the **Euler method**. Geometrically, each step starts from the current point and walks a straight segment in the direction (the tangent) that the equation prescribes at that point (Figure 7.2.1). Leonhard Euler proposed the method in the first volume of his *Institutiones calculi integralis* (Foundations of integral calculus), published in 1768, to approximate the solutions of differential equations that could not then be solved analytically. Two and a half centuries later it is still one of the most widely used methods, and the default integrator of physics engines such as MuJoCo is a variant of it (Section 7.2.6).

**Example 7.2.1** Simulate the motor start-up of Example 7.1.2 with Euler's method, h = {{ex7_2.h1_ms:.0f}} ms. Find the speed at t = 50 ms and compare it with the analytic solution.

The equation is $\dot\omega = (\omega_\infty - \omega)/\tau$, with $\omega_\infty$ = {{ex7_2.w_inf:.2f}} rad/s, τ = {{ex7_2.tau_ms:.3f}} ms and ω₀ = 0. First step: $\omega_1 = 0 + h\,\omega_\infty/\tau$ = {{ex7_2.s1:.1f}} rad/s; second step: $\omega_2 = \omega_1 + h(\omega_\infty - \omega_1)/\tau$ = {{ex7_2.s2:.1f}} rad/s; continuing for ten steps gives $\omega_{10}$ = {{ex7_2.w1:.2f}} rad/s, while the analytic solution is {{ex7_2.w_exact:.2f}} rad/s, an error of {{ex7_2.e1:.2f}} rad/s, about {{ex7_2.e1_rel:.1f}}%.

For this linear equation the result of Euler's method can also be written as a formula. Let $\delta_k = \omega_k - \omega_\infty$; by Eq. (7.2.2), $\delta_{k+1} = (1 - h/\tau)\,\delta_k$, so

$$
\omega_k = \omega_\infty\Big[1 - \Big(1 - \frac{h}{\tau}\Big)^k\Big] . \tag{7.2.3}
$$

The exact solution is $\omega(t_k) = \omega_\infty[1 - (e^{-h/\tau})^k]$. The only difference between the two is that 1 − h/τ replaces $e^{-h/\tau}$, and the former is precisely the first-order Taylor approximation of the latter. This observation will be used again when we discuss stability.

### 7.2.3 Errors and the order of a method

Figure 7.2.1 shows two kinds of error. The deviation produced by a single step starting from a point on the exact solution comes from the approximation made by the method itself; the total deviation from the exact solution after many steps is the result of the deviations of all the steps accumulating and being propagated by the later steps.

**Definition 7.2.1 (Local truncation error and global error)** Let x(t) be the exact solution. The difference between the result of the one-step method applied to the exact value $x(t_k)$ and $x(t_{k+1})$ is called the **local truncation error** of step k + 1; the difference between the numerical solution and the exact solution, $e_k = x_k - x(t_k)$, is called the **global error**.

For Euler's method, the local truncation error is just $x(t_k + h) - x(t_k) - h\dot x(t_k)$. By Theorem 7.1.1, if $|\ddot x| \le M$, it is at most $Mh^2/2$, proportional to h². Is the global error proportional to h² as well? No. The theorem below shows that it is only proportional to h, and the reason is simple: reaching a fixed time T takes T/h steps, the number of steps is inversely proportional to h, and h² times 1/h leaves h.

**Theorem 7.2.1 (Global error of Euler's method)** Let f satisfy the Lipschitz condition (7.1.7) with constant L, and let the exact solution satisfy $|\ddot x| \le M$. Then, integrating with Euler's method from $t_0$ to $t_N = t_0 + Nh$, the global error satisfies

$$
|e_N| \le \frac{Mh}{2L}\Big(e^{L(t_N - t_0)} - 1\Big) . \tag{7.2.4}
$$

*Proof*　The exact solution satisfies $x(t_{k+1}) = x(t_k) + h f(t_k, x(t_k)) + d_k$, where $|d_k| \le Mh^2/2$ is the local truncation error. Subtracting this from Eq. (7.2.2),

$$
e_{k+1} = e_k + h\big[f(t_k, x_k) - f(t_k, x(t_k))\big] - d_k .
$$

By the Lipschitz condition the absolute value of the bracket is at most $L|e_k|$, so $|e_{k+1}| \le (1 + hL)|e_k| + Mh^2/2$. Using this inequality repeatedly, starting from $e_0 = 0$,

$$
|e_N| \le \frac{Mh^2}{2}\big[1 + (1 + hL) + \cdots + (1 + hL)^{N-1}\big] = \frac{Mh^2}{2}\cdot\frac{(1 + hL)^N - 1}{hL} .
$$

Finally, $1 + hL \le e^{hL}$ (from the series of $e^{hL}$, whose terms are all non-negative) gives $(1 + hL)^N \le e^{NhL} = e^{L(t_N - t_0)}$, which yields Eq. (7.2.4).　∎

In Eq. (7.2.4), everything except h is independent of the step size, so the global error is proportional to h. This property has a name of its own.

**Definition 7.2.2 (Order of a method)** If the global error of a method over a fixed time interval satisfies $|e_N| \le C h^p$, with C independent of h, the method is said to be of order p. Euler's method is a first-order method.

The order can be measured by experiment: if the error ≈ $Ch^p$, after halving the step the error becomes $C(h/2)^p$, and the ratio of the two is $2^p$. So we need only halve the step again and again and see by what factor the error shrinks: a factor of about 2 means first order, about 4 second order, about 16 fourth order. For Example 7.2.1, with h = 5, 2.5, 1.25 and 0.625 ms, the errors at 50 ms are {{ex7_2.em1:.3f}}, {{ex7_2.em2:.3f}}, {{ex7_2.em3:.3f}} and {{ex7_2.em4:.3f}} rad/s, and the ratios of neighbours are {{ex7_2.rm1:.3f}}, {{ex7_2.rm2:.3f}} and {{ex7_2.rm3:.3f}}: first order indeed.

The drawback of a first-order method is obvious: to reduce the error to a thousandth, the computation has to grow a thousandfold. Section 7.2.5 will give methods whose error falls faster with h.

### 7.2.4 Numerical stability: what happens when the step is too large

In Eq. (7.2.3), each step multiplies the deviation from the final value by 1 − h/τ. For h < τ this factor lies between 0 and 1, and the deviation decreases monotonically; for τ < h < 2τ the factor lies between −1 and 0, the deviation decreases while alternating in sign, and the numerical solution converges while oscillating above and below the final value; for h > 2τ the factor is less than −1, the deviation alternates in sign and grows larger and larger, and the numerical solution diverges. Yet the real speed of the motor never exceeds its final value.

**Example 7.2.2** For the motor of Example 7.2.1, take h = 30 ms and h = 45 ms.

τ = {{ex7_2.tau_ms:.2f}} ms and 2τ = {{ex7_2.hmax_ms:.2f}} ms. For h = 30 ms the factor is {{ex7_2.g30:.3f}}; the first two steps give {{ex7_2.w30_1:,.0f}} rad/s and {{ex7_2.w30_2:,.0f}} rad/s, jumping up and down about the final value, and after 0.3 s the result is {{ex7_2.w30_end:.1f}} rad/s. For h = 45 ms the factor is {{ex7_2.g45:.3f}}; the first two steps give {{ex7_2.w45_1:,.0f}} rad/s and {{ex7_2.w45_2:,.0f}} rad/s, step {{ex7_2.n45}} already reaches {{ex7_2.w45_last:,.0f}} rad/s, and it keeps growing (Figure 7.2.2). The simulated speed is at one moment negative and at another ten times the final speed, which is plainly absurd. The trouble lies not in the model but in the step size.

This is not the same thing as accuracy: an error that is large but gradually dies away is completely different in nature from an error that is amplified step by step. To study it separately from any particular problem, methods are tested on the simplest possible "test equation".

**Definition 7.2.3 (Numerical stability and region of absolute stability)** For the test equation $\dot x = \lambda x$ (λ complex; for Re λ < 0 the exact solution decays), if a one-step method gives $x_{k+1} = G(h\lambda)\,x_k$, then G is called the amplification factor of the method. The set of complex numbers hλ for which |G(hλ)| ≤ 1 is called the **region of absolute stability** of the method. When hλ lies in this region, the numerical solution does not grow without bound, and the method is said to have **numerical stability** at that step size.

**Theorem 7.2.2** The amplification factor of Euler's method is G(z) = 1 + z, and its region of absolute stability is the disc in the complex plane with centre −1 and radius 1. For real λ < 0, Euler's method is stable if and only if $h \le 2/|\lambda|$.

*Proof*　Substituting f = λx into Eq. (7.2.2), $x_{k+1} = x_k + h\lambda x_k = (1 + h\lambda)x_k$, so G(z) = 1 + z. |1 + z| ≤ 1 says that the distance from z to −1 is at most 1, which is the disc above. When λ is a negative real number, the condition becomes −1 ≤ 1 + hλ ≤ 1, that is, $0 < h \le 2/|\lambda|$.　∎

The motor equation is exactly the test equation (for the deviation δ), with λ = −1/τ, so the condition for stability is h ≤ 2τ. The stability of a nonlinear equation near a given state can be judged by the linearization of Section 7.1.5: take λ to be the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix. A system usually has several eigenvalues, and the step must put every hλ inside the region of stability; the fastest mode (the one with the largest |λ|) sets the upper limit on the step. Section 7.4 will show that when the fastest and the slowest modes differ enormously, this limit makes explicit methods pay a very high price.

One more kind of eigenvalue deserves attention: the eigenvalues of the frictionless link at θ = 0 are ±iω_n, purely imaginary. Then $|G(\pm ih\omega_n)| = \sqrt{1 + h^2\omega_n^2} > 1$: however small h is, every step of Euler's method enlarges the swing a tiny bit. Section 7.2.6 will show what this means for long simulations.

### 7.2.5 Runge–Kutta methods

Euler's method uses only the slope at the starting point. If the slope changes over the interval, using only the slope at the start is bound to be off. A natural improvement is to take half a step with Euler's method first, to estimate the state at the midpoint of the interval, and then to take the full step with the slope at the midpoint:

$$
k_1 = f(t_k, x_k), \qquad k_2 = f\Big(t_k + \frac h2,\ x_k + \frac h2 k_1\Big), \qquad x_{k+1} = x_k + h\,k_2 . \tag{7.2.5}
$$

This is called the **midpoint method**. It evaluates f twice per step, and in return it has second-order accuracy.

**Theorem 7.2.3** If f is sufficiently smooth, the local truncation error of the midpoint method is proportional to h³, and the method is of second order.

*Proof*　For simplicity, let f depend only on x, and let x be a number (the derivation in the general case is the same, only longer to write out). The second-order Taylor expansion of the exact solution is $x(t+h) = x + h\dot x + \frac{h^2}{2}\ddot x + O(h^3)$, where $\dot x = f(x)$ and, by the chain rule, $\ddot x = f'(x)\dot x = f'(x)f(x)$; the notation $O(h^3)$ denotes a quantity whose absolute value is at most some constant times $h^3$. On the other hand, applying the first-order Taylor formula to $k_2 = f(x + \frac h2 f(x))$ gives $k_2 = f(x) + \frac h2 f'(x)f(x) + O(h^2)$, so

$$
x + h k_2 = x + h f + \frac{h^2}{2}f'f + O(h^3) .
$$

This agrees term by term with the expansion of the exact solution, differing only in the $O(h^3)$ term. The local truncation error is proportional to h³, and by the argument in the proof of Theorem 7.2.1, after 1/h steps have accumulated the global error is proportional to h².　∎

Program 7.2.1 checks this for the link as a pendulum at an arbitrarily chosen state (0.7 rad, −1.3 rad/s): for h = 0.02 s and 0.01 s, the one-step errors of the midpoint method are $ {{ex7_2.loc1:sci2}} $ and $ {{ex7_2.loc2:sci2}} $, with ratio {{ex7_2.loc_ratio:.2f}}, close to 2³ = 8.

Take this idea one step further: take the slopes at several points in the interval and average them with weights chosen so that the Taylor expansion agrees with that of the exact solution to a higher order. The German mathematician Carl Runge first proposed methods of this kind in 1895; Karl Heun in 1900 and Wilhelm Kutta in 1901 developed them further, and Kutta gave the following fourth-order method, the most widely used of all:

$$
\begin{aligned}
k_1 &= f(t_k, x_k), & k_2 &= f\Big(t_k + \frac h2,\ x_k + \frac h2 k_1\Big), \\
k_3 &= f\Big(t_k + \frac h2,\ x_k + \frac h2 k_2\Big), & k_4 &= f(t_k + h,\ x_k + h k_3), \\
x_{k+1} &= x_k + \frac h6\,(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4) .
\end{aligned} \tag{7.2.6}
$$

Methods of this kind are collectively called **Runge–Kutta methods**, and Eq. (7.2.6) is called the classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method, RK4 for short. The four slopes are taken at the start, twice at the midpoint, and at the end; the weights of the two midpoint slopes add up to 4, and the weights 1 : 4 : 1 for the start, the midpoint and the end are exactly the weights of Simpson's quadrature rule. Proving that it is of fourth order requires expanding the exact solution up to the h⁴ term and comparing term by term, with a great many terms; the systematic approach is the theory of "rooted trees" developed by the New Zealand mathematician John C. Butcher in the 1960s; see the chapter references. Here we confirm its order by experiment.

### 7.2.6 Symplectic integration: energy in long simulations

Section 7.2.5 compared accuracy over 2 s. When a robot is simulated working continuously for minutes or hours, or simulated millions of times over in reinforcement learning for training robots, what matters is not only short-term accuracy but also long-term "quality": for a frictionless link, energy should be conserved; if the simulated energy keeps growing or shrinking, then over a long time the motion becomes unrecognizable.

**Example 7.2.4** The link is released from horizontal (without friction). Compare the energy errors of the methods in a long simulation.

The results are plotted in Figure 7.2.4. The energy of Euler's method (h = 0.02 s) grows fast: after 10 s it is {{ex7_2.eu10x:.2f}} times its initial value; once the energy exceeds twice its initial value, the link has already gone over the top and is turning round and round, whereas the real link only swings between ±90°. RK4 does much better: with h = 0.02 s the energy has fallen by {{ex7_2.r4_100:.4f}}% after 100 s and by {{ex7_2.r4_1000:.3f}}% after 1000 s; the error is small, but it keeps accumulating with time. If RK4 is to cost the same as the Verlet method below (h = 0.02 s, one evaluation of f per step), its step must be h = 0.08 s, and then the energy has fallen by {{ex7_2.r4c_100:.1f}}% after 100 s and by {{ex7_2.r4c_1000:.0f}}% after 1000 s, and the amplitude of the swing visibly decays. The other two methods behave quite differently: the energy error of the symplectic Euler method (h = 0.02 s) oscillates back and forth within ±{{ex7_2.se_max:.1f}}%; for the Störmer–Verlet method the largest error in the first 100 s is {{ex7_2.vv_max_100:.3f}}%, and over 1000 s it is still {{ex7_2.vv_max:.3f}}%, with no tendency to accumulate.

This phenomenon, in which the energy error keeps accumulating in one direction, is called **energy drift**. To understand why some methods drift and others do not, we need to look at the motion of the link from another angle.

**Phase space and area.** Introduce the angular momentum $p = J_o\dot\theta$ and write the energy as a function of θ and p, $H(\theta, p) = p^2/(2J_o) + m\mathfrak g\,l_c(1 - \cos\theta)$. Then Eq. (7.1.8) (without friction and external torque) can be written as

$$
\dot\theta = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p} , \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial\theta} . \tag{7.2.7}
$$

Equations given in this way by the partial derivatives of a single function H are called a **Hamiltonian system**; H is called the Hamiltonian, and the space in which (θ, p) lives is called the **phase space**. This formulation of mechanics was put forward in 1834–1835 by the Irish mathematician William Rowan Hamilton (the discoverer of the quaternions of Chapter 4). Every mechanical system without friction whose forces come only from a potential (a conservative system) can be written in this form. Hamiltonian systems have two basic properties: H is constant along the motion (as in Eq. (7.1.16)); and, if we take any small region of phase space and let every point in it move according to the equations, the shape of the region changes but its area does not. The latter is called Liouville's theorem (Joseph Liouville, 1838).

That the area does not change can be seen as follows. After a very short time h, the state changes from x to $x + h f(x) + O(h^2)$. How this transformation stretches things near a point is determined by its Jacobian matrix $I + h\,\partial f/\partial x + O(h^2)$; Chapter 2 explained that the determinant is the factor by which areas are magnified, and $\det(I + hA) = 1 + h\,\mathrm{tr}A + O(h^2)$. For Eq. (7.2.7), $\mathrm{tr}(\partial f/\partial x) = \partial^2 H/\partial\theta\partial p - \partial^2 H/\partial p\partial\theta = 0$, so each short time h changes the area only by $O(h^2)$; covering a fixed time T takes T/h such pieces, so the total change is at most $O(h)$, and letting h → 0 shows that the area is exactly unchanged.

One step of a numerical method is also a transformation, taking $(\theta_k, p_k)$ to $(\theta_{k+1}, p_{k+1})$. What is the Jacobian determinant of Euler's method? For the link,

$$
\det\frac{\partial(\theta_{k+1}, \dot\theta_{k+1})}{\partial(\theta_k, \dot\theta_k)} = \det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h\omega_n^2\cos\theta_k & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1 + h^2\omega_n^2\cos\theta_k . \tag{7.2.8}
$$

While the link is in the lower half of its circle (cos θ > 0), it is greater than 1: every step enlarges the area in phase space a little. Figure 7.2.5(a) shows this: a small patch of initial states in phase space, after 2 s of Euler's method (h = 0.05 s), has an area nearly 6 times its original area, whereas the exact flow keeps the area unchanged (Figure 7.2.5(c)). A transformation that keeps enlarging areas cannot keep the state point on its original energy curve; this is where the energy drift comes from.

**Definition 7.2.4 (Symplectic map)** In a two-dimensional phase space, a transformation whose Jacobian determinant is identically 1, that is, which preserves area, is called a **symplectic map**. A numerical method whose one-step map is a symplectic map is called a symplectic method, and numerical integration with such a method is called **symplectic integration**. (In higher dimensions the "symplectic" condition is stronger than preserving volume: it requires the preservation of an antisymmetric bilinear form called the symplectic form; see the book by Hairer, Lubich and Wanner.)

A small change to Euler's method is enough to obtain a symplectic method: first update the angular velocity with the old angle, then update the angle with the new angular velocity,

$$
\dot\theta_{k+1} = \dot\theta_k - h\,\omega_n^2\sin\theta_k , \qquad \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + h\,\dot\theta_{k+1} . \tag{7.2.9}
$$

This is called the **symplectic Euler method**, also the semi-implicit Euler method. It costs the same as Euler's method; the only difference is the order of the updates. The default integrator of MuJoCo is precisely this semi-implicit Euler method, "update the velocity first, then update the position with the new velocity".

**Theorem 7.2.4** The one-step map of the symplectic Euler method (7.2.9) is a symplectic map. For the linearized link (sin θ replaced by θ), it exactly preserves

$$
Q = \dot\theta^2 + \omega_n^2\theta^2 - h\,\omega_n^2\,\theta\,\dot\theta , \tag{7.2.10}
$$

so the energy $\frac12(\dot\theta^2 + \omega_n^2\theta^2)$ differs from the conserved quantity Q/2 only by the term $\frac h2\omega_n^2\theta\dot\theta$, which is proportional to h and changes sign back and forth as the motion proceeds; when $h\omega_n < 2$, Q is a positive definite quadratic form, the state is confined to an ellipse, and the energy cannot drift in one direction.

*Proof*　Split one step into two transformations: first $(\theta, \dot\theta) \mapsto (\theta, \dot\theta - h\omega_n^2\sin\theta)$, then $(\theta, \dot\theta') \mapsto (\theta + h\dot\theta', \dot\theta')$. Their Jacobian matrices are $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -h\omega_n^2\cos\theta & 1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & h \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, both triangular, with determinant 1. When the two transformations are composed, the Jacobian matrices are multiplied, and so are the determinants; the result is still 1.

For the linear case, write $\dot\theta' = \dot\theta - h\omega_n^2\theta$ and $\theta' = \theta + h\dot\theta'$. Then

$$
\omega_n^2\theta'^2 - h\omega_n^2\theta'\dot\theta' = \omega_n^2\theta'(\theta' - h\dot\theta') = \omega_n^2\theta'\theta = \omega_n^2\theta^2 + h\omega_n^2\theta\dot\theta' ,
$$

so $Q' = \dot\theta'^2 + \omega_n^2\theta^2 + h\omega_n^2\theta\dot\theta' = \dot\theta'(\dot\theta' + h\omega_n^2\theta) + \omega_n^2\theta^2 = \dot\theta'\dot\theta + \omega_n^2\theta^2$. In the same way, $Q = \dot\theta(\dot\theta - h\omega_n^2\theta) + \omega_n^2\theta^2 = \dot\theta\dot\theta' + \omega_n^2\theta^2$. The two are equal.　∎

Program 7.2.1 checks by numerical differentiation that, at the state (0.7 rad, −1.3 rad/s) with h = 0.02 s, the Jacobian determinant of the one-step map is {{ex7_2.det_eu:.6f}} for Euler's method, in agreement with Eq. (7.2.8); {{ex7_2.det_se:.6f}} for the symplectic Euler method; also {{ex7_2.det_vv:.6f}} for the Störmer–Verlet method; and {{ex7_2.det_r4:.10f}} for RK4, slightly less than 1.

The second half of Theorem 7.2.4 reveals the essence of symplectic methods: they do not preserve the original energy exactly; instead they preserve exactly (or approximately over extremely long times) a "modified energy" that differs from the original energy by O(h). This result also holds for general Hamiltonian systems, with the modification proportional to $h^p$, where p is the order of the method; it was proved in the 1990s by "backward error analysis". This is why the energy error of a symplectic method is bounded and does not drift.

The symplectic Euler method is of first order. Symmetrizing it gives the second-order **Störmer–Verlet method**:

$$
\dot\theta_{k+\frac12} = \dot\theta_k + \frac h2\,a(\theta_k) , \qquad \theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + h\,\dot\theta_{k+\frac12} , \qquad \dot\theta_{k+1} = \dot\theta_{k+\frac12} + \frac h2\,a(\theta_{k+1}) , \tag{7.2.11}
$$

where $a(\theta) = -\omega_n^2\sin\theta$ is the angular acceleration. Each of the three substeps is a transformation that "changes only one variable", with determinant 1, so this too is a symplectic method. The $a(\theta_{k+1})$ computed at the end of one step is exactly what the next step needs at its start, so only one evaluation of the acceleration is needed per step. The Norwegian mathematician Carl Størmer used it in 1907 to compute the orbits of charged particles in the Earth's magnetic field, in order to explain the aurora; the French physicist Loup Verlet applied it to molecular dynamics in 1967, and from then on it became widely known. Systematic work on symplectic methods began with a report by René de Vogelaere in the 1950s; in the 1980s the foundations were laid independently by Ronald Ruth (1983) and by the Chinese mathematician Feng Kang (at the international symposium on differential geometry and differential equations held in Beijing in 1984); Feng Kang and his school call them "symplectic geometric algorithms for Hamiltonian systems".

**How to choose.** Over short times RK4 is the most accurate; as long as the step is small enough, its energy drift is negligible in many applications. The strength of symplectic methods lies in long times and fairly large steps: in Example 7.2.4, at equal cost, the energy error of the Verlet method stays within {{ex7_2.vv_max:.2f}}% over 1000 s, while RK4 has already lost half its energy. This is why physics engines, molecular dynamics and celestial mechanics generally use symplectic methods. For robots with friction and control inputs, the system itself does not conserve energy and the advantage of symplectic methods is smaller, but "not creating or destroying energy artificially" remains a valuable property: a simulated robot will not start shaking for no reason, nor stop for no reason.

### 7.2.7 Lie group integration: integrating the orientation from gyroscope readings

The methods so far assume that the state is a vector in $\mathbb R^n$ that can be added freely. An orientation is not such a quantity. The gyroscope in the inertial measurement unit of a quadrotor or on the end-effector of an arm measures the body angular velocity $\omega_b$. By Eq. (6.2.5) of Chapter 6, the orientation satisfies

$$
\dot R = R\,[\omega_b(t)] , \tag{7.2.12}
$$

where R is the rotation matrix of the body frame {b} relative to the space frame {s}, and $[\omega_b]$ is a skew-symmetric matrix. R must remain a rotation matrix at all times: $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$, det R = 1.

Treating the nine entries of R as ordinary state variables and applying Euler's method directly gives

$$
R_{k+1} = R_k\big(I + h[\omega_b(t_k)]\big) . \tag{7.2.13}
$$

The trouble is that $I + h[\omega]$ is not a rotation matrix: its eigenvalues are 1 and $1 \pm ih|\omega|$, and its determinant is $1 + h^2|\omega|^2 > 1$. With each multiplication R strays further from SO(3): its columns are no longer of unit length, nor mutually perpendicular. Chapter 6 has already pointed out the correct way: within one step, treat the angular velocity as constant and update with the exponential map,

$$
R_{k+1} = R_k\,\exp\big(h[\omega_b(t_k)]\big) , \tag{7.2.14}
$$

with $\exp$ computed by Rodrigues' formula (4.4.5). The product of two rotation matrices is again a rotation matrix, so $R_k$ stays on SO(3) for ever, with no error other than the computer's rounding error. Methods of this kind, which advance on a Lie group by the exponential map so that the numerical solution always keeps the structure of the Lie group, are called **Lie group integration**.

**Theorem 7.2.5** If $R_0 \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$, then all the $R_k$ obtained by Eq. (7.2.14), or by Eqs. (7.2.15) and (7.2.17) below, belong to SO(3).

*Proof*　By Section 6.2, the exponential of a skew-symmetric matrix is a rotation matrix; SO(3) is closed under multiplication (Chapter 4). Induction on k gives the result.　∎

Eq. (7.2.14) is called the Lie–Euler method. Like Euler's method, it lets the angular velocity at the start represent the whole interval, and it is of first order. To raise the order, we can, as in the midpoint method, use the angular velocity at the midpoint of the interval:

$$
R_{k+1} = R_k\,\exp\Big(h\Big[\omega_b\Big(t_k + \frac h2\Big)\Big]\Big) . \tag{7.2.15}
$$

This is the formula already used in Section 6.2.5 of Chapter 6, and it is of second order (the Lie midpoint method). Going further, the direction of the angular velocity also changes within a step, and "first turning about this axis and then about that one" is not the same as "turning about the average axis" (the Lie bracket of Section 6.7 in Chapter 6); a fourth-order method has to make up for this difference. In 1954 the German mathematician Wilhelm Magnus gave the solution of linear matrix differential equations in exponential form, called the **Magnus expansion**. The fourth-order method obtained by truncating it is as follows: take the angular velocities $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ at the two Gauss points $t_k + (\frac12 \mp \frac{\sqrt3}{6})h$ of the interval, and let

$$
\Omega = \frac h2(\omega_1 + \omega_2) + \frac{\sqrt3}{12}h^2\,\omega_1 \times \omega_2 , \tag{7.2.16}
$$

$$
R_{k+1} = R_k\,\exp\big([\Omega]\big) . \tag{7.2.17}
$$

The cross-product term $\omega_1\times\omega_2$ is exactly the vector form of the Lie bracket $[[\omega_1], [\omega_2]] = [\omega_1\times\omega_2]$ (Section 6.7 of Chapter 6): it is non-zero only when the axis of rotation is changing. Systematizing one-step methods on SO(3) into "Runge–Kutta methods on Lie groups" was the work of Peter Crouch and Robert Grossman in 1993 and of Hans Munthe-Kaas in 1995–1998; the survey by Arieh Iserles and others in 2000 summed up the field. The "coning compensation" algorithms of inertial navigation, which compute the attitude from gyroscope increments, rest essentially on the same idea.

**Example 7.2.5** A quadrotor performs a coning motion: $R(t) = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, at)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, bt)$, with a = {{ex7_2_lie.a:.0f}} rad/s and b = {{ex7_2_lie.b:.0f}} rad/s. Its body angular velocity is

$$
\omega_b(t) = \big(b,\ a\sin bt,\ a\cos bt\big)^{\mathsf T} \ \mathrm{rad/s} . \tag{7.2.18}
$$

Integrate from $\omega_b(t)$ over 2 s with five methods, and compare the orientation errors and the orthogonality errors $\|R^{\mathsf T}R - I\|$.

First check Eq. (7.2.18): $\dot R = [a\hat z]\,R + R\,[b\hat x]$, so $R^{\mathsf T}\dot R = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, bt)^{\mathsf T}\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, at)^{\mathsf T}[a\hat z]\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, at)\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, bt) + [b\hat x]$. By Eq. (3.4.6), $R^{\mathsf T}[\omega]R = [R^{\mathsf T}\omega]$; noting that $\mathrm{Rot}(\hat z, at)^{\mathsf T}\hat z = \hat z$ (an axis is not changed by a rotation about itself), we get $\omega_b = a\,\mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, bt)^{\mathsf T}\hat z + b\hat x$, which is Eq. ### 7.2.8 Everyday examples

When you navigate with a map on your phone and lose the satellite signal in a tunnel, the phone "dead-reckons" its position from the speed and direction it had before entering the tunnel. This is Euler's method: every short interval, the position is increased by velocity times time. The error of dead reckoning accumulates with time; when you come out of the tunnel, the position on the map is often some way off the actual one, and it is corrected only when the satellite signal returns. Inertial navigation and robot odometry (Chapter 19) face the same problem.

Celestial mechanics provides the best example of symplectic methods. Simulate the Earth going round the Sun with Euler's method, and every orbit is a little larger than the one before; with a fairly large step, after a few dozen orbits the Earth has "escaped" from the solar system. With the Störmer–Verlet method, even with a fairly large step, the energy error stays bounded throughout, the orbit does not spiral outwards, and the error shows up only as a slow drift in the phase of the orbit and a tiny precession of the orbit. The long-term computations that study whether the solar system is stable over billions of years rely mainly on specially designed symplectic methods.

Numerical integration turns a differential equation into a sequence of algebraic operations. But Section 7.4 will show that some methods (implicit methods) have to solve an equation at every step; and in inverse kinematics we face nonlinear equations directly, such as "given the position of the end-effector, find the joint angles". The next section discusses how to solve nonlinear equations numerically.

**Engineer's notes**

- The order in which to choose an integration method: first ask whether the system conserves energy and how long it has to be simulated. For short times with accuracy required, use RK4 or an adaptive-step method (such as RK45 in SciPy or ode45 in MATLAB; they are based on the Dormand–Prince formulas of 1980, which compute a fourth-order and a fifth-order result at every step, use their difference to estimate the error and adjust the step automatically); for long simulations of mechanical systems, use the symplectic Euler method or the Störmer–Verlet method.
- Once an integration program is written, the first thing to do is to measure its order: take three or four step sizes, each half the previous one, and see whether the error ratios are close to $2^p$. If the ratios are wrong, the program almost certainly has a bug, for example a slope evaluated at the wrong time, or a component of the state that is not updated.
- The upper limit on the step is set by the fastest mode: for Euler's method, h < 2/|λ|_max; for RK4, on the negative real axis, about h < {{ex7_2.rk4_lim:.3f}}/|λ|_max. Discretization in controllers (Chapter 42) must satisfy the same condition.
- Always integrate the orientation by updating with the exponential map or with quaternion multiplication, with the gyroscope reading on the right (Chapter 6). If all you have is the average angular velocity over each sampling period, Eq. (7.2.14) is already second-order accurate, because the average plays the role of the midpoint value.
- Do not use "a small enough step" to cover up a structural defect of a method: when Euler's method integrates a frictionless system, however small the step, the energy only drifts more slowly; it does not stop drifting. When the matrix entries are integrated, however small the step, the orthogonality error only grows more slowly.
