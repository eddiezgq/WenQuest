### 7.3.1 The problem

A planar two-link arm (a 2R arm) has an upper arm of length $l_1$ = 0.425 m and a forearm of length $l_2$ = 0.392 m, the same as the upper arm and forearm of the UR5e. The controller receives a command: move the end-effector to $p_d$ = (0.45, 0.35) m. What should the two joint angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ be?

Finding the position of the end-effector from the joint angles is a direct calculation (Chapters 2 and 12):

$$
p(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} l_1\cos\theta_1 + l_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \\ l_1\sin\theta_1 + l_2\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \end{pmatrix} . \tag{7.3.1}
$$

Going the other way, from the position of the end-effector to the joint angles, means solving the equation $p(\theta) = p_d$; this is called inverse kinematics. For a 2R arm an analytic solution can be found with the law of cosines (Chapter 14); but for a general six- or seven-axis arm an analytic solution either does not exist or is extremely tedious to derive, and in engineering practice numerical methods are used almost everywhere (Chapter 15). At the heart of these numerical methods is Newton's method, the subject of this section. We use the 2R arm as a test case because it has an analytic solution to check against.

What equations of this kind have in common is that they are "nonlinear": the unknowns appear inside sin and cos, so they cannot be solved in one pass of elimination like a system of linear equations. The idea of numerical methods is iteration: start from a guess and correct it again and again until the equation is satisfied. Section 7.2 advanced time step by step; here we approach the answer step by step. We begin with equations in a single unknown.

### 7.3.2 Equations in one unknown: bisection

**Example 7.3.1** A link (the same as in Section 7.1: m = 2 kg, $l_c$ = 0.25 m) is supported by a torsion spring; θ is measured from the horizontal, positive upwards. The torsion spring has stiffness $k_s$ = 10 N·m/rad and exerts no torque at θ = 60°; the gravity torque is $m\mathfrak g\,l_c\cos\theta$, acting downwards. Find the equilibrium angle of the link.

At equilibrium the spring torque balances the gravity torque:

$$
f(\theta) = k_s\Big(\frac{\pi}{3} - \theta\Big) - m\mathfrak g\,l_c\cos\theta = 0 . \tag{7.3.2}
$$

Here $m\mathfrak g\,l_c$ = {{ex7_3.mgl:.3f}} N·m. θ appears both inside the cos and outside it; an equation like this is called transcendental, and in general it has no closed-form solution.

The most naive approach is trial. f(0) = {{ex7_3.f0:.3f}} N·m > 0 and f(π/3) = −{{ex7_3.mgl:.3f}} × 0.5 < 0. f is continuous and goes from positive to negative, so it must pass through zero in between (this is the intermediate value theorem for continuous functions). Take the midpoint π/6 of the interval, look at the sign of f there, and keep the half of the interval whose ends still have opposite signs; take its midpoint, keep half again, and so on. Each step halves the length of the interval. This is called the **bisection method**.

The advantage of bisection is that it is completely reliable: as long as the two ends of the starting interval have opposite signs, it is sure to converge. Its drawback is that it is slow: each step only halves the error, and shrinking an interval of length π/3 to $10^{-10}$ takes $\lceil\log_2(\frac{\pi/3}{10^{-10}})\rceil$ = {{ex7_3.n_bis}} steps. The equilibrium angle obtained is θ* = {{ex7_3.root:.10f}} rad, that is, {{ex7_3.root_deg:.4f}}°.

### 7.3.3 Newton's method

Bisection uses only the sign of f and throws away its size and how it is changing. If we know the value and the derivative of f at $\theta_k$, we can approximate f by its tangent:

$$
f(\theta) \approx f(\theta_k) + f'(\theta_k)\,(\theta - \theta_k) .
$$

Here $f'$ denotes the derivative of f with respect to θ (as distinct from the time derivative $\dot{\ }$). Setting the right-hand side to zero and solving for the point where the tangent meets the horizontal axis gives the next guess:

$$
\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k - \frac{f(\theta_k)}{f'(\theta_k)} . \tag{7.3.3}
$$

This is **Newton's method** (Figure 7.3.1(a)). Its geometric meaning is clear: draw the tangent at the current point and follow it down to zero. If f were a straight line, one step would get there; when f is curved, the tangent departs from the curve, and a few more iterations are needed.

**Example 7.3.2** Solve Eq. (7.3.2) by Newton's method, starting from $\theta_0$ = 0.

$f'(\theta) = -k_s + m\mathfrak g\,l_c\sin\theta$. In the first step, $f(0)$ = {{ex7_3.f0:.4f}}, $f'(0)$ = {{ex7_3.df0:.0f}}, and $\theta_1 = 0 - {{ex7_3.f0:.4f}}/({{ex7_3.df0:.0f}})$ = {{ex7_3.nt1:.10f}}. Continuing the iteration gives the results in the table below.

**Theorem 7.3.1 (Quadratic convergence of Newton's method)** Let θ* be a root of f, and on an interval containing θ* and $\theta_k$ let $|f''| \le M$ and $|f'| \ge m > 0$. Then the error $e_k = \theta_k - \theta^*$ of Newton's method satisfies

$$
|e_{k+1}| \le \frac{M}{2m}\,e_k^2 . \tag{7.3.4}
$$

*Proof*　By Eq. (7.3.3) and $f(\theta^*) = 0$,

$$
e_{k+1} = \theta_k - \theta^* - \frac{f(\theta_k)}{f'(\theta_k)} = \frac{f(\theta^*) - f(\theta_k) - f'(\theta_k)(\theta^* - \theta_k)}{f'(\theta_k)} .
$$

The numerator is exactly the error, at θ*, of the first-order Taylor approximation of f at $\theta_k$. By Theorem 7.1.1 (which holds equally for h < 0: just replace s by −s in the argument), the absolute value of the numerator is at most $\frac M2 e_k^2$; the absolute value of the denominator is at least m.　∎

When the error is proportional to the square of the previous error, this is called **quadratic convergence**. In general, if $|e_{k+1}| \le C|e_k|^q$, q is called the **order of convergence** of the iteration: the error of bisection halves at each step, q = 1, which is called linear convergence; for Newton's method q = 2. Eq. (7.3.4) also gives a condition for convergence: as long as $\frac{M}{2m}|e_0| < 1$, the error gets smaller at every step, and it shrinks faster and faster. In other words, Newton's method converges extremely fast near a root, but the starting guess must be "close enough".

One can go further and prove that $e_{k+1}/e_k^2$ tends to $f''(\theta^*)/(2f'(\theta^*))$. For Example 7.3.2 the absolute value of this quantity is {{ex7_3.C:.4f}}, while the measured ratios at steps 2 and 3 of the table are {{ex7_3.r1:.4f}} and {{ex7_3.r2:.4f}}, in agreement.

**History.** In his 1669 manuscript *De analysi per aequationes numero terminorum infinitas* (On analysis by equations with an infinite number of terms), Newton solved the cubic equation $x^3 - 2x - 5 = 0$ by successive corrections, but he worked by successive substitutions in the polynomial and did not use the language of derivatives. The English mathematician Joseph Raphson gave, in 1690, an iterative form closer to Eq. (7.3.3), which is why the method is also called the Newton–Raphson method. The form used today, written with derivatives (fluxions) and extended to systems of two equations in two unknowns, appears in a 1740 work by Thomas Simpson. The Soviet mathematician Leonid Kantorovich proved a convergence theorem for general systems of equations in 1948, giving a quantitative condition for "a close enough starting guess".

**Square roots by Newton's method.** Finding $\sqrt a$ means solving $x^2 - a = 0$. With $f'(x) = 2x$, Eq. (7.3.3) gives

$$
x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{x_k^2 - a}{2x_k} = \frac12\Big(x_k + \frac{a}{x_k}\Big) . \tag{7.3.5}
$$

This formula has a simple meaning: if $x_k$ is too large, $a/x_k$ is too small, and their average is closer to $\sqrt a$. It is already found in the *Metrica* of the Greek mathematician Heron in the 1st century AD, and historians believe that the Babylonians already used a similar method.

**Example 7.3.3** Use Eq. (7.3.5) to find √2, starting from $x_0$ = 1.

The successive values are 1.5, {{ex7_3.he2:.7f}}, {{ex7_3.he3:.10f}}, {{ex7_3.he4:.13f}}. The errors are $ {{ex7_3.hee1:sci1}} $, $ {{ex7_3.hee2:sci1}} $, $ {{ex7_3.hee3:sci1}} $, $ {{ex7_3.hee4:sci1}} $; again the number of correct digits doubles at each step. The square-root functions of calculators and computers use exactly this iteration internally (together with a good starting guess).

**Newton's method can also fail.** When the starting guess is too far from the root, the tangent may send the iteration even farther away. Take f(x) = arctan x, whose only root is 0. Starting from 1.3, the iterates are {{ex7_3.at13_1:.4f}}, {{ex7_3.at13_2:.4f}}, …, converging to 0 with alternating signs; starting from 1.5, they are {{ex7_3.at15_1:.4f}}, {{ex7_3.at15_2:.4f}}, {{ex7_3.at15_3:.4f}}, …, diverging with alternating signs. Between the two lies a critical value {{ex7_3.x_crit:.4f}}: starting from it, one step lands exactly on its negative, and from then on the iteration jumps back and forth between the two points. The reason is that arctan x is very flat far from the origin, so the slope of the tangent is small and the tangent meets the horizontal axis a long way off. Whenever f′ comes close to zero during the iteration, Newton's method runs into this kind of trouble.

### 7.3.4 Newton's method for systems of equations

Back to the 2R arm. Now there are two unknowns, $\theta = (\theta_1, \theta_2)$, and two equations,

$$
F(\theta) = p(\theta) - p_d = 0 . \tag{7.3.6}
$$

In one unknown, Newton's method approximates f by its tangent; in several unknowns, it approximates F by the linearization of Section 7.1.5: near $\theta^{(k)}$,

$$
F(\theta^{(k)} + \Delta\theta) \approx F(\theta^{(k)}) + J(\theta^{(k)})\,\Delta\theta , \qquad J = \frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta} = \frac{\partial p}{\partial\theta} . \tag{7.3.7}
$$

J is the 2×2 Jacobian matrix; column j is the rate of change of the position of the end-effector when only joint j turns. Setting the right-hand side to zero and solving the linear system $J\,\Delta\theta = -F$ gives

$$
\theta^{(k+1)} = \theta^{(k)} - J(\theta^{(k)})^{-1}\,F(\theta^{(k)}) . \tag{7.3.8}
$$

This is Newton's method for systems of equations. In actual computation the inverse matrix is not formed; instead a linear system is solved (Chapter 2). Eq. (7.3.8) has a direct physical meaning: $F(\theta^{(k)})$ is the deviation of the current position of the end-effector from the target, and $J^{-1}F$ answers "how much must each joint turn to move the end-effector back by this amount". If the position of the end-effector were linear in the joint angles, one step would get there; it is not, so we iterate.

Taking partial derivatives of Eq. (7.3.1) term by term:

$$
J(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} -l_1\sin\theta_1 - l_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) & -l_2\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2) \\ l_1\cos\theta_1 + l_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) & l_2\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) \end{pmatrix} . \tag{7.3.9}
$$

The second column contains only $l_2$: when joint 2 turns, only the forearm moves. Expanding directly gives

$$
\det J = l_1 l_2 \sin\theta_2 . \tag{7.3.10}
$$

Program 7.3.1 checks Eq. (7.3.9) at θ = (0.4, 1.1) rad by numerical differentiation, and verifies that det J = {{ex7_3.detJ:.6f}} m² equals $l_1l_2\sin 1.1$.

The iterates are $\theta^{(1)} = {{ex7_3.thA1}}$, $\theta^{(2)} = {{ex7_3.thA2}}$, $\theta^{(3)} = {{ex7_3.thA3}}$, … (in rad; the superscript (k) denotes the k-th iteration, to avoid confusion with the joint number). {{ex7_3.rA0:.4f}},\quad {{ex7_3.rA1:.4f}},\quad {{ex7_3.rA2:.5f}},\quad {{ex7_3.rA3:sci2}},\quad {{ex7_3.rA4:sci2}},\quad {{ex7_3.rA5:sci1}}\ \mathrm m ,

After iteration {{ex7_3.nA}} the result reaches floating-point precision: $\theta^* = {{ex7_3.solA}}$ rad, that is, $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ = {{ex7_3.solA_deg}}. In the last few steps the exponent of the residual roughly doubles, just as in one unknown: this is quadratic convergence.

### 7.3.5 Starting guesses, singularities and out-of-reach targets

Newton's method for systems inherits all the problems of the one-unknown case, and adds some new ones.

**The starting guess decides which solution is reached.** A nonlinear equation may have several solutions, and which one Newton's method converges to depends on the starting guess. Figure 7.3.3(a) runs Newton's method from each of {{ex7_3.grid_n}} × {{ex7_3.grid_n}} starting guesses on [−π, π]² and colours each by the outcome: about {{ex7_3.frac_up:.0f}}% of the starting guesses converge to the first solution, about {{ex7_3.frac_down:.0f}}% to the second, and the remaining {{ex7_3.frac_fail:.0f}}% or so do not converge within 40 iterations. The set of starting guesses that converge to a given solution is called the **basin of attraction** of that solution. Near the solutions the basins are large solid patches, but farther away they break up into interleaved stripes: a slightly different starting guess gives a completely different result.

Most of the starting guesses that fail do not diverge to infinity; instead they fall into a cycle, jumping back and forth. For example, starting from {{ex7_3.cyc0}}, the iteration soon settles into alternating between two states, with the end-effector staying about {{ex7_3.cyc_res:.2f}} m from the target; further iterations bring no improvement.

**Line search.** A simple and effective remedy rests on the observation that the direction of the correction Δθ given by Newton's method is usually right; the step may just be too long. So first try a full step; if the end-effector error does not decrease, halve the step and try again, until the error decreases:

$$
\theta^{(k+1)} = \theta^{(k)} + \alpha\,\Delta\theta , \qquad \big\|F(\theta^{(k+1)})\big\| < \big\|F(\theta^{(k)})\big\| , \tag{7.3.11}
$$

where α is tried as 1, 1/2, 1/4, … and the first value that satisfies the inequality is taken. This is called backtracking **line search**. Near a solution α = 1 is always accepted, so quadratic convergence is unaffected; far from a solution it stops the iteration from jumping about wildly. Figure 7.3.3(b) shows the result with line search: the share of starting guesses that fail drops from about {{ex7_3.frac_fail:.0f}}% to {{ex7_3.frac_fail_ls:.2f}}%, the {{ex7_3.n_sing}} that remain are exactly the starting guesses with θ₂ = 0 or ±π, and the median number of iterations is still {{ex7_3.med_iter_ls:.0f}}.

**Singularities.** By Eq. (7.3.10), det J = 0 when θ₂ = 0 or π: the Jacobian matrix is not invertible, and Eq. (7.3.8) cannot be evaluated. Geometrically, the upper arm and the forearm then lie on one straight line (fully stretched or fully folded), and the velocity of the end-effector along that line is zero: however the two joints turn, the end-effector cannot move along the direction of the arm. Such a configuration is called a singular configuration; Chapter 16 discusses its physical meaning in detail. Newton's method cannot take a single step at a singular configuration, and near one it produces enormous corrections. Practical programs therefore never start from a stretched arm, and they check det J during the iteration.

**Out-of-reach targets.** If the target lies outside the workspace, for example at (0.9, 0.3) m, its distance from the base, {{ex7_3.dist_far:.4f}} m, exceeds the total length of the arm, {{ex7_3.reach:.3f}} m, and the equation has no solution at all. Newton's method then jumps about the workspace: starting from (0.2, 0.5) rad and iterating 40 times, the smallest residual is {{ex7_3.resF_min:.4f}} m, but the last one is {{ex7_3.resF_last:.3f}} m. The sensible requirement is to settle for the next best thing: find the configuration closest to the target. One way is to replace $J^{-1}$ in Eq. (7.3.8) by

$$
\Delta\theta = -J^{\mathsf T}\big(JJ^{\mathsf T} + \lambda^2 I\big)^{-1} F , \tag{7.3.12}
$$

where λ is a positive number called the damping factor, with the same unit as length. When λ = 0 this is just $-J^{-1}F$; when λ > 0, $JJ^{\mathsf T} + \lambda^2 I$ is always invertible, so the step can be computed even at a singularity, and the correction never becomes too large. This is called **damped least squares**. It comes from the method of Kenneth Levenberg (1944) and Donald Marquardt (1963) for least-squares problems, and was applied to the inverse kinematics of robot arms in 1986, independently, by Charles Wampler and by Yoshihiko Nakamura and Hideo Hanafusa. With λ = {{ex7_3.lam:.1f}} m and the same starting guess, the iteration converges to θ = {{ex7_3.th_dls_deg}}: the arm is stretched out and points at the target, and the residual {{ex7_3.res_dls:.4f}} m is exactly the distance from the target to the base minus the total length of the arm. Why this is so, and how to choose λ, are discussed in Chapter 8 (nonlinear least squares) and Chapter 15 (numerical inverse kinematics).

Newton's method will appear many more times in this book: Chapter 8 uses it to find the minimum of a function, Chapter 15 extends it to the Lie group SE(3) to solve the inverse kinematics of six-axis arms, and the implicit integration methods of Section 7.4 use it to solve an equation at every step.

### 7.3.6 Everyday examples

The autofocus of a phone camera, in one of its forms, works much like Newton's method: the camera measures the sharpness of the image at the current lens position and how it changes with the lens position, estimates where the sharpest position is, moves the lens there, then measures and corrects again. If it starts too far from the sharp position, the image is so blurred that no trend can be seen, and the camera "hunts" back and forth; this is exactly the predicament of Newton's method with a poor starting guess and a derivative close to zero.

Another familiar example is taking a square root in your head. To find √10: guess 3; 10 ÷ 3 ≈ 3.33, and averaging with 3 gives {{ex7_3.s10_1:.4f}}; divide 10 by this, average again, and you get {{ex7_3.s10_2:.6f}}, while √10 = {{ex7_3.s10:.6f}}… Two steps give five correct significant digits. This is Eq. (7.3.5).

Numerical integration and Newton's method both assume that we know the function itself and its derivative. But what a robot's sensors give is often a sequence of discrete measurements: the encoder reports an angle every millisecond, while we need the angular velocity. The next section discusses how to obtain derivatives from data, as well as another kind of equation that is hard to handle in numerical integration: stiff equations.

**Engineer's notes**

- A convergence test for Newton's method should look at two things at once: whether the residual ‖F‖ is below a tolerance (for example 1 µm), and whether the correction ‖Δθ‖ is small enough. Looking only at the correction can mistake a stall near a singularity for convergence; looking only at the residual keeps the iteration running forever when the target is out of reach. Also set a maximum number of iterations (for example 20–50) as a backstop.
- When inverse kinematics is solved again and again within the control cycle, use the joint angles from the previous control cycle as the starting guess. The target moves only a very small distance in each cycle, so the starting guess is very close to the solution; usually 1–3 iterations are enough, and the iteration will not jump unexpectedly to another solution (for example, a sudden flip of the elbow).
- Whenever the Jacobian matrix can be written in closed form, write it in closed form; when it can only be computed numerically, use the central difference of Section 7.4, with a difference step h of about $10^{-6}$ (in radians or metres).
- Line search and damped least squares add almost no computation yet greatly improve reliability; practical numerical inverse-kinematics programs use at least one of them.
- Newton's method gives "a" solution, not necessarily the one you want. For an arm with several solutions, if a particular one is required (for example, elbow up), check after the iteration has finished and, if necessary, recompute from a different starting guess.
