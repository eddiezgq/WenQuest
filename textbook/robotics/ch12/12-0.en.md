title: Chapter Overview

At every moment a robot controller is answering the same question: the joints have turned to these angles, so where is the end-effector, and which way does it point? This is forward kinematics. It is the common foundation of velocity analysis, inverse kinematics, trajectory planning and dynamics, and in a robot program it is often the function called more than any other.

This chapter solves forward kinematics with the product of exponentials formula. The idea behind the method is very simple: every joint is an axis, and the motion of a joint is a turn about that axis. If we know the pose of the end-effector when all the joints are at zero, and where each axis was at that moment, then composing the motions of the joints in order gives the pose of the end-effector at any joint angles. An axis is described by six numbers, called a screw axis; the motion of one joint is expressed as a matrix exponential whose exponent is built from its screw axis; and the whole arm is the product of these exponentials, which is where the name "product of exponentials" comes from.

The chapter is organized as follows:

- Section 12.1 starts from the question "how does the end-effector move when one joint turns", introduces the home pose M and the joint screw axes, and writes down all six screw axes of the UR5e;
- Section 12.2 derives the space form of the product of exponentials formula from the idea of "turning from the last joint backwards", and then obtains the body form by a similarity transformation;
- Section 12.3 applies the formula to the UR5e, the seven-axis Panda and a SCARA with a prismatic joint, checks each against the 3D models in the WenQuest parts library, and discusses why the manufacturer's parameters and the model differ by a millimetre;
- Section 12.4 explains the product of exponentials geometrically: why the order cannot be reversed, a second "inside-out" way of reading it, and a comparison with the DH parameter method.

This chapter uses the cross product from Chapter 3, and the rotation matrix, Rodrigues' formula and the matrix exponential from Chapter 4. Readers who have not yet studied homogeneous transformations (Chapter 5) or screw theory (Chapter 6) can still read it directly: every result used here is explained where it is used, with a note on which chapter gives the details. This chapter is the backbone of kinematics for the whole book: the DH parameter method of Chapter 13 will be compared with it, the inverse kinematics of Chapters 14 and 15 solves the inverse of this problem, and the Jacobian matrix of Chapter 16 follows directly from its screw axes.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. explain what the home pose M and the joint screw axes mean, and write down the screw axes of revolute and prismatic joints from a drawing or model of the robot;
2. derive the space form of the product of exponentials formula, explain what the order of the matrices means, and use it to compute the pose of the end-effector at any joint angles;
3. obtain the body-form screw axes from the space-form ones, and say which situations each form suits;
4. write the forward kinematics of the UR5e, the Panda and a SCARA, and check them against 3D models; understand the difference between model parameters, nominal parameters and calibrated parameters;
5. explain the product of exponentials with the two readings, "outside in" and "inside out", and state the similarities and differences between the product of exponentials and the DH parameter method.
