### 12.2.1 From one joint to all the joints

The previous section answered how the end-effector moves when only one joint turns. Now let all the joints take arbitrary values $\theta_1, \theta_2, \ldots, \theta_n$ at once. What is the pose of the end-effector?

The configuration the robot finally ends up in is decided only by the angles of the joints, not by which joint turns first and which later. This gives us a freedom of choice: we may imagine the joints turning into place one after another in any order, as long as the final angles are right. We shall see below that turning from the last joint backwards makes the computation simplest.

**Step one: turn only joint n.** Starting from the home configuration, the robot first turns the outermost joint n through $\theta_n$. By the previous section, the pose of the end-effector becomes

$$
T = e^{[\mathcal S_n]\theta_n} M . \tag{12.2.1}
$$

**Step two: then turn joint n − 1.** When joint n − 1 turns, everything beyond it, including joint n, which has already turned, and the end-effector, turns as one body through $\theta_{n-1}$ about the axis of joint n − 1. The key question is: where is the axis of joint n − 1 at this moment? Step one turned only joint n, which lies outside it; joint n − 1 itself and all the links between it and the base have not moved, so its axis is still where it was in the home configuration, and its screw axis is still $\mathcal S_{n-1}$. So the turn about this axis is $e^{[\mathcal S_{n-1}]\theta_{n-1}}$. This axis is fixed in the space frame, so by Eq. (12.1.2) the turn about it premultiplies the pose obtained in step one, the same rule as "a turn about a fixed axis premultiplies" in Chapter 4:

$$
T = e^{[\mathcal S_{n-1}]\theta_{n-1}}\, e^{[\mathcal S_n]\theta_n} M . \tag{12.2.2}
$$

**And so on.** For the joint turned at each step, the links between it and the base have not yet moved, so its axis is always where it was in the home configuration, and we always use its home screw axis $\mathcal S_i$. Carrying on all the way to joint 1 gives

$$
T(\theta) = e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}\, e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2} \cdots e^{[\mathcal S_n]\theta_n}\, M . \tag{12.2.3}
$$

This is the **product of exponentials formula** (PoE), put forward by R. Brockett in 1984. Because all the screw axes are expressed in the space frame {s}, Eq. (12.2.3) is called the **space form** of the product of exponentials formula. Its entire input is the two things named in Section 12.1: the home pose M, and the screw axes of the joints in the home configuration, $\mathcal S_1, \ldots, \mathcal S_n$.

Note the order of the matrices. Read from right to left, it is the order of turning we imagined: joint n first, joint 1 last. The M on the far right is moved first by the outermost joint and then by the inner joints. If the order were written the other way round, starting from joint 1, then by the time joint 2 turned, its axis would already have been carried off by joint 1 and would no longer be $\mathcal S_2$, and using $\mathcal S_2$ directly would be wrong. Section 12.4 shows quantitatively what goes wrong, and gives another correct way of reading the formula.

**Example 12.2.1** The joint angles of the planar 3R arm (Example 12.1.1) are $\theta = (30^\circ, 45^\circ, -90^\circ)$. Find the pose of the end-effector with the product of exponentials formula.

Following Eq. (12.2.3), multiply from right to left one factor at a time, noting the position of the end-effector after each step:

1. $e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3} M$: the end-effector turns −90° about $q_3$ and its position becomes ${{ex12_2.p3}}$. The end-effector was $L_3$ to the right of $q_3$; after turning 90° clockwise it is $L_3$ directly below $q_3$.
2. Premultiply by $e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}$: the whole result of the previous step turns 45° about $q_2$, and the end-effector arrives at ${{ex12_2.p2}}$.
3. Premultiply by $e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}$: the whole turns 30° about the origin, and the end-effector arrives at ${{ex12_2.p1}}$.

The final result is

$$
T = {{ex12_2.T}} .
$$

Check it geometrically. The position of the end-effector is the sum of the three link vectors, and the orientation angle of each link is the sum of the joint angles from the base up to that link:

$$
x = L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) + L_3\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2 + \theta_3), \quad
y = L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) + L_3\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2 + \theta_3) .
$$

Substituting gives $x = {{ex12_2.x:.4f}}$ m and $y = {{ex12_2.y:.4f}}$ m, and the orientation angle of the end-effector is $\theta_1 + \theta_2 + \theta_3 = {{ex12_2.phi:.0f}}^\circ$. This agrees with the fourth column of T; the 2×2 block in the top left corner of T is a planar rotation by −15°, for example $r_{11} = \cos(-15^\circ) = {{ex12_2.c15:.4f}}$.

For the planar 3R arm the geometric method is also convenient. But for a six-axis arm in space the trigonometric functions nest layer upon layer, and the geometric method soon becomes impossible to write by hand, whereas the product of exponentials formula is written exactly as in the planar case: write down the six screw axes and M, and multiply in turn.

### 12.2.2 The body form

The screw axes in Eq. (12.2.3) are all expressed in the space frame. Look at it from another angle: if we stood on the end-effector and looked at the joint axes, what formula would we get?

First a simple fact. Let A be a square matrix and P an invertible matrix of the same size. Then

$$
P^{-1} e^{A} P = e^{P^{-1} A P} . \tag{12.2.4}
$$

The proof needs only the series definition of the matrix exponential: $P^{-1}A^kP = (P^{-1}AP)^k$, because each adjacent pair $P P^{-1}$ cancels; substituting term by term into the series gives the result. Eq. (12.2.4) is equivalent to $e^{A} P = P e^{P^{-1} A P}$; that is, P can be "moved" from the right of the exponential to its left, at the cost of replacing the matrix in the exponent by $P^{-1} A P$.

Take P = M and apply this to Eq. (12.2.3): first move M past $e^{[\mathcal S_n]\theta_n}$, then past $e^{[\mathcal S_{n-1}]\theta_{n-1}}$, all the way to the far left. Writing $[\mathcal B_i] = M^{-1} [\mathcal S_i] M$, we obtain

$$
T(\theta) = M\, e^{[\mathcal B_1]\theta_1}\, e^{[\mathcal B_2]\theta_2} \cdots e^{[\mathcal B_n]\theta_n} . \tag{12.2.5}
$$

This is the **body form** of the product of exponentials formula.

$\mathcal B_i$ has a clear geometric meaning: it is the screw axis of joint i expressed in the end-effector frame {b} of the home configuration. That is, the angular-velocity part of $\mathcal B_i$ consists of the components of the axis direction in {b}, and the linear-velocity part is $-\hat\omega \times q$ computed from a point on the axis measured in {b}.

How is $M^{-1}[\mathcal S_i]M$ computed? More generally, for any pose $T = (R, p)$ and any screw $\mathcal S$, multiplying out the 4×4 matrices directly (Exercise 12.2.3) shows that $T[\mathcal S]T^{-1}$ is again the 4×4 form of some screw, and that this screw equals a 6×6 matrix times $\mathcal S$. Define

$$
[\mathrm{Ad}_T] = \begin{pmatrix} R & 0 \\ [p]\,R & R \end{pmatrix} , \tag{12.2.6}
$$

called the **adjoint matrix** of T. Then

$$
T\,[\mathcal S]\,T^{-1} = \big[\,[\mathrm{Ad}_T]\,\mathcal S\,\big] . \tag{12.2.7}
$$

Taking $T = M^{-1}$ (so that $T^{-1} = M$) gives

$$
\mathcal B_i = [\mathrm{Ad}_{M^{-1}}]\, \mathcal S_i , \qquad [\mathrm{Ad}_{M^{-1}}] = \begin{pmatrix} R^{\mathsf T} & 0 \\ -R^{\mathsf T}[p] & R^{\mathsf T} \end{pmatrix} , \tag{12.2.8}
$$

where R and p are the rotation part and the translation part of M. The reason $M^{-1}$ appears rather than M is that in $[\mathcal B_i] = M^{-1}[\mathcal S_i]M$ the inverse of M stands on the left.

The adjoint matrix changes a screw from one frame to another. It is discussed systematically in Chapter 6, and it is used again and again when the Jacobian matrix is found in Chapter 16. Here we only need to know how to use Eq. (12.2.8).

**Example 12.2.2** Find the body-form screw axes of the planar 3R arm, and recompute Example 12.2.1 with Eq. (12.2.5).

In the home configuration {b} has the same orientation as {s}, with its origin at $x = L_1 + L_2 + L_3$. Seen in {b}, the three joint centres are at $x = -(L_1 + L_2 + L_3)$, $-(L_2 + L_3)$ and $-L_3$, and the axis is still $(0, 0, 1)$. By $v = -\hat\omega \times q$:

$$
\mathcal B_1 = {{ex12_2.B1}}, \quad \mathcal B_2 = {{ex12_2.B2}}, \quad \mathcal B_3 = {{ex12_2.B3}} .
$$

Computing directly with Eq. (12.2.8) gives the same result. Substituting into Eq. (12.2.5) gives a T exactly the same as in Example 12.2.1 (Program 12.2.1 checks it to $10^{-12}$).

The body form can also be understood step by step like the space form, only in the opposite direction: read from left to right, turning joint 1 first. When joint 1 turns, the end-effector carries {b} about the axis of joint 1; then joint 2 turns, and at this point the axis of joint 2, seen in {b}, has not moved and is still $\mathcal B_2$. So each step is a turn "about an axis fixed in the body frame", and postmultiplies. This is exactly the rule of Chapter 4, "a turn about the body's own axis postmultiplies", extended to poses.

### 12.2.3 Choosing between the two forms

The two forms give exactly the same pose of the end-effector, with the same amount of computation. Which one to choose depends on where the data come from and what will be changed later.

| Situation | Space form $\mathcal S_i$ | Body form $\mathcal B_i$ |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point of the description | The joint axes seen from the base, the same way a drawing of the robot is labelled | The joint axes seen from the end-effector |
| Fitting a longer tool | Only M changes; the $\mathcal S_i$ stay the same | Both M and the $\mathcal B_i$ change |
| Mounting the robot on another base ({s} fixed to the workbench) | Both M and the $\mathcal S_i$ change | Only M changes |
| Later uses | Space Jacobian (Chapter 16) | Body Jacobian; force control at the end-effector |

In industry tools are changed often, so the space form is used more; when motion is planned in the end-effector frame or force control is done there, the body form is more convenient.

**Engineer's notes**

- In a program, the product of exponentials formula is just a loop: `T = I; for i in 1..n: T = T @ exp6(S[i], θ[i]); T = T @ M`. The function `exp6` computes Eq. (12.1.5): Rodrigues' formula for a revolute joint, a plain translation for a prismatic joint.
- The table of screw axes and M are the robot's "identity card"; they should be filed together with the robot type, the definition of its home configuration and the tool type. The controller, the simulation software and the vision calibration must all use the same copy.
- The body-form $\mathcal B_i$ need not be worked out by hand; converting them from the $\mathcal S_i$ with Eq. (12.2.8) is the least error-prone way.
