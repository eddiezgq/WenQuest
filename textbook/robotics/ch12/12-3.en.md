### 12.3.1 Three typical structures

The first two sections used the planar 3R arm to show where the product of exponentials formula comes from. This section applies it to three typical industrial robot arms:

- **UR5e.** Six revolute joints; the most common structure among collaborative robots;
- **Panda.** Seven revolute joints, one more than the six needed for tasks in space; it is a redundant arm;
- **SCARA.** Two joints turning about vertical axes, one vertical prismatic joint and a revolute wrist joint; the workhorse of assembly and sorting lines.

For each of them the procedure is exactly the same: write down the home pose M and the screw axes of the joints, and substitute into Eq. (12.2.3). All three models come from the WenQuest parts library, and they are the ones shown in the 3D labs of this chapter. We shall use programs to check the results of the product of exponentials against the models one by one.

### 12.3.2 The UR5e: forward kinematics and a check against the model

Section 12.1 has already written down the screw axes of the UR5e (Table 12.1.1) and the home pose M. Now we take any set of joint angles and compute the pose of the end-effector.

**Example 12.3.1** The joint angles of the UR5e are $\theta = (30^\circ, -60^\circ, 90^\circ, -120^\circ, -90^\circ, 45^\circ)$. Find the pose of the end-effector.

Substituting the six screw axes of Table 12.1.1 and M into Eq. (12.2.3), and computing the six matrix exponentials in turn and multiplying them, gives

$$
T = {{ex12_3.T}} .
$$

The centre of the flange is at $({{ex12_3.px:.4f}},\ {{ex12_3.py:.4f}},\ {{ex12_3.pz:.4f}})$ m. The third column of T is the direction of the z axis of the end-effector in {s}; its z component is 0, which shows that the z axis of the end-effector is horizontal at this moment. Recomputing with the body form (12.2.5) gives the same result.

**Checking against the parts-library model.** The UR5e in the 3D labs does not use the product of exponentials formula. The 3D engine starts from the base and multiplies link by link, using the relative positions of the links in the model file, to find where each link is in space. This is an algorithm completely independent of the product of exponentials formula, and the two also have different inputs: the product of exponentials uses Table 12.1.1, which we wrote from the dimensions, while the 3D engine uses the link matrices in the model file.

Program 12.3.1 generates {{ex12_3.N}} random sets of joint angles and computes the pose of the end-effector for each set with both algorithms. The largest difference in the position of the end-effector is ${{ex12_3.err_p:sci2}}$ m, and the largest difference in any entry of the orientation matrix is ${{ex12_3.err_R:sci2}}$. These differences are of the order of computer rounding error, far below 1 micrometre ($10^{-6}$ m). In other words, Table 12.1.1 together with Eq. (12.2.3) matches the arm seen in the labs exactly.

### 12.3.3 The manufacturer's parameters and the model: where the millimetre discrepancy comes from

Universal Robots publishes standard DH parameters for the UR5e (the DH parameter method is in Chapter 13). The six length parameters are

$$
d_1 = {{ex12_3.d1}},\quad a_2 = {{ex12_3.a2}},\quad a_3 = {{ex12_3.a3}},\quad d_4 = {{ex12_3.d4}},\quad d_5 = {{ex12_3.d5}},\quad d_6 = {{ex12_3.d6}} \quad (\mathrm{m}) .
$$

(In the manufacturer's table $a_2$ and $a_3$ carry a minus sign, meaning the negative direction of the x axis; only the lengths are listed here.) Computing with these parameters, the centre of the flange in the home configuration is at ${{ex12_3.dh0}}$ m, very close to, but not the same as, the $({{ex12_1.ur_x}},\ {{ex12_1.ur_y}},\ {{ex12_1.ur_z}})$ m of Section 12.1.

This shows one thing first of all: the base frame used for the manufacturer's DH parameters is the same as the {s} of this chapter; otherwise the two would be far apart (for example with x and y of opposite sign). The difference is only of the order of millimetres. Over {{ex12_3.N}} random sets of joint angles, the largest difference between the two end-effector positions is {{ex12_3.gap_max:.2f}} mm and the mean is {{ex12_3.gap_mean:.2f}} mm; the distribution is shown in Figure 12.3.1.

Comparing item by item, the source of the difference is plain to see: the dimensions of the parts-library model are given to the millimetre, while the manufacturer's parameters are given to 0.1 mm. Shoulder height 0.163 against {{ex12_3.d1}}, forearm 0.392 against {{ex12_3.a3}}, the algebraic sum of the three sideways offsets $W_1 - W_2 + W_3 = 0.134$ against $d_4 = {{ex12_3.d4}}$, and the 0.1 and 0.1 of the wrist against {{ex12_3.d5}} and {{ex12_3.d6}}. Each item differs by only a few tenths of a millimetre, and together they add up to one or two millimetres.

These one or two millimetres illustrate an important engineering point: **the computed end-effector position is only as accurate as the parameters.**

- For teaching, simulation and 3D display, a model accurate to the millimetre is enough.
- The repeatability of the UR5e is ±0.03 mm; that is, for the same set of joint angles the robot returns every time to almost the same position. But "returning to the same position" is not the same as "reaching the computed position". The latter is called absolute accuracy, and it is limited by errors in the parameters.
- Even relative to the manufacturer's nominal parameters, manufacturing tolerances make each individual robot's actual parameters differ by a few tenths of a millimetre. Where high absolute accuracy is required (for example machining directly to CAD coordinates, or offline programming), this particular robot must first be kinematically calibrated to measure its own parameters. The UR controller stores each robot's factory-calibrated parameters.

One more difference deserves attention. In the manufacturer's DH parameters, the z axis of the end-effector frame points outwards along the normal of the flange; but the {b} of this chapter is taken from the last link of the model, and the normal of the flange is its y axis. Program 12.3.1 verifies that the two orientations differ by one fixed rotation at every set of joint angles: turning the manufacturer's end-effector frame +90° about its own x axis gives the {b} of this chapter, that is, $R_{\mathrm{DH},b} = \mathrm{Rot}(\hat x, 90^\circ)$. So when two sets of parameters are compared, first make sure they refer to the same frame.

### 12.3.4 The Panda: seven joints

The Franka Emika Panda has seven revolute joints. The axes of the joints and the points on them in the home configuration are read from the model and listed in Table 12.3.1. In the home configuration the arm of the Panda points straight up; the end flange is about 0.93 m directly above the base and reaches 0.088 m forwards, with the flange facing down. For {b} we take the gripper frame of the model: the origin at the centre of the flange, the z axis outwards along the normal of the flange, and the x and y axes turned 45° about z relative to the flange, which is why $\cos 45^\circ$ appears in M. Note that this home configuration is only a mathematical convention: the allowed range of joint 4 of the Panda is about −176° to −4°, so the real robot cannot reach $\theta_4 = 0$. The forward kinematics formula does not require the home configuration to be reachable; it is only the starting point of the computation.

$$
M = {{ex12_3.Mp}} .
$$

$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}

{{ex12_3.pa_rows}}
\end{array}
$$

The screw axis of each joint is computed from this table by Eq. (12.1.3). Substituting into Eq. (12.2.3) and checking against the model at {{ex12_3.N}} random sets of joint angles, the largest difference in the position of the end-effector is ${{ex12_3.err_pa:sci2}}$ m. Seven joints are no different from six: the formula simply has one more exponential.

What seven joints bring is redundancy. The pose of the end-effector has 6 degrees of freedom, but the Panda has 7 joints, so for the same end-effector pose there are in general infinitely many sets of joint angles. Table 12.3.1 shows the simplest example: whenever $\theta_2 = 0$, the axes of joint 1 and joint 3 are both vertical and both pass through the origin, so they are the same line. Then turning joint 1 through some angle and joint 3 through the same angle in the opposite direction leaves everything beyond joint 3 completely still, and the end-effector does not move. Program 12.3.1 verifies this for a configuration with joints 4 and 6 bent: the difference in end-effector pose is ${{ex12_3.self_motion:sci1}}$, that is, only rounding error. This motion, however, merely spins links 1 and 2 about their own vertical axis; the visible shape of the arm does not change, and it is of little use. The truly useful kind of "end-effector still, arm moving" motion that redundancy offers is a different one: the elbow swings about the shoulder–wrist line (a self-motion), to avoid obstacles or keep away from joint limits. Chapter 17 discusses it in detail.

### 12.3.5 The SCARA: with a prismatic joint

The SCARA robot in the parts library has four joints ({b} is taken at the origin of the tool link, that is, the centre of the tool mounting face; the tool point that the parts library defines separately for this SCARA is 0.05 m below it). Joints 1 and 2 turn about vertical axes and move the end-effector in a horizontal plane; joint 3 is a vertical prismatic joint (a lead screw), positive downwards; joint 4 turns about a vertical axis and rotates the tool in the horizontal plane. The robot's screw axes are listed in Table 12.3.2; the third joint is written as $(0, v)$ by Eq. (12.1.4).

::: Table 12.3.2
caption: Joint screws of the SCARA (home configuration; units m)
:::

$$
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
i & \text{type} & \hat\omega_i\ \text{or}\ v_i & q_i & \mathcal S_i \\ \hline
{{ex12_3.sc_rows}}
\end{array}
$$

In the row of the prismatic joint, the column "$\hat\omega_i$ or $v_i$" holds the direction of sliding v, and "$q_i$" only marks where the lead screw is; it does not enter the screw axis. In the home configuration

$$
M = {{ex12_3.Ms}} .
$$

**Example 12.3.2** The joint variables of the SCARA are $\theta = (40^\circ, -70^\circ, 0.12\ \mathrm{m}, 90^\circ)$. Find the pose of the end-effector.

Substituting into Eq. (12.2.3) gives

$$
T = {{ex12_3.Tsc}} .
$$

This result is easy to check intuitively. Height of the end-effector: 0.242 m in the home configuration; the lead screw moves down 0.12 m, giving {{ex12_3.scz:.3f}} m. Horizontal position: the two horizontal links, 0.35 m and 0.25 m long, form a planar 2R arm, and the end-effector is at $(0.35\cos 40^\circ + 0.25\cos(-30^\circ),\ 0.35\sin 40^\circ + 0.25\sin(-30^\circ)) = ({{ex12_3.scx:.4f}},\ {{ex12_3.scy:.4f}})$ m. Tool heading: all three revolute joints turn about vertical axes, so their angles add, $40^\circ - 70^\circ + 90^\circ = 60^\circ$, and the top left corner of T is exactly a planar rotation by 60°. Checked against the model, the largest difference in the position of the end-effector is ${{ex12_3.err_sc:sci2}}$ m.

**Engineer's notes**

- When you get a new robot, check the home configuration first: with all joints reading zero, the measured (or modelled) position of the end-effector should equal the fourth column of M. Then turn each joint on its own and check the direction of its axis. These two steps catch the great majority of mistakes.
- The manufacturer's DH parameters, the CAD model and simulation models (URDF, MJCF) often differ at the millimetre level. For simulation, use the same set of parameters as the 3D display; for precision work, use the calibrated parameters.
- The forward kinematics of a redundant arm is no harder than that of a six-axis arm; what is harder is the inverse kinematics (Chapters 14, 15 and 17).
- The variable of a prismatic joint is in metres, that of a revolute joint in radians. When a program mixes the two kinds of joint, take extra care with units.
