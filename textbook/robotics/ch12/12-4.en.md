### 12.4.1 Why the factors cannot be reordered

The product of exponentials formula (12.2.3) is a product of a string of matrices. Chapter 4 showed that rotations do not in general commute, and poses even less so. What happens if the exponentials in Eq. (12.2.3) are written in the opposite order?

**Example 12.4.1** For the planar 3R arm and joint angles of Example 12.2.1, write the product of exponentials in reverse and compute $e^{[\mathcal S_3]\theta_3} e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2} e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1} M$.

The computed end-effector is at $({{ex12_2.wx:.4f}},\ {{ex12_2.wy:.4f}})$ m, {{ex12_2.wdist:.3f}} m away from the correct result. More telling still, its distance from the origin is {{ex12_2.wnorm:.3f}} m, whereas the three links fully stretched out reach only {{ex12_2.reach:.3f}} m. Writing the order in reverse gives a position the robot cannot reach at all.

What went wrong? With the order reversed, reading from right to left means turning joint 1 first, then joint 2, and joint 3 last. When joint 1 turns first, the whole arm turns about the origin, and the axis of joint 2 is carried along to a new place. Yet the second factor $e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2}$ still turns about the axis of joint 2 in the home configuration, where there is no longer any joint. It is like turning the base of a desk lamp to face another way and then still reaching for the hinge of the lamp arm where it used to be: you are turning air.

### 12.4.2 The second reading: from the inside out

Can we, then, start turning from joint 1, that is, read the product from the inside out (from the base outwards)? Yes, provided that each time a joint turns we update the axes of all the joints beyond it to their new places.

**Theorem 12.4.1** Let $\mathcal S_1$ and $\mathcal S_2$ be two screw axes. Write $E_1 = e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}$ and $\mathcal S_2' = [\mathrm{Ad}_{E_1}]\,\mathcal S_2$. Then

$$
e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1}\, e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2} = e^{[\mathcal S_2']\theta_2}\, e^{[\mathcal S_1]\theta_1} .
$$

*Proof*　Taking $P = E_1^{-1}$ in Eq. (12.2.4) gives $E_1 e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2} E_1^{-1} = e^{E_1 [\mathcal S_2] E_1^{-1}\,\theta_2}$. By Eq. (12.2.7), $E_1 [\mathcal S_2] E_1^{-1} = [\,[\mathrm{Ad}_{E_1}]\mathcal S_2\,] = [\mathcal S_2']$. Postmultiplying both sides by $E_1$ gives the result.　∎

The $\mathcal S_2'$ of the theorem has a clear geometric meaning: it is the new position of the axis of joint 2 after joint 1 has turned through $\theta_1$. For a revolute joint, $[\mathrm{Ad}_{E_1}]$ turns the direction of the axis $\hat\omega_2$ into $R_1\hat\omega_2$, and carries the point $q_2$ on the axis to $E_1 q_2$. So what the theorem says is:

> Turning first about the original axis of joint 2 and then about joint 1 is the same as turning first about joint 1 and then about the "new axis" of joint 2.

This is exactly our intuition about a real robot: after the base turns, the shoulder axis has already moved with it by the time the shoulder joint turns.

For the planar 3R arm, after joint 1 has turned through 30°, the centre of joint 2 moves from $(L_1, 0)$ to ${{ex12_4.q2n}}$, and the new screw axis is $\mathcal S_2' = {{ex12_4.S2n}}$. Applying the theorem joint after joint gives the second reading of the product of exponentials:

1. turn through $\theta_1$ about the axis of joint 1, and move the axes of joints 2, …, n to their new places with $[\mathrm{Ad}_{E_1}]$;
2. turn through $\theta_2$ about the new axis of joint 2, then move the axes of joints 3, …, n to their new places;
3. and so on, up to joint n.

The two readings give the same result. Program 12.4.1 verifies this for the planar 3R arm (at the joint angles of the example) and for the UR5e (at two thousand random sets of joint angles); the largest difference is ${{ex12_4.worst:sci1}}$.

The advantage of the first reading (outside in) is that all the screw axes stay fixed where they were in the home configuration and need no updating, which is why Eq. (12.2.3) can be written so simply. The second reading (inside out) is closer to how people intuitively think about the motion of a robot, and it reappears when the Jacobian matrix is computed: Chapter 16 will show that column i of the space Jacobian is exactly the screw axis of joint i in the current configuration, that is, the $\mathcal S_i'$ after it has been moved by joints 1, …, i−1.

### 12.4.3 Comparison with the DH parameter method

Chapter 13 introduces the other standard method of forward kinematics, the DH parameter method. Here we first compare the two geometrically, to help the reader see why this book takes the product of exponentials as its backbone.

| | Product of exponentials formula | DH parameter method |
|---|---|---|
| Frames to set up | Only two, {s} and {b} | One per link, assigned by the DH conventions |
| Quantities describing a joint | Screw axis: the direction of the axis and a point on it, with an intuitive meaning | Four parameters: two distances and two angles, defined relative to adjacent frames |
| Revolute and prismatic joints | The same formula | The variables of the two kinds of joint appear in different parameters |
| Adjacent axes nearly parallel | No effect | The parameters are extremely sensitive to tiny errors; another parametrization is needed for calibration |
| Number of parameters (n joints) | 6 numbers per revolute joint (with the two constraints $\|\hat\omega\| = 1$ and $\hat\omega \cdot v = 0$), plus the 6 degrees of freedom of M; the parameters vary continuously with the axis | 4 per link, the most economical description of adjacent links; but base and tool transformations must be added, and the parameters jump discontinuously when adjacent axes become parallel |
| Connection with the Jacobian matrix and dynamics | The columns of the Jacobian matrix are the screw axes in the current configuration, $[\mathrm{Ad}]\mathcal S_i$, computed directly from the $\mathcal S_i$ | Must be derived separately |

The row "adjacent axes nearly parallel" deserves a further word. Joints 2, 3 and 4 of the UR5e are parallel to each other. In real manufacturing these axes cannot be exactly parallel; there is always a deviation of a few thousandths of a degree. In the DH description, such a tiny deviation can make some parameters jump suddenly from zero to very large values, which causes great trouble for kinematic calibration, so another parametrization is often used for calibration. The product of exponentials formula writes down the direction and position of each axis directly: if an axis is off by a little, its screw axis changes by a little, with no such jump. This is one of the reasons the product of exponentials formula is gaining more and more attention in robot calibration.

The two methods of course give the same pose of the end-effector, as long as the parameters describe the same robot. Much of the industrial literature and many controllers still give DH parameters, so Chapter 13 introduces them in detail and shows how to convert between the two kinds of parameters.

### 12.4.4 Back to the opening question

The question posed at the start of this chapter was: the controller reads six joint angles; how does it compute where the end-effector is? The answer is now clear:

1. write down in advance the home pose M and the six screw axes; they are decided by the structure of the robot and are written only once;
2. in every control cycle, substitute the joint angles read into Eq. (12.2.3), and evaluate six matrix exponentials and perform six matrix multiplications.

On an ordinary industrial controller this takes only microseconds. The whole geometric content of the product of exponentials formula is one sentence: **Every joint is a screw axis, the motion of a joint is a screw motion about that axis (a revolute joint is a screw of zero pitch, a prismatic joint a screw of infinite pitch), and the motion of the whole arm is these motions composed in order.** The screw theory of Chapter 6 will show that every rigid-body motion can be written as such a screw motion; this is the content of Chasles' theorem.

**Engineer's notes**

- To implement forward kinematics in a program, use the first reading: the table of screw axes stays fixed, and one loop multiplies everything together. The second reading suits explanation and debugging: drawing the position of each joint axis at every step is the easiest way to find an axis whose direction or position is written wrongly.
- When forward kinematics gives a wrong result, first check whether the order of the exponentials has been reversed, or M has been put on the far left. Both mistakes give a result that "looks like a matrix but means nothing at all"; but with the order reversed, the end-effector often lands outside the workspace, which gives a quick way to tell.
- In calibration, a product of exponentials parametrization avoids the parameter jumps caused by parallel axes, and many modern calibration methods are based on screw axes.
