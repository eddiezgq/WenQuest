### 12.1.1 The problem: the controller knows only the joint angles

Each joint of the UR5e contains an encoder, so the controller can read at any moment the angles through which the six joints have turned. Yet the teach pendant screen shows the position and orientation of the end-effector: how far in front of the base and how high the tool is, and which way it points. When the vision system finds a part on the conveyor, the controller must also judge how far the end-effector still is from the part. All of this requires computing the pose of the end-effector from the six angles.

Finding the pose of the end-effector from the joint variables is called **forward kinematics**. It is the most basic computation in robotics: velocity analysis (Chapter 16), inverse kinematics (Chapters 14 and 15), trajectory planning and dynamics, which come later, are all built on it.

There are two standard algorithms for forward kinematics. One is the DH parameter method, long used in industry, which fixes a frame on every link and then multiplies link by link; it is introduced in Chapter 13. The other is the **product of exponentials formula** of this chapter. It needs to know only two things:

1. where the end-effector is, and which way it points, when all the joints are at zero;
2. where the axis (or the direction of sliding) of each joint is at that moment.

There is no need to set up a frame on each link, and no "frame-assignment rules" to remember. Both things can be written down just by looking at a drawing or model of the robot. This section makes the two things clear; the next section combines them into a formula.

### 12.1.2 Preliminaries: pose and homogeneous transformation

First we recall the tools for describing the position and orientation of a rigid body; the detailed derivation is in Chapter 5.

Fix a space frame {s} on the base of the robot, and a body frame {b} on the end-effector (for example at the centre of the flange). Chapter 4 showed that the orientation of {b} relative to {s} can be described by a rotation matrix $R \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$, whose three columns are the components of the three axes of {b} in {s}. The position of the origin of {b} in {s} is written $p \in \mathbb{R}^3$. Putting the two together in one 4×4 matrix gives

$$
T_{sb} = \begin{pmatrix} R & p \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \tag{12.1.1}
$$

which is the **homogeneous transformation** matrix, or pose. The set of all such matrices is written SE(3). It has two uses. First, it describes the pose of {b} relative to {s}. Second, it can act on points: if a point has coordinates $p_b$ in {b}, appending a 1 to write it as $(p_b, 1)$ and premultiplying by $T_{sb}$ gives the coordinates of the point in {s}, $(R p_b + p,\ 1)$. Composing two pose transformations is just matrix multiplication, exactly as with the composition rule for rotation matrices in Chapter 4.

### 12.1.3 The home configuration

The **home configuration** is the configuration of the robot when all the joint variables are zero. The pose of the end-effector frame {b} relative to {s} in the home configuration is written

$$
M = T_{sb}(0) .
$$

The home configuration need not be a "nice-looking" or commonly used posture of the robot; it only has to be agreed on. But it must agree with the convention of the controller: only the configuration the robot is really in when the controller reads zero is the home configuration of the formula. For the UR5e, when all six joints are at zero the upper arm and forearm stretch out horizontally, and this is exactly its home configuration.

Figure 12.1.1 shows a three-joint arm that moves only in a plane, called a planar 3R arm. All three joints are revolute joints, with axes perpendicular to the page. For comparison with the UR5e later, we take its two link lengths equal to the lengths of the upper arm and forearm of the UR5e, $L_1 = 0.425$ m and $L_2 = 0.392$ m, with the end-effector reaching a further $L_3 = 0.1$ m. In the home configuration the three links lie in a straight line along the x axis, and the end-effector frame has the same orientation as {s}, only shifted along the x axis by $L_1 + L_2 + L_3$, so

$$
M = {{ex12_1.M3r}} .
$$

### 12.1.4 How the end-effector moves when one joint turns

Now let the robot start from the home configuration and turn only one joint i, through an angle θ, with the other joints held at zero. The part beyond this joint (all the links from joint i to the end-effector) turns like one rigid body through θ about the axis of joint i; the part between the base and joint i does not move.

This axis is a straight line in space. Describing it takes two things: the direction of the line, given by a unit vector $\hat\omega$, whose positive sense agrees with the direction of turning by the right-hand rule; and any point q on the line.

Let us write down this rotation. Suppose a point of the end-effector was at r and has gone to $r'$ after turning through θ about this axis. If the axis passed through the origin, then by Chapter 4 $r' = Rr$, where $R = e^{[\hat\omega]\theta}$ is given by Rodrigues' formula, Eq. (4.4.9). Now the axis does not pass through the origin but through the point q. We can do it in three steps: first translate the whole body by −q so that the axis passes through the origin; then turn about the axis through the origin; finally translate back. This gives

$$
r' = q + R\,(r - q) = R\,r + (I - R)\,q .
$$

This holds for every point of the end-effector. The end-effector frame moves with the end-effector: its origin is a point of the end-effector and moves by the formula above; its three axes are directions, affected only by the turn, and are each multiplied by R. If in the home configuration $M = \begin{pmatrix} R_0 & p_0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, then after the turn the orientation becomes $R R_0$ and the origin becomes $R p_0 + (I - R)\,q$. Together these are exactly a 4×4 matrix premultiplying M:

$$
T = \begin{pmatrix} R & (I - R)\,q \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} M , \qquad R = e^{[\hat\omega]\theta} . \tag{12.1.2}
$$

The 4×4 matrix on the left is determined entirely by $\hat\omega$, q and θ, and does not depend on the shape of the end-effector. One detail is worth checking here: the point q on the axis can be chosen freely, so does the result change with the choice of q? Replacing q by another point of the same axis, $q + k\hat\omega$, and using $R\hat\omega = \hat\omega$, we get $(I - R)(q + k\hat\omega) = (I - R)\,q$, so the result is unchanged. This agrees with geometric intuition: it is the same axis, whichever point we measure it from.

### 12.1.5 Joint screws: writing an axis as six numbers

Eq. (12.1.2) shows that a revolute joint is completely determined by its axis, and the axis is given by $\hat\omega$ and q. $\hat\omega$ has 3 components and so does q, but q can slide freely along the axis and is not unique. Can we use instead a quantity that does not depend on the choice of q?

Consider the cross product $\hat\omega \times q$. Replacing q by $q + k\hat\omega$ changes the cross product to $\hat\omega \times q + k\,\hat\omega \times \hat\omega = \hat\omega \times q$, which is the same. So $\hat\omega \times q$ is a property of the axis itself. Take $v = -\hat\omega \times q$ and stack $\hat\omega$ and v into a six-dimensional column vector:

$$
\mathcal{S} = \begin{pmatrix} \hat\omega \\ v \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \hat\omega \\ -\hat\omega \times q \end{pmatrix} . \tag{12.1.3}
$$

$\mathcal S$ is called the **screw axis** of this joint, or the joint screw. Why the minus sign in v? Imagine the body turning about this axis at unit angular velocity. The point of the body that happens to be at the origin at this moment has velocity $\hat\omega \times (0 - q) = -\hat\omega \times q$, which is exactly v. So the first three components of $\mathcal S$ are an angular velocity, and the last three are the linear velocity of the point at the origin. Chapter 6 discusses such six-dimensional quantities, "angular velocity first, linear velocity second", systematically, starting from the velocity of a rigid body; they are called twists. This chapter uses only the definition.

A prismatic joint does not turn; it only translates along one direction. Let the unit vector of the direction of sliding be v; the joint variable θ is then the distance moved. In this case we take

$$
\mathcal S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ v \end{pmatrix} . \tag{12.1.4}
$$

After a joint moves, how is the pose of the end-effector computed from $\mathcal S$? As in Chapter 4, where the skew-symmetric matrix $[\hat\omega]$ represented the cross product, we write $\mathcal S$ as the 4×4 matrix

$$
[\mathcal S] = \begin{pmatrix} [\hat\omega] & v \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} ,
$$

and then the transformation for a turn through θ (or a slide through θ) of the joint is the matrix exponential

$$
e^{[\mathcal S]\theta} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{[\hat\omega]\theta} & G(\theta)\,v \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad
G(\theta) = I\theta + (1 - \cos\theta)\,[\hat\omega] + (\theta - \sin\theta)\,[\hat\omega]^2 . \tag{12.1.5}
$$

Here $G(\theta)$ is only notation local to this chapter and has nothing to do with the $G_i$ that denotes inertia in dynamics. For a prismatic joint, $\hat\omega = 0$ and the formula reduces to $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta} = \begin{pmatrix} I & v\theta \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, a translation by θ along v. For a revolute joint, substituting $v = -\hat\omega \times q$, one can verify that $G(\theta)\,v = (I - e^{[\hat\omega]\theta})\,q$ (Exercise 12.1.3), so Eq. (12.1.5) is exactly the same as Eq. (12.1.2). The derivation of Eq. (12.1.5) follows the same method as finding $e^{[\hat\omega]\theta}$ from the series in Section 4.4.4: use $[\hat\omega]^3 = -[\hat\omega]$ to collect the series; the details are in Chapter 6. Its advantage is that revolute and prismatic joints use the same formula.

This chapter needs only the conclusion: **The motion of a joint is the matrix exponential of its screw axis.** The notation $e^{[\mathcal S]\theta}$ is exactly the "exponential" in the name "product of exponentials".

**Example 12.1.1** Write down the three joint screws of the planar 3R arm in Figure 12.1.1, and find the position of the end-effector when only joint 2 turns, with $\theta_2 = 30^\circ$.

All three axes are perpendicular to the page; take $\hat\omega = (0, 0, 1)$. For the points on the axes take the centres of the three joints, $q_1 = (0, 0, 0)$, $q_2 = (L_1, 0, 0)$, $q_3 = (L_1 + L_2, 0, 0)$. Compute $v_i = -\hat\omega \times q_i$ by Eq. (12.1.3); for example $v_2 = -(0, 0, 1) \times (L_1, 0, 0) = (0, -L_1, 0)$. So

$$
\mathcal S_1 = (0,\ 0,\ 1,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0)^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \mathcal S_2 = {{ex12_1.S2}}, \qquad \mathcal S_3 = {{ex12_1.S3}} .
$$

The pattern is clear: for a revolute joint in the plane, the two components of v are the coordinates (x, y) of the joint centre changed into (y, −x).

When only joint 2 turns, the end-effector turns through 30° about $q_2$. The distance from the end-effector to $q_2$ is $L_2 + L_3$, so by geometry the end-effector is at $(L_1 + (L_2 + L_3)\cos 30^\circ,\ (L_2 + L_3)\sin 30^\circ) = ({{ex12_1.tip_x:.4f}},\ {{ex12_1.tip_y:.3f}})$ m. Computing $e^{[\mathcal S_2]\theta_2} M$ with Eq. (12.1.5) gives the same position. ### 12.1.6 The home configuration and the six joint screws of the UR5e

Now we write down all the joint screws of a real robot arm. We use the UR5e model in the WenQuest parts library. It comes from the public models of MuJoCo Menagerie, and it is the arm shown in the 3D labs of this chapter.

**Base frame.** For {s} we take the base frame of the robot: the origin at the centre of the mounting face of the base, the z axis vertically upwards. The x and y axes point the same way as in the base frame the manufacturer uses when publishing its DH parameters (Section 12.3 checks this). In this frame, the arm stretches out horizontally along −x in the home configuration.

**Dimensions.** The distances between the joints are read from the joint table of the model (in m):

| Symbol | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $H_1$ | 0.163 | Height from joint 1 to joint 2 (shoulder height) |
| $W_1$ | 0.138 | Sideways offset of joint 2 from joint 1 |
| $L_1$ | 0.425 | Upper-arm length (joint 2 to joint 3) |
| $W_2$ | 0.131 | Sideways inset of joint 3 relative to joint 2 |
| $L_2$ | 0.392 | Forearm length (joint 3 to joint 4) |
| $W_3$ | 0.127 | Sideways offset of joint 5 from joint 4 |
| $H_2$ | 0.1 | Distance of joint 6 below joint 5 |
| $W_4$ | 0.1 | Distance along the axis of joint 6 from the centre of joint 6 to the flange face |

**Axes.** The axis of joint 1 points vertically upwards, $\hat\omega_1 = (0, 0, 1)$. ### 12.1.7 Screw axes in everyday life

A desk lamp usually has a base, two arm segments and a shade. Its hinges are all revolute joints, and the centre line of each hinge pin is an axis. Swing the lamp about one hinge, and everything beyond it turns about that pin while everything on the base side stays still. This is exactly the motion that Eq. (12.1.2) describes. The slides of a drawer form a prismatic joint, and its screw axis is just the direction of sliding, Eq. (12.1.4).

Writing down a screw axis takes only two things: which way the axis points, and which point it passes through. In the next section we shall see that combining the screw axes of all the joints with the home pose M gives the pose of the end-effector at any joint angles.

**Engineer's notes**

- The positive direction of each joint must agree with the controller. The direction of $\hat\omega$ agrees, by the right-hand rule, with the direction of turning in which the joint angle increases; if it is written the wrong way round, the computed end-effector turns to the mirror-image side. The most reliable check is to turn a single joint of the real robot through a small angle and see which way the end-effector goes.
- The home configuration must agree with the controller. Some manufacturers put "home" with the arm pointing straight up, others with it stretched out horizontally. If the robot is not in the configuration you assumed when the encoders read zero, subtract an offset angle from each joint angle, or simply rewrite M for the controller's home configuration.
- The point q on the axis can be chosen freely; choose whichever makes the computation simplest, usually the joint centre or the point where the axis meets a coordinate plane.
- When reading screw axes from a CAD model or from the parts library, first check the units (metres or millimetres), then check that $\|\hat\omega\| = 1$.
