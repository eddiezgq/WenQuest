### 9.5.1 The problem: measuring on the move

The quantities estimated in the previous sections were all constant: a force at rest, a fixed distance, the two parameters of a calibration line. A more common task for a robot is to estimate a quantity that keeps changing. An AGV carries material along a straight aisle, and every 0.1 s the controller needs to know where it is at that moment.

Two kinds of information are at hand. One is odometry: the wheel encoders give the vehicle's speed, multiplying by the time gives the distance travelled in these 0.1 s, and adding these up gives the position; this method is called dead reckoning. Over short times it is very accurate and very smooth, but wheel slip and errors in the wheel diameter put a small error into every step, and these errors only accumulate; they never die away on their own: after 100 steps, the standard deviation of the position error is $\sqrt{100} = 10$ times that of a single step (Theorem 9.1.4). The other is an external position measurement, for example from ultra-wideband (UWB) positioning anchors mounted on the workshop ceiling, which give a position every 0.1 s. Their error does not accumulate over time, but every reading fluctuates randomly by a few centimetres.

Each has its strengths and weaknesses, and they complement each other exactly. We already know from Section 9.4 that two independent measurements should be combined with weights equal to the reciprocals of their variances. The difficulty is this: odometry does not give a direct measurement of the position, but "the position at the previous instant plus the distance travelled", so its error depends on how accurate the estimate at the previous instant was; and the estimate at the previous instant was itself the result of combining the estimate from the instant before that with a measurement. We need a recursive method: at each new instant, using only the estimate at the previous instant and its variance, together with the odometry and the measurement at this instant, compute the estimate at this instant and its variance. This is the Kalman filter. This section derives it completely in the one-dimensional case. All the tools used come from the earlier sections of this chapter: the propagation of variance (Section 9.2), the Bayesian update of a Gaussian prior by a Gaussian measurement (Section 9.3), and weighting by variance (Section 9.4). The multidimensional Kalman filter and its many extensions are the subject of Chapter 46.

### 9.5.2 State, process model and measurement model

The **state** is a set of quantities that describes the situation of a system at this moment and is sufficient to predict how it will evolve. Here the state is the position x of the AGV along the aisle; the position at the k-th instant is written $x_k$, k = 0, 1, 2, …, and neighbouring instants are Δt apart.

**Definition 9.5.1 (Process model and measurement model)** The equation that describes how the state evolves from one instant to the next is called the **process model**; the equation that describes the relation between the measured value and the state is called the **measurement model**. For the AGV of this section,

$$
x_k = x_{k-1} + u\,\Delta t + w_{k-1} , \tag{9.5.1}
$$

$$
z_k = x_k + v_k . \tag{9.5.2}
$$

Here u is the speed given by odometry and $z_k$ is the UWB position reading. $w_{k-1}$ is called the **process noise**; it represents the odometry error in this step, that is, the difference between the distance actually travelled and uΔt; $v_k$ is called the **measurement noise**.

In this section we make the following assumptions about the noise:

1. $w_k \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_w^2)$ and $v_k \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_v^2)$, with known variances;
2. all the $w_k$, all the $v_k$ and the initial position $x_0$ are mutually independent.

A sequence of zero-mean noise values that are independent from one instant to the next is called **white noise**. The name is borrowed from optics: white light contains components of all frequencies, and the energy of noise that is independent from instant to instant is likewise spread evenly over all frequencies. Assumption 2 is important, and it often does not hold exactly; Section 9.5.6 will return to this point.

**Example 9.5.1** The odometry speed of the AGV is u = 0.5 m/s, the sampling period is Δt = 0.1 s, and the standard deviation of the odometry error per step is $\sigma_w$ = 0.01 m; the standard deviation of the UWB positioning is $\sigma_v$ = 0.05 m. When the AGV is switched on, the controller estimates that it is at x = 0, with a standard deviation of 0.2 m (it is actually at 0.15 m). Compute the estimates for the first three steps.

The next three subsections derive the formulas, and then we return to this example.

### 9.5.3 Prediction: the growth of uncertainty

Let the estimate at instant k − 1 be $\hat x_{k-1}$, with variance $P_{k-1}$. More precisely, after all the measurements up to instant k − 1 have been used, the posterior distribution of the position is $\mathcal N(\hat x_{k-1}, P_{k-1})$. In this section $P_k$ denotes the variance of the estimate; this is the usual notation in the Kalman filter literature, and it agrees with Chapter 46.

Before the measurement at instant k has arrived, what can we say about $x_k$? By the process model (9.5.1), $x_k$ is $x_{k-1}$ plus the constant uΔt plus $w_{k-1}$. Since $x_{k-1}$ and $w_{k-1}$ are independent, Theorems 9.1.1 and 9.1.4 give

$$
\hat x_k^- = \hat x_{k-1} + u\,\Delta t , \tag{9.5.3}
$$

$$
P_k^- = P_{k-1} + \sigma_w^2 . \tag{9.5.4}
$$

The superscript "−" means "before the measurement is used". The sum of two independent Gaussian variables is again a Gaussian variable (Section 9.2.5), so the predicted distribution is $\mathcal N(\hat x_k^-, P_k^-)$. This step is called prediction: the mean is pushed forward by odometry, and the variance gains one step of process noise. Prediction always increases the variance; without measurements the variance grows by $\sigma_w^2$ every step and the standard deviation grows as $\sqrt k$, which is exactly the accumulation of dead-reckoning error.

### 9.5.4 Update: correcting with the measurement

The measurement $z_k$ at instant k has arrived. The predicted distribution $\mathcal N(\hat x_k^-, P_k^-)$ is the prior; the measurement is $z_k = x_k + v_k$ with $v_k$ independent of the prior, which is exactly the situation of Theorem 9.3.3. Replacing μ₀, σ₀² and σ_z² in the theorem by $\hat x_k^-$, $P_k^-$ and $\sigma_v^2$ gives

$$
K_k = \frac{P_k^-}{P_k^- + \sigma_v^2} , \tag{9.5.5}
$$

$$
\hat x_k = \hat x_k^- + K_k\,(z_k - \hat x_k^-) , \tag{9.5.6}
$$

$$
P_k = (1 - K_k)\,P_k^- . \tag{9.5.7}
$$

$K_k$ is called the **Kalman gain**; it lies between 0 and 1 and decides how far this measurement can pull the prediction towards itself. $z_k - \hat x_k^-$ is the difference between the measured value and the predicted value, that is, the "unexpected" part of the measurement, and is called the **innovation**: if the measured value agrees exactly with the prediction, it brings no new information and the estimate need not be corrected. Eq. (9.5.7) shows that an update always decreases the variance.

Putting prediction and update together gives the one-dimensional Kalman filter:

1. Initialization: $\hat x_0$ and $P_0$ are given;
2. for k = 1, 2, …: predict with Eqs. (9.5.3) and (9.5.4), then update with Eqs. (9.5.5) to (9.5.7).

**Theorem 9.5.1** Under the model and assumptions of Section 9.5.2, if the initial distribution is $\mathcal N(\hat x_0, P_0)$, then for every k the posterior distribution of $x_k$ given $z_1, \ldots, z_k$ is exactly $\mathcal N(\hat x_k, P_k)$, where $\hat x_k$ and $P_k$ are computed by the recursion above.

*Proof*　By mathematical induction. For k = 0 the statement holds by assumption. Suppose it holds for k − 1, that is, given $z_1, \ldots, z_{k-1}$, $x_{k-1} \sim \mathcal N(\hat x_{k-1}, P_{k-1})$. By assumption 2, $w_{k-1}$ is independent of $x_0, w_0, \ldots, w_{k-2}, v_1, \ldots, v_{k-1}$, and hence of $x_{k-1}$ and of the measurements already made. So, given $z_1, \ldots, z_{k-1}$, $x_k = x_{k-1} + u\Delta t + w_{k-1}$ is the sum of two independent Gaussian variables plus a constant, and its distribution is $\mathcal N(\hat x_k^-, P_k^-)$. Now consider $z_k = x_k + v_k$: $v_k$ is independent of $x_k$ and of the earlier measurements, so given $x_k$, $z_k$ is conditionally independent of the earlier measurements (Section 9.3.6), and the earlier measurements have no influence on the likelihood of $z_k$. By Eq. (9.3.8), the posterior is proportional to $p(z_k \mid x_k)$ times the prior $\mathcal N(\hat x_k^-, P_k^-)$, and by Theorem 9.3.3 it is $\mathcal N(\hat x_k, P_k)$.　∎

The significance of Theorem 9.5.1 is this: the Kalman filter is not a smoothing trick that "happens to work well in practice", but an exact Bayesian computation under the model above. A Gaussian distribution is completely determined by its mean and variance, and after the linear prediction and update it is still Gaussian, so passing on just two numbers preserves all the information in all the past measurements. It also follows that, among all estimates that use only $z_1, \ldots, z_k$, $\hat x_k$ has the smallest mean squared error: for any number c computed from these data, taking the expectation with respect to the posterior distribution and arguing as in Theorem 9.4.1 gives $\mathrm E[(x_k - c)^2] = P_k + (\hat x_k - c)^2$, which is smallest when c is the posterior mean $\hat x_k$.

Step 1. Prediction: $\hat x_1^- = 0 + 0.5 \times 0.1 = 0.05$ m, $P_1^- = 0.04 + 0.0001$ = {{ex9_5.s1_Pm:.4f}} m². Gain: $K_1$ = {{ex9_5.s1_Pm:.4f}}/({{ex9_5.s1_Pm:.4f}} + 0.0025) ≈ {{ex9_5.s1_K:.4f}}. $$

The initial estimate is very poor (standard deviation 0.2 m), while the standard deviation of the UWB is only 0.05 m, so the gain is close to 1 and the first measurement is adopted almost completely; in a single step the standard deviation drops from 0.2 m to {{ex9_5.s1_sd:.4f}} m.

Step 2. $\hat x_2^- = {{ex9_5.s1_x:.4f}} + 0.05 = {{ex9_5.s2_xm:.4f}}$ m, $P_2^-$ = {{ex9_5.s2_Pm:.6f}} m², $K_2$ ≈ {{ex9_5.s2_K:.4f}}. As time goes on, the prediction becomes more and more accurate, the gain becomes smaller and smaller, and the role of the measurement becomes one of "fine adjustment".

**Comparison with a one-shot solution.** The Kalman filter works recursively, step by step. A completely different approach is also possible: treat all {{ex9_5.nb}} positions $x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_{100}$ as unknowns, stack the prior on the initial value, the 100 odometry equations $x_k - x_{k-1} = u\Delta t$ (standard deviation $\sigma_w$) and the 100 measurement equations $x_k = z_k$ (standard deviation $\sigma_v$) into one overdetermined system, and solve it in one go by the weighted least squares of Section 9.4. Program 9.5.1 does exactly this: the one-shot solution gives $x_{100}$ = {{ex9_5.batch_end:.6f}} m, and the Kalman filter gives $\hat x_{100}$ = {{ex9_5.kf_end:.6f}} m, the same to within $10^{-12}$ m; the variance given by Eq. (9.4.9) is also the same as $P_{100}$. This shows from another angle that the Kalman filter is a recursive algorithm for weighted least squares: it need not store the whole history of the data, and each step takes only a few multiplications and divisions, yet the final result is exactly the same as solving with all the data put together. (The one-shot solution also gives the positions at earlier instants, and these are more accurate than the filter's results, because they also use the measurements made after those instants. This is called smoothing and is discussed in Chapter 46.)

**The steady state.** Figure 9.5.3 shows that $P_k$ and $K_k$ stop changing after a dozen or so steps. This is because neither the process model nor the measurement model changes with time, so the variance added by prediction and the variance removed by update eventually balance. In the steady state $P_k^- = P_{k-1}^-$, which we write $P_\infty^-$. Substituting Eq. (9.5.7) into Eq. (9.5.4):

$$
P_\infty^- = \frac{P_\infty^-\,\sigma_v^2}{P_\infty^- + \sigma_v^2} + \sigma_w^2 .
$$

Multiplying both sides by $P_\infty^- + \sigma_v^2$ and rearranging gives $(P_\infty^-)^2 - \sigma_w^2 P_\infty^- - \sigma_w^2\sigma_v^2 = 0$; taking the positive root,

$$
P_\infty^- = \frac{\sigma_w^2 + \sqrt{\sigma_w^4 + 4\sigma_w^2\sigma_v^2}}{2} , \tag{9.5.8}
$$

$$
K_\infty = \frac{P_\infty^-}{P_\infty^- + \sigma_v^2} . \tag{9.5.9}
$$

Dividing $P_\infty^-$ by $\sigma_v^2$ shows that $K_\infty$ depends only on the ratio $\sigma_w/\sigma_v$: the larger the process noise is in relative terms (the less reliable the odometry), the larger the gain and the more the filter listens to the measurement. For Example 9.5.1, $\sigma_v/\sigma_w$ = 5 and $K_\infty$ ≈ {{ex9_5.K_inf:.4f}}; in the steady state the variance after the update is $P_\infty = (1 - K_\infty)P_\infty^-$, and the standard deviation of the estimate is $\sqrt{P_\infty}$ ≈ {{ex9_5.sd_inf:.1f}} mm, less than half that of a single UWB measurement. According to Program 9.5.1, $K_k$ comes within 1% of its steady-state value at step {{ex9_5.k_conv}}, and at step 100 $K_{100}$ = {{ex9_5.K_end:.6f}}, in agreement with Eq. (9.5.9).

In the steady state, Eq. (9.5.6) can be written $\hat x_k = (1 - K_\infty)\hat x_k^- + K_\infty z_k$. Without odometry (u = 0), this is the first-order low-pass filter $y_k = y_{k-1} + \alpha(x_k - y_{k-1})$ of Section 39.4, with α = $K_\infty$. The two differ in two respects: the α of a first-order low-pass filter has to be tuned by experience, whereas the Kalman filter computes the optimal value from the noise levels; and a first-order low-pass filter can only "smooth", so when it tracks a quantity moving at constant speed its output always lags behind the input, whereas the Kalman filter predicts the motion from odometry and has no such systematic lag.

**Example 9.5.3** The filter "says" that its error variance is $P_k$. Can we believe it?

Program 9.5.1 repeats Example 9.5.2 {{ex9_5.R}} times with different random seeds, computes the sample variance of the error at every step, and compares it with $P_k$ (Figure 9.5.3, left). At step 100 the sample standard deviation of the error is {{ex9_5.sd_mc:.1f}} mm, and the ratio of the sample variance to $P_{100}$ is {{ex9_5.ratio_mc:.3f}}; the mean of the squared error divided by $P_{100}$ is {{ex9_5.nees:.3f}}, close to 1. This shows that the filter estimates its own uncertainty correctly; such a filter is said to be "consistent". This check is very important in engineering: if $\sigma_w$ or $\sigma_v$ in the model is set wrongly, or if the noise is not in fact independent, the filter will still produce a $P_k$, but it may be far smaller than the true error variance. When the filter is "overconfident", its gain is too small and it filters out real changes as if they were noise; in severe cases the estimate drifts further and further from the true value, which is called filter divergence.

### 9.5.6 Towards many dimensions: the general form of the Kalman filter

When the AGV moves in the plane, its state is the pose (x, y, φ); if the velocity is to be estimated too, the state has still more components. Replacing every step of this section by matrices gives the general form of the Kalman filter. Let the state be $x_k \in \mathbb R^n$, with process model and measurement model

$$
x_k = F\,x_{k-1} + B\,u_{k-1} + w_{k-1} , \qquad z_k = H\,x_k + v_k ,
$$

and $w \sim \mathcal N(0, Q)$, $v \sim \mathcal N(0, R)$. By Theorem 9.2.3, the prediction is

$$
\hat x_k^- = F\,\hat x_{k-1} + B\,u_{k-1} , \qquad P_k^- = F\,P_{k-1}F^{\mathsf T} + Q . \tag{9.5.10}
$$

The update corresponds term by term to the one-dimensional form:

$$
K_k = P_k^-H^{\mathsf T}\big(HP_k^-H^{\mathsf T} + R\big)^{-1} , \qquad \hat x_k = \hat x_k^- + K_k\big(z_k - H\hat x_k^-\big) , \qquad P_k = (I - K_kH)\,P_k^- . \tag{9.5.11}
$$

Here P is the covariance matrix, and Q and R are the covariance matrices of the process noise and the measurement noise respectively. This is the standard notation of the Kalman filter literature; in this book R also denotes a rotation matrix, and Chapter 46 will keep the two apart where both appear. The formula for $P_k^-$ is precisely the covariance propagation of Eq. (9.2.5); the formula for the gain is the multidimensional generalization of Theorem 9.3.3, and its derivation needs the matrix inversion lemma, so it is left to Chapter 46. When H = I, F = I and all the matrices are 1×1, Eqs. (9.5.10) and (9.5.11) reduce to Eqs. (9.5.3) to (9.5.7) of this section.

Real robots seldom satisfy all the assumptions made here. The motion model and the measurement model are often nonlinear: for example, when the AGV turns, the change in position depends on the sine and cosine of the heading angle; a lidar measures the range and bearing to a landmark rather than the position itself. One then linearizes at the current estimate, as in Section 9.2.6, which gives the extended Kalman filter; when the errors are larger and linearization is not accurate enough, the unscented Kalman filter or the particle filter is used. Nor is the noise necessarily independent: wheel slip, for instance, often biases several consecutive steps to the same side. All this is the subject of Chapters 46 and 47. The probability tools of this chapter are the common foundation for understanding them.

Rudolf E. Kalman published his filtering method for discrete time in 1960, and the following year, together with Richard S. Bucy, gave the continuous-time form. Earlier, during the Second World War, Norbert Wiener had studied a similar problem for anti-aircraft fire control, but his method required the signals to be stationary and could only be designed in the frequency domain; Kalman's state-space method applies to time-varying systems and lends itself to recursive computation on a digital computer. Soon after Kalman's paper appeared, Stanley F. Schmidt of NASA's Ames Research Center recognized its value for navigation in the Apollo programme, and developed a way of linearizing the nonlinear orbital equations, the forerunner of the extended Kalman filter. The Kalman filter then became the standard method of aerospace navigation, and today it is also one of the most basic algorithms in robot localization, sensor fusion and autonomous driving.

### 9.5.7 Everyday examples

When you navigate on foot with a phone, the phone combines two kinds of information: the accelerometer and the step counter estimate how far each step went and in which direction (the equivalent of odometry), and GPS gives a position once a second (the equivalent of a measurement). In open ground, GPS keeps correcting the accumulated error of step counting; when you walk into a tunnel or between tall buildings, the GPS signal drops out, and the blue dot on the map keeps moving forward with your steps, but the pale circle around it that shows the uncertainty grows larger and larger: this is the growth of $P_k^-$ when there is only prediction and no update. When you come out of the tunnel and GPS returns, the circle shrinks at once and the blue dot jumps back to the correct position.

Tasting a dish for salt while cooking works the same way: from how much salt you put in, you already have a "prediction" in mind; a taste is a "measurement". If you know for certain how much salt you added, the taste serves only for fine adjustment; if someone else cooked the dish, you go mainly by your tongue. Experienced cooks taste less often precisely because their "process model" is accurate.

Starting from a fluctuating reading, this chapter built up the concepts of a random variable, variance and the Gaussian distribution; learned to propagate errors from the joints to the end-effector, to revise our knowledge in the light of measurements with Bayes' rule, and to combine several measurements by maximum likelihood and weighted least squares; and finally put all of these together into the Kalman filter. The chapter summary collects these results in one place.

**Engineer's notes**

- How well a Kalman filter works depends mainly on whether $\sigma_w$ and $\sigma_v$ (Q and R in many dimensions) are set correctly. $\sigma_v$ can be measured at rest; $\sigma_w$ is hard to measure directly, so usually one first estimates a value from the calibration error of the odometry and then adjusts it with a consistency check (Example 9.5.3): the mean of the squared error divided by P should be close to 1.
- Set the noise somewhat too large rather than too small. With the noise set too small, the filter is overconfident and may diverge; with it set too large, the estimate is merely a little worse.
- The innovation $z_k - \hat x_k^-$ is a window for checking the sensor. The innovation divided by $\sqrt{P_k^- + \sigma_v^2}$ should behave like a standard Gaussian variable; if at some step it exceeds 3 or 4, the measurement is probably an outlier (Section 9.4.6) and should be discarded rather than used for the update.
- Measurement timestamps must be accurate. When the AGV is travelling at 0.5 m/s, a measurement that arrives 20 ms late but is treated as the value at the current instant introduces an error of 10 mm, of the same order as the noise of the measurement itself.
- The initial variance $P_0$ must be set large enough to reflect the real ignorance about the initial state. If it is set too small, the filter distrusts the measurements at the start and takes a long time to correct the initial error.
