### 9.2.1 The problem: a little off in the joint angles, how much off at the end-effector?

The controller of a robot arm computes the end-effector position from the joint angles. The joint angles come from encoders, which have quantization errors (Section 9.1); gearboxes have backlash, and links bend under load; all of these make the joint angles the controller "thinks" it has differ by a small amount from the actual ones. Suppose the upper arm and forearm of the UR5e work in a vertical plane and are treated as the planar 2R arm of Section 2.1, with $L_1$ = 0.425 m, $L_2$ = 0.392 m and joint angles θ = (30°, 60°), and that each of the two joint angles has an independent random error with a standard deviation of 0.1°. How large is the error of the end-effector position, and in which direction?

0.1° looks small, but multiplied by an arm nearly a metre long it is of the order of a millimetre or more, which can no longer be neglected in assembly and welding. More importantly there is the direction: the end-effector error is not equally large in all directions. If the error lies mainly along a slot in the workpiece, an insertion may go well; if it lies mainly across the slot, the part may jam. So we need a tool that describes "the size and direction of the errors of several quantities, and the relations between them". The variance of Section 9.1 describes only one quantity; this section generalizes it to the covariance matrix and answers the question: given the covariance matrix of the inputs (the joint angles), how do we find the covariance matrix of the outputs (the end-effector position)? This problem is called **uncertainty propagation**.

### 9.2.2 Two random variables: covariance and correlation coefficient

The end-effector position has two coordinates, x and y, and since their errors both come from the same two joint angles, they cannot be unrelated. Let us first look at a simpler example. On each side of a gripper there is a force sensor, and the two share a power supply. Fluctuations S of the supply voltage make both readings high together or low together; in addition each reading has its own noise $N_1$, $N_2$:

$$
X = F + S + N_1 , \qquad Y = F + S + N_2 ,
$$

where F is the true force. The scatter plot in the middle of Figure 9.2.1 shows many such pairs (X, Y): the cloud of points leans towards the upper right. When X is high, Y also tends to be high. The left plot shows the readings of two independent sensors: the cloud is round and shows no tendency at all. The natural way to measure this tendency to "vary together" is to multiply the two deviations and average: when both are high or both are low the product is positive; when one is high and the other low it is negative.

**Definition 9.2.1 (Covariance and correlation coefficient)** Let the random variables X and Y have means $\mu_X$, $\mu_Y$ and standard deviations $\sigma_X$, $\sigma_Y$. Their **covariance** and **correlation coefficient** are

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathrm E\big[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)\big] , \tag{9.2.1}
$$

$$
\rho_{XY} = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y} . \tag{9.2.2}
$$

The unit of the covariance is the product of the units of the two quantities, and its size changes with the units; the correlation coefficient divides out the two standard deviations and is a number without a unit, which makes comparison easy. Clearly Cov(X, X) = Var(X). If X and Y are independent, then by Eq. (9.1.17) Cov(X, Y) = E[X − μ_X] E[Y − μ_Y] = 0 and ρ = 0; they are then said to be uncorrelated. The converse does not necessarily hold: being uncorrelated says only that there is no "linear" relation. For example, if X is uniformly distributed on [−1, 1] and Y = X², then Y is completely determined by X, yet the covariance is zero. Only for the multivariate Gaussian distribution of Section 9.2.5 are uncorrelatedness and independence equivalent.

The concept of "correlation" was introduced by Francis Galton in the 1880s in his studies of heredity and anthropometry; in 1888 he published a method of measuring the degree of correlation, and in 1895 Karl Pearson gave Eq. (9.2.2), the form in general use today.

**Theorem 9.2.1** The correlation coefficient satisfies $|\rho_{XY}| \leq 1$. Equality holds if and only if Y is a linear function of X (with probability 1).

*Proof*　Write $U = (X - \mu_X)/\sigma_X$ and $V = (Y - \mu_Y)/\sigma_Y$; then E[U²] = E[V²] = 1 and E[UV] = ρ. For any real number t,

$$
0 \leq \mathrm E\big[(V - tU)^2\big] = 1 - 2t\rho + t^2 .
$$

This is a quadratic function of t that is non-negative everywhere, so its discriminant satisfies $4\rho^2 - 4 \leq 0$, that is, |ρ| ≤ 1. If |ρ| = 1, take t = ρ to get E[(V − ρU)²] = 1 − ρ² = 0; a non-negative random variable with zero expectation must equal zero with probability 1, so V = ρU, and Y is a linear function of X. Conversely, if Y = aX + b (a ≠ 0), then V = ±U (with the sign of a), and ρ = E[UV] = ±1.　∎

ρ = 1 means that all the points lie on a rising straight line, and ρ = −1 that they lie on a falling one; the closer |ρ| is to 1, the narrower the cloud. With the covariance we can write down the variance of a sum of two quantities that are not independent. Repeating the derivation of Section 9.1.6, the cross terms no longer vanish:

$$
\mathrm{Var}(X \pm Y) = \mathrm{Var}(X) + \mathrm{Var}(Y) \pm 2\,\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) . \tag{9.2.3}
$$

**Example 9.2.1** Two force sensors share a power supply. The fluctuation S caused by the supply has a standard deviation of 0.06 N, the noise of each sensor has a standard deviation of 0.08 N, and the three are independent. Find the covariance and the correlation coefficient of the two readings, and the standard deviations of their sum and their difference.

X − μ_X = S + N_1 and Y − μ_Y = S + N_2. Expanding the product, the cross terms between S and N_1, N_2, and between N_1 and N_2, all have zero expectation, leaving only E[S²]:

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(X, Y) = \sigma_S^2 = 0.06^2 = 0.0036\ \mathrm{N^2} , \qquad
\sigma_X^2 = \sigma_Y^2 = 0.06^2 + 0.08^2 = 0.01\ \mathrm{N^2} , \qquad \rho = 0.36 .
$$

By Eq. (9.2.3) the variance of the difference is 0.01 + 0.01 − 2 × 0.0036, and its standard deviation is {{ex9_2.sd_diff:.4f}} N, exactly $\sqrt 2 \times 0.08$: the common fluctuation of the supply cancels completely in the subtraction. The standard deviation of the sum is {{ex9_2.sd_sum:.4f}} N, larger than the {{ex9_2.sd_sum_indep:.4f}} N we would get if the two were independent. Program 9.2.1 simulates this {{ex9_2.NMC:,}} times: the sample covariance is {{ex9_2.cov_mc:.2f}} × 10⁻⁴ N², the sample correlation coefficient is {{ex9_2.rho_mc:.3f}}, and the sample standard deviation of the difference is {{ex9_2.sd_diff_mc:.4f}} N.

This example illustrates the principle of differential measurement: when two channels are subject to the same interference, taking their difference removes it. The Wheatstone bridge and the differential amplifier of Section 39.2 are both based on this principle. Conversely, if the readings of the two channels are added or averaged in the hope of reducing the noise to $1/\sqrt 2$, this hope is not fulfilled, because the common part is not reduced by averaging.

### 9.2.3 Random vectors and covariance matrices

Several random variables arranged in a column, $x = (X_1, \ldots, X_n)^{\mathsf T}$, form a **random vector**. The end-effector position (x, y), the pose (x, y, φ) of an AGV and the six joint angles are all random vectors. Its mean is the vector of the means of its components, μ = E[x]. To describe its spread we need the variance of each component and the covariance between every two components.

**Definition 9.2.2 (Covariance matrix)** The **covariance matrix** of a random vector x is

$$
\Sigma = \mathrm E\big[(x - \mu)(x - \mu)^{\mathsf T}\big] , \qquad \Sigma_{ij} = \mathrm{Cov}(X_i, X_j) . \tag{9.2.4}
$$

Here $(x - \mu)(x - \mu)^{\mathsf T}$ is an n×1 column vector times a 1×n row vector, giving an n×n matrix whose (i, j) element is $(X_i - \mu_i)(X_j - \mu_j)$; the expectation of a matrix is taken element by element. So the diagonal elements of Σ are the variances of the components, and the off-diagonal elements are the covariances between pairs of them. For two components,

$$
\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_x^2 & \rho\,\sigma_x\sigma_y \\ \rho\,\sigma_x\sigma_y & \sigma_y^2 \end{pmatrix} .
$$

This book writes covariance matrices as Σ, the usual notation in probability theory and the robotics literature. It is the same letter as the diagonal matrix Σ of the singular value decomposition in Section 2.4, but the two never appear in the same place, so no confusion arises.

**Theorem 9.2.2** A covariance matrix is a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix: $\Sigma^{\mathsf T} = \Sigma$, and $a^{\mathsf T}\Sigma a \geq 0$ for every vector a.

*Proof*　Symmetry follows from $\mathrm{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = \mathrm{Cov}(X_j, X_i)$. For any constant vector a, $a^{\mathsf T}(x - \mu)$ is a scalar random variable, and its variance is non-negative:

$$
0 \leq \mathrm E\big[(a^{\mathsf T}(x - \mu))^2\big] = \mathrm E\big[a^{\mathsf T}(x - \mu)(x - \mu)^{\mathsf T}a\big] = a^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\,a .
$$

∎

The quantity $a^{\mathsf T}\Sigma a$ that appears in the proof has an intuitive meaning: for a unit vector a, $a^{\mathsf T}(x - \mu)$ is the projection of the deviation onto the direction a, so $a^{\mathsf T}\Sigma a$ is exactly "the variance along the direction a". By the spectral theorem of Section 2.3, the symmetric matrix Σ has a set of mutually orthogonal eigenvectors $v_1, \ldots, v_n$, and the corresponding eigenvalues $\lambda_i = v_i^{\mathsf T}\Sigma v_i$ are the variances along these directions, all non-negative by Theorem 9.2.2. The largest eigenvalue is the largest variance over all directions, and its eigenvector is the direction of the largest error. This is precisely the "in which direction" asked in Section 9.2.1.

### 9.2.4 Propagation through linear transformations

The simplest and also the most important propagation relation is the linear one.

**Theorem 9.2.3 (Linear propagation)** Let a random vector x have mean μ and covariance matrix Σ. If y = Ax + b, with A a constant matrix and b a constant vector, then

$$
\mathrm E[y] = A\mu + b , \qquad \Sigma_y = A\,\Sigma\,A^{\mathsf T} . \tag{9.2.5}
$$

*Proof*　By the linearity of expectation (Theorem 9.1.1, applied once to each component), E[y] = Aμ + b. Hence y − E[y] = A(x − μ), and

$$
\Sigma_y = \mathrm E\big[A(x - \mu)(x - \mu)^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}\big] = A\,\mathrm E\big[(x - \mu)(x - \mu)^{\mathsf T}\big]A^{\mathsf T} = A\,\Sigma\,A^{\mathsf T} ,
$$

where the constant matrix A can be taken outside the expectation for the same reason as in Theorem 9.1.1.　∎

Eq. (9.2.5) contains several of the results of Section 9.1. When y is a scalar, $y = a^{\mathsf T}x$, we get $\mathrm{Var}(y) = a^{\mathsf T}\Sigma a$; if the components are independent, Σ is diagonal and $\mathrm{Var}(y) = \sum_i a_i^2\sigma_i^2$, which generalizes Eq. (9.1.18) and Theorem 9.1.2. Taking $a = (1, \pm 1)^{\mathsf T}$ gives Eq. (9.2.3).

A common special case is the change of coordinate frame. Let the covariance matrix of an error in the body frame {b} be $\Sigma_b$, and let the orientation of {b} relative to the space frame {s} be $R_{sb}$. The components of the error vector in the two frames satisfy $e_s = R_{sb}e_b$ (Chapter 4), so by Eq. (9.2.5)

$$
\Sigma_s = R_{sb}\,\Sigma_b\,R_{sb}^{\mathsf T} . \tag{9.2.6}
$$

This is exactly the same as the transformation rule for matrix-type quantities in Eq. (2.2.7): a covariance matrix is a second-order tensor, and on a change of frame it is multiplied by the rotation matrix once on each side.

**Example 9.2.2** An AGV uses the lidar at its front to measure the position of a landmark ahead. The range measurement has a standard deviation of 0.02 m along the beam (the x axis of the body frame); the error of the angle measurement gives a standard deviation of 0.05 m across the beam (the y axis of the body frame); the two are independent. The heading of the AGV makes an angle of 30° with the x axis of {s}. Find the covariance matrix of the error of the landmark position in {s}.

In the body frame, $\Sigma_b = \mathrm{diag}(0.02^2, 0.05^2) = {{ex9_2.Sig_b}} \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2}$. By Eq. (9.2.6), with $R_{sb} = R(30^\circ)$,

$$
\Sigma_s = {{ex9_2.Sig_s}} \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2} .
$$

In {s} the standard deviations in the x and y directions are {{ex9_2.sxs:.1f}} mm and {{ex9_2.sys_:.1f}} mm, and the correlation coefficient is {{ex9_2.rho_s:.3f}}. Two errors that were originally uncorrelated have become correlated in a rotated frame. Whether they are correlated depends on the choice of frame, while the shape and orientation of the error ellipse itself do not change: the trace and the determinant of the matrix are the same before and after the transformation (Section 2.2). Program 9.2.1 generates random errors in the body frame, transforms them to {s} and takes statistics there; the sample covariance matrix is ${{ex9_2.Sig_s_mc}} \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2}$.

### 9.2.5 The multivariate Gaussian distribution and the uncertainty ellipse

A one-dimensional Gaussian distribution is completely determined by μ and σ². The same holds in several dimensions: given a mean vector and a covariance matrix, there is a corresponding Gaussian distribution.

**Definition 9.2.3 (Multivariate Gaussian distribution)** Let Σ be an n×n symmetric positive definite matrix. If the probability density of an n-dimensional random vector x is

$$
\mathcal N(x; \mu, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^n \det\Sigma}}\,\exp\!\Big(-\frac12 (x - \mu)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)\Big) , \tag{9.2.7}
$$

x is said to follow the **multivariate Gaussian distribution** with mean μ and covariance matrix Σ, written x ~ 𝒩(μ, Σ).

For n = 1, Σ = σ² and Eq. (9.2.7) is just Eq. (9.1.13). If Σ is diagonal, the components are independent and the density is the product of n one-dimensional Gaussian densities: the quadratic form in the exponent becomes $\sum_i (x_i - \mu_i)^2/\sigma_i^2$, and $\det\Sigma = \prod_i\sigma_i^2$. Conversely, if the components of a multivariate Gaussian distribution are pairwise uncorrelated, Σ is diagonal, the density factorizes into a product, and the components are independent. This is what Section 9.2.2 meant by saying that, for the Gaussian distribution, uncorrelatedness and independence are equivalent.

A Gaussian distribution remains Gaussian under linear transformations: if x ~ 𝒩(μ, Σ) and A is an invertible square matrix, then y = Ax + b ~ 𝒩(Aμ + b, AΣAᵀ). This takes only one step of calculation: the density of y equals the density of x at $x = A^{-1}(y - b)$ divided by |det A| (volumes are enlarged |det A| times, so the density shrinks correspondingly). Substituting into Eq. (9.2.7), the quadratic form in the exponent is

$$
(A^{-1}(y - b) - \mu)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}(A^{-1}(y - b) - \mu) = (y - A\mu - b)^{\mathsf T}(A\Sigma A^{\mathsf T})^{-1}(y - A\mu - b) ,
$$

and the coefficient is $1/\big(|\det A|\sqrt{(2\pi)^n\det\Sigma}\big) = 1/\sqrt{(2\pi)^n\det(A\Sigma A^{\mathsf T})}$, which is exactly the density of 𝒩(Aμ + b, AΣAᵀ). The result still holds when A is not square; the proof is omitted. This also confirms that the μ and Σ in Eq. (9.2.7) really are the mean and the covariance matrix: take Σ = LLᵀ (L invertible) and let w be a standard Gaussian vector with independent components; then the density of μ + Lw is exactly Eq. (9.2.7), while by Theorem 9.2.3 its mean and covariance matrix are μ and LLᵀ = Σ (Exercise 9.2.4). So a Gaussian distribution, after linear propagation, is still completely determined by the mean and covariance matrix of Eq. (9.2.5). This is the fundamental reason why the Kalman filter (Section 9.5) can compute with means and covariance matrices alone.

The contour lines of the density are the points where the quadratic form in the exponent is constant:

**Definition 9.2.4 (Mahalanobis distance and uncertainty ellipse)** The quantity

$$
d(x) = \sqrt{(x - \mu)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}(x - \mu)} \tag{9.2.8}
$$

is called the **Mahalanobis distance** from x to the centre μ of the distribution. The curve (a surface in three dimensions) formed by the points with d(x) = c is called the cσ **uncertainty ellipse** (ellipsoid).

The Mahalanobis distance was introduced by the Indian statistician Prasanta Chandra Mahalanobis in 1936. It differs from the ordinary distance in that it "uses the standard deviation as the ruler": along a direction of large variance, a deviation of one millimetre is nothing much; along a direction of small variance, a deviation of one millimetre is quite suspicious. In one dimension d = |x − μ|/σ, which is just "how many σ off".

Why is this contour line an ellipse? Let $\Sigma = V\Lambda V^{\mathsf T}$ be the spectral decomposition (Section 2.3), where the columns of V are the unit eigenvectors $v_i$ and $\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$. Let $u = V^{\mathsf T}(x - \mu)$, the components of the deviation along the eigenvectors; then $\Sigma^{-1} = V\Lambda^{-1}V^{\mathsf T}$, and

$$
d^2 = u^{\mathsf T}\Lambda^{-1}u = \frac{u_1^2}{\lambda_1} + \cdots + \frac{u_n^2}{\lambda_n} = c^2 . \tag{9.2.9}
$$

This is the standard equation of an ellipse (ellipsoid) whose principal axes are the eigenvectors and whose semi-axes have lengths $c\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. Therefore: **the principal axes of the uncertainty ellipse lie along the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, and the semi-axis lengths are c times the square roots of the eigenvalues** (Figure 9.2.2).

How much probability does the ellipse contain?

**Theorem 9.2.4** Let x ~ 𝒩(μ, Σ) be a two-dimensional Gaussian random vector. Then

$$
\Pr\big(d(x) \leq c\big) = 1 - e^{-c^2/2} . \tag{9.2.10}
$$

*Proof*　By the transformation above, $w_i = u_i/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ are two independent standard Gaussian variables (a linear transformation preserves Gaussianity, and the covariance matrix becomes the identity matrix), and $d^2 = w_1^2 + w_2^2$. The joint density of $(w_1, w_2)$ is $\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(w_1^2 + w_2^2)/2}$, to be integrated over the disc of radius c. Change to polar coordinates as in the proof of Theorem 9.1.3:

$$
\Pr(d \leq c) = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{c}\frac{1}{2\pi}\,e^{-r^2/2}\,r\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\varphi = \big[-e^{-r^2/2}\big]_0^{c} = 1 - e^{-c^2/2} .
$$

∎

So the two-dimensional 1σ ellipse contains only {{ex9_2.p1:.2f}}% of the probability, far less than the 68% of ±σ in one dimension; the 2σ ellipse contains {{ex9_2.p2:.2f}}%; to contain 95%, set $1 - e^{-c^2/2} = 0.95$, which gives $c = \sqrt{-2\ln 0.05}$ = {{ex9_2.c95:.3f}}. The higher the dimension, the less probability an ellipsoid of the same scale contains. When reporting an "error ellipse", always state its scale (how many σ) or the probability it contains.

### 9.2.6 Linearization of nonlinear functions

The relation p = f(θ) between the end-effector position and the joint angles is not linear, so Eq. (9.2.5) cannot be used directly. But the errors of the joint angles are small, and over a small range every smooth function is approximately linear. For a function of one variable this is familiar: $f(\theta) \approx f(\mu) + f'(\mu)(\theta - \mu)$, the curve is replaced by its tangent. The "derivative" of a function of several variables is the matrix of partial derivatives, the Jacobian matrix $J = \partial f/\partial\theta$, whose (i, j) element is the partial derivative of $f_i$ with respect to $\theta_j$ (the rate of change of $f_i$ when only $\theta_j$ varies and the others are held fixed). For the 2R arm it is Eq. (2.1.9) of Section 2.1: there we used it to turn joint velocities into end-effector velocities, and here we use it to turn small deviations of the joint angles into small deviations of the end-effector, by the same reasoning. Hence

$$
f(\theta) \approx f(\mu) + J\,(\theta - \mu) , \qquad J = \frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}\Big|_{\theta = \mu} . \tag{9.2.11}
$$

This step is called **linearization**. The right-hand side is a linear function of θ, so by Theorem 9.2.3

$$
\mathrm E[p] \approx f(\mu) , \qquad \Sigma_p \approx J\,\Sigma_\theta\,J^{\mathsf T} . \tag{9.2.12}
$$

This is the first-order formula for uncertainty propagation. If the components of the input are independent and the output is a scalar, it becomes

$$
\sigma_y^2 \approx \sum_i \Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\Big)^2\sigma_i^2 , \tag{9.2.13}
$$

that is, the error of each input is multiplied by its "sensitivity coefficient" $\partial f/\partial x_i$, and the results are added in squares. This is the law of propagation of uncertainty of the *Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement* (GUM); Eq. (39.1.4) is the special case in which all the sensitivity coefficients are 1. In robotics, the 1986 paper by Randall C. Smith and Peter Cheeseman used Eq. (9.2.12) systematically to compute the uncertainty of the positions of robots and sensors after a chain of coordinate transformations, and this approach became the foundation of later work on localization and mapping for mobile robots.

**Example 9.2.3** For the planar 2R arm of Section 9.2.1 at θ = (30°, 60°), find the covariance matrix of the end-effector position, the standard deviations in the two directions and the uncertainty ellipse. Then repeat the calculation for the nearly straight configuration θ = (30°, 10°).

The standard deviation of the joint angles is 0.1° = {{ex9_2.sth_mrad:.4f}} mrad; the two joints are independent, so $\Sigma_\theta = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma^2, \sigma^2)$. By Eq. (2.1.9),

$$
J = {{ex9_2.JA}}\ \mathrm{m/rad} .
$$

By Eq. (9.2.12),

$$
\Sigma_p = \sigma^2 J J^{\mathsf T} = {{ex9_2.SpA}}\ \mathrm{mm^2} .
$$

The standard deviations in the x and y directions are $\sigma_x$ = {{ex9_2.sxA:.4f}} mm and $\sigma_y$ = {{ex9_2.syA:.4f}} mm, and the correlation coefficient is {{ex9_2.rA:.4f}}, a strong negative correlation: when x is too large, y is most likely too small (Figure 9.2.1, right). The square roots of the two eigenvalues of $\Sigma_p$, that is, the two semi-axes of the 1σ ellipse, are {{ex9_2.l1A:.4f}} mm and {{ex9_2.l2A:.4f}} mm; the major axis makes an angle of {{ex9_2.angA:.1f}}° with the x axis. The semi-axes of the 95% ellipse are {{ex9_2.a95A:.3f}} mm and {{ex9_2.b95A:.3f}} mm.

This shape can be understood geometrically. When joint 1 turns, the end-effector moves around the base, with a velocity perpendicular to the line from the base to the end-effector; when joint 2 turns, the end-effector moves around the elbow, with a velocity perpendicular to the line from the elbow to the end-effector, that is, perpendicular to the forearm. Here the forearm points straight up (θ₁ + θ₂ = 90°), so the error of joint 2 moves the end-effector horizontally, and the error of joint 1 moves it perpendicular to the line from the base. Both have large x components, while the y component comes only from joint 1 and has the opposite sign to its x component; so the ellipse is both elongated and tilted.

Program 9.2.1 checks this result by the **Monte Carlo method**, drawing {{ex9_2.NMC:,}} sets of joint angles at random from 𝒩(30°, 0.1²) and 𝒩(60°, 0.1²), computing the end-effector positions by forward kinematics (without any approximation), and then taking the sample standard deviations and the correlation coefficient; it obtains {{ex9_2.sxA_mc:.4f}} mm, {{ex9_2.syA_mc:.4f}} mm and {{ex9_2.rA_mc:.4f}}, which agree with the linearized results to three significant figures. The samples inside the 1-fold, 2-fold and 95% ellipses make up {{ex9_2.f1:.2f}}%, {{ex9_2.f2:.2f}}% and {{ex9_2.f95:.2f}}%, in agreement with the {{ex9_2.p1:.2f}}%, {{ex9_2.p2:.2f}}% and 95% of Theorem 9.2.4. The idea of the Monte Carlo method is to simulate a random phenomenon directly by a large number of random trials; Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann developed it in the late 1940s for computing neutron transport, and the 1949 paper by Nicholas Metropolis and Ulam made it widely known. It is simple and reliable but needs many evaluations; the linearized formula needs only one evaluation but is an approximation. In engineering the former is often used to check the latter.

For θ = (30°, 10°),

$$
\Sigma_p = {{ex9_2.SpB}}\ \mathrm{mm^2} ,
$$

and the two semi-axes are {{ex9_2.l1B:.3f}} mm and {{ex9_2.l2B:.4f}} mm, a ratio of {{ex9_2.ratioB:.0f}} (compared with {{ex9_2.ratioA:.1f}} at θ = (30°, 60°)). The ellipse has almost degenerated into a line segment perpendicular to the arm (Figure 9.2.3). When the arm is nearly straight, turning either joint moves the end-effector perpendicular to the arm, while along the arm it hardly moves: the distance from the end-effector to the base depends only on θ₂, and when θ₂ varies around 10°, cos θ₂ hardly changes. This is the situation discussed in Section 2.4, where one singular value of the Jacobian matrix tends to zero near a singular configuration. From the point of view of accuracy it has a good side: the positioning error along the arm is very small.

**Example 9.2.4** Each of the six joint angles of the UR5e has an independent error with a standard deviation of 0.02°, and the configuration is θ = (30°, −60°, 90°, −120°, −90°, 45°) of Example 12.3.1. Find the covariance matrix of the position of the flange centre.

The end-effector position p is a three-dimensional function of θ, and the Jacobian matrix J is 3×6. Its column i is the velocity of the end-effector when only joint i turns, which equals the cross product of the current direction $\hat\omega_i$ of joint axis i with "the vector from a point on the axis to the end-effector" (v = ω × r of Section 3.6). Program 9.2.1 computes the current positions of the axes from the screw axes of Table 12.1.1, finds J by this geometric method, and checks it by differences (changing one joint angle at a time by ±10⁻⁶ rad and seeing how far the end-effector moves). The flange centre is at ${{ex9_2.pur}}$ m, the same as in Example 12.3.1. By Eq. (9.2.12),

$$
\Sigma_p = \sigma^2\,J J^{\mathsf T} = {{ex9_2.Sur}} \times 10^{-8}\ \mathrm{m^2} ,
$$

where σ is the joint-angle standard deviation in radians, 0.02° = 0.02 × π/180 rad.

The standard deviations in the x, y and z directions are ${{ex9_2.sdur}}$ mm. The three semi-axes are {{ex9_2.lmax:.3f}} mm, {{ex9_2.lmid:.3f}} mm and {{ex9_2.lmin:.3f}} mm, and the longest principal axis has the direction ${{ex9_2.v1}}$, close to vertical. Broken down by joint, joints 1, 2 and 3 contribute {{ex9_2.shareJ1:.0f}}%, {{ex9_2.shareJ2:.0f}}% and {{ex9_2.shareJ3:.0f}}% of the total variance, while the three wrist joints together contribute only a few per cent, because they are close to the flange centre and their "lever arms" are short. With {{ex9_2.n_ur:,}} Monte Carlo runs, Program 9.2.1 obtains standard deviations of ${{ex9_2.sdur_mc}}$ mm in the three directions.

This result is of direct practical value: to improve the positioning accuracy of this arm in this configuration, the accuracy of the three joints near the base should be improved first. The direction of the largest end-effector error is vertical; if the task is sensitive to height (press-fitting, for example), this is the direction that needs particular attention.

**When linearization fails.** Eq. (9.2.12) assumes that the errors are small enough for f to be approximately linear over their range. If the joint-angle standard deviations of the 2R arm are increased to 8°, the Monte Carlo points no longer form an ellipse but bend into a banana shape along an arc centred on the base (Figure 9.2.4). The mean of the end-effector position is no longer equal to f(μ) but is shifted towards the base by {{ex9_2.shift_mm:.1f}} mm: points that swing to either side along the arc all fall on the inner side of the chord, so on average they move towards the centre of the circle. Expanding f to second order gives a shift of approximately $\frac12\sum_i \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial\theta_i^2}\sigma_i^2$, which works out at {{ex9_2.shift2_mm:.1f}} mm, in agreement with the Monte Carlo result. A simple way to judge whether linearization is usable is to compare how much J changes across the range of the error: if σ times the second derivative is much smaller than J itself, linearization is usable. For robot arms with joint-angle errors within a few tenths of a degree this always holds; but after a mobile robot has travelled a long distance, the error of its heading angle can reach ten degrees or more, the distribution of its position then bends into a banana shape, linearization-based methods (such as the extended Kalman filter of Chapter 46) give biased results, and methods such as the particle filter are needed.

### 9.2.7 An everyday example

Point a hand-held laser pointer at a wall 5 m away; if your wrist trembles by 0.5°, the dot wobbles on the wall by about 5 m × 0.5 × π/180 ≈ 4 cm; double the distance and the wobble doubles. This is Eq. (9.2.13) with only "one joint": the sensitivity coefficient is the distance to the wall. The hand itself also shifts by a few millimetres, and the dot moves by as much as the hand shifts, independently of the distance. The two parts of the error are independent, and their variances add: the variance of the dot equals (distance × standard deviation of the rotation angle)² plus (standard deviation of the shift)². Close to the wall the shift dominates; far from it the rotation dominates. When a marksman shoots at long range, the first thing to steady is the rotation of the gun, for the same reason.

This section has derived the errors of results from known errors. The next section asks the reverse question: after seeing a measurement result with an error, how should we revise our knowledge of the unknown quantity? The answer is Bayes' rule.

**Engineer's notes**

- For uncertainty propagation, first estimate with the linearized formula (9.2.12), then check with a Monte Carlo simulation of a few thousand to a few tens of thousands of runs; this is reliable and efficient. If the two differ noticeably, the errors are too large or the function is strongly curved at that point, and the Monte Carlo result should be taken. Supplement 1 to the GUM, JCGM 101, specifies the procedure for propagating uncertainty by the Monte Carlo method.
- A covariance matrix is transformed between frames by $R\Sigma R^{\mathsf T}$, multiplied on both sides. Multiplying on one side only gives a matrix that is not symmetric, which is a simple sign of a programming error.
- In numerical computation a covariance matrix may lose its symmetry or acquire very small negative eigenvalues through rounding errors; the usual remedy is to take $(\Sigma + \Sigma^{\mathsf T})/2$ and set the negative eigenvalues to zero.
- When reporting an error ellipse, state clearly its scale (how many σ) or the probability it contains. The two-dimensional "1σ ellipse" contains only about 39% of the points, not 68%.
- The unit of the Jacobian matrix is m/rad, so the joint-angle standard deviation substituted into it must be in radians. Substituting degrees makes the result 57 times too large.
