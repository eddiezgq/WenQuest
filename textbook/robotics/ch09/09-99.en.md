The main thread of this chapter can be summed up in one sentence: **describe an uncertain quantity by a distribution; the distribution propagates through computations and is updated by measurements.**

We started from the fluctuating readings of a force sensor. A single reading cannot be predicted, but the distribution of a large number of readings is definite: it is described by a probability density, its centre is measured by the expectation and its spread by the variance and the standard deviation. The sum of many small independent disturbances is approximately Gaussian; this is what the central limit theorem tells us, and it is why sensor noise is mostly bell-shaped. The variances of independent random variables add, so the standard deviation of the average of n readings is reduced to $1/\sqrt n$ of its value; the sample variance must be divided by n − 1 to be unbiased. The standard deviation of a uniform distribution is $1/\sqrt{12}$ of the width of the interval, which gives the size of the quantization error of encoders and analogue-to-digital converters.

When several quantities are considered together, the variance generalizes to the covariance matrix, which is symmetric and positive semidefinite; its eigenvectors give the principal directions of the error, the square roots of its eigenvalues give the standard deviations along those directions, and plotting them gives the uncertainty ellipse. Under a linear transformation the covariance matrix propagates as $A\Sigma A^{\mathsf T}$; a nonlinear function is linearized at the mean, with the Jacobian matrix in place of A. The error ellipse of a manipulator's end-effector changes with the configuration, and near a singular configuration it degenerates into a line segment perpendicular to the arm. Results obtained by linearization should be checked by Monte Carlo simulation; when the errors are large, the distribution bends and its mean shifts as well.

Once a measurement has been obtained, Bayes' rule tells us how to revise our knowledge: the posterior is proportional to the likelihood times the prior. When the prior is very small, an alarm need not be believable even if the sensor is very accurate. Motion flattens the distribution and is computed by the law of total probability; measurement sharpens the distribution and is computed by Bayes' rule; alternating the two is the Bayes filter. The product of a Gaussian prior and a Gaussian measurement is again Gaussian: the means are weighted by the reciprocals of the variances, and the reciprocals of the variances add.

The principle of maximum likelihood gives a unified way of constructing estimators. Under Gaussian noise it is weighted least squares, with the inverse of the noise covariance matrix as the weight and $(A^{\mathsf T}WA)^{-1}$ as the covariance matrix of the estimate; even when the noise is not Gaussian, the Gauss–Markov theorem guarantees that it has the smallest variance among linear unbiased estimators. A sum of squares is sensitive to outliers, and robust methods such as the median and the Huber estimator should then be used instead.

Finally, applying "covariance propagation" to prediction and "the Gaussian Bayesian update" to measurement gives the one-dimensional Kalman filter. Under a linear Gaussian model it is an exact Bayesian computation, and it is also a recursive algorithm for weighted least squares; when the model does not change, the gain converges to a steady-state value that depends only on the ratio of the noises. The multidimensional and nonlinear generalizations are left to Chapter 46.

| Concept | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| Random variable | A number that varies with the outcome of a trial; a single value cannot be predicted, but the distribution is definite |
| Probability density | Probability per unit length; the area under the curve is a probability |
| Variance and standard deviation | The expectation of the squared deviation from the mean; the standard deviation has the same units as the readings |
| Gaussian distribution | A bell-shaped distribution completely determined by its mean and variance; the sum of many small independent disturbances approximately follows it |
| Covariance matrix | The diagonal entries are variances, the off-diagonal entries pairwise covariances; the eigenvectors are the principal directions of the error |
| Uncertainty ellipse | The points at equal Mahalanobis distance; in 2D the 1σ ellipse contains only about 39% of the probability |
| Monte Carlo method | Simulate directly with a large number of random trials; used to check the results of linearization |
| Prior, likelihood, posterior | Knowledge before the measurement, the support the data give to each hypothesis, knowledge after the measurement; the posterior is proportional to the likelihood times the prior |
| Conditional independence | Once the common true state is accounted for, the errors are unrelated; the precondition for sequential updating |
| Maximum likelihood estimate | The parameter value that makes the data actually seen most probable |
| Weighted least squares | The maximum likelihood estimate under Gaussian noise; the weight is the inverse of the noise covariance matrix |
| Robust estimation | Estimation that is insensitive to outliers, such as the median, the trimmed mean and the Huber estimator |
| Kalman gain | The fraction of the innovation that is adopted; the less accurate the prediction and the more accurate the measurement, the larger the gain |
| Innovation | The difference between the measured and the predicted value: the "unexpected" part of the measurement |
