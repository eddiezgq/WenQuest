title: Chapter Overview
---

Robots never work in a world that is certain. Sensor readings fluctuate about the true value, encoders can only read discrete angles, wheels slip, and calibrated dimensions have errors too. At every moment the controller has to answer two kinds of question: given these inaccurate data, what is the best estimate, and how far can this estimate be trusted? The first decides what the robot does, the second whether it dares to do it: if the positioning error of the end-effector may exceed the assembly clearance, the part should be aligned before it is inserted; if the position of an AGV is not certain enough, it should slow down or measure once more. This chapter provides the common mathematical tools for answering both kinds of question: probability and estimation.

The chapter starts from scratch and assumes only secondary-school mathematics and the matrix operations of Chapter 2. The main line unfolds as follows:

- Section 9.1 starts from the fluctuating readings of a force sensor, builds up the concepts of random variable, probability density, expectation and variance, derives the Gaussian distribution, and explains why averaging reduces errors and why noise is mostly Gaussian;
- Section 9.2 generalizes the variance to the covariance matrix, studies how errors propagate through linear transformations and Jacobian matrices, describes the size and direction of the end-effector error of a robot arm by an uncertainty ellipse, and checks the result by Monte Carlo simulation;
- Section 9.3 derives Bayes' rule from conditional probability, shows how to revise our knowledge of an unknown state from a measurement, and uses it to localize an AGV in a corridor;
- Section 9.4 starts from the principle of maximum likelihood, shows that under Gaussian noise the maximum likelihood estimate is the least-squares solution and that several sensors should be weighted by the reciprocals of their variances, and introduces robust estimation for dealing with outliers;
- Section 9.5 combines the results of the preceding sections into a one-dimensional Kalman filter, which predicts with odometry and updates with position measurements, in preparation for the state estimation of Chapter 46.

The chapter is related to the chapters before and after it as follows. The least squares, the eigenvalue decomposition and the Jacobian matrix of the planar 2R arm from Chapter 2 are used again and again; the three questions left open in Section 2.5, "why a sum of squares", "how to weight" and "how to deal with bad points", are answered in Section 9.4. Section 9.2 uses the product-of-exponentials forward kinematics of the UR5e from Chapter 12. The results of this chapter in turn underlie many later chapters: the evaluation of measurement uncertainty and sensor calibration in Chapter 39, the Kalman filter and sensor fusion in Chapter 46, the localization of mobile robots in Chapter 47 and robust fitting in visual pose estimation in Chapter 54 all build directly on this chapter. A few proofs in Sections 9.1.5 and 9.2.5 use double integrals and the change to polar coordinates; on a first reading, the conclusions are enough.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. compute the sample mean and the sample standard deviation of a set of readings, judge whether the noise is approximately Gaussian, and estimate the effect of averaging by $\sigma/\sqrt n$;
2. write down the covariance matrix of a random vector, propagate joint-angle errors to the end-effector by $J\Sigma J^{\mathsf T}$, draw the uncertainty ellipse, and check by Monte Carlo simulation whether the linearization is usable;
3. update probability distributions from measurements and motions by Bayes' rule and the law of total probability, understand prior, likelihood and posterior correctly, and avoid the error of "base-rate neglect";
4. write down a likelihood function, fuse several sensors by weighted least squares and give the covariance matrix of the estimate, and choose the median or the Huber estimator when there are outliers;
5. write down the prediction and update formulas of the one-dimensional Kalman filter, explain the meaning of the Kalman gain, compute the steady-state gain, and check the consistency of the filter.
