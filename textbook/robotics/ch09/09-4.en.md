### 9.4.1 The problem: two sensors, which one to trust?

When an AGV returns to its charger, it must stop steadily at a certain distance from the face of the charger and then move up slowly, so that the charging contacts on the vehicle line up with the contacts on the charger. The front of the vehicle carries two range sensors: a laser rangefinder with a standard deviation of 3 mm, and an ultrasonic range sensor, cheap but coarse, with a standard deviation of 10 mm. At a certain moment the laser reads 152 mm and the ultrasonic sensor 143 mm. What should we take as the distance to the face of the charger?

Using only the laser gives 152 mm, and the information from the ultrasonic sensor is simply wasted. Averaging the two gives {{ex9_4.avg:.1f}} mm, but the ultrasonic sensor is much coarser, and putting it on an equal footing with the laser is clearly unreasonable. Intuition tells us to "trust the laser more", but by how much? Can "trust" be given a standard that can be computed and justified?

Problems of this kind are everywhere in robotics: in calibration there are dozens of measurements but only a few parameters (TCP calibration in Section 2.5, sensor calibration in Section 39.5); in localization there are several tags and several sensors but only one position. Section 2.5 handled such overdetermined problems by the method of least squares, and left three questions open: why take the sum of squares; how to weight equations of different accuracy; and what to do when a few bad points are among the data. This section answers these three questions from the point of view of probability.

### 9.4.2 Estimators and how good they are

First let us be clear about what an "estimate" is. The unknown quantity x is a definite number (the true distance to the face of the charger); we simply do not know it. The measured value z is random. A rule that computes an approximate value of x from the measured values is called an estimator.

**Definition 9.4.1 (Estimator, bias and unbiasedness)** A function $\hat x = g(z)$ that computes the unknown quantity x from the measured values z is called an **estimator** of x. Since z is random, $\hat x$ is a random variable too.

$$
b = \mathrm E[\hat x] - x \tag{9.4.1}
$$

is called the bias of the estimator. An estimator with zero bias is called an **unbiased estimator**.

"Use only the laser", "plain average" and "weighted average" are all estimators, and they are all unbiased (the means of the two readings both equal the true distance, so by Theorem 9.1.1 any weighted average of them whose weights add up to 1 is unbiased as well). They differ in variance: the smaller the variance, the more likely a single estimate is to be close to the true value. Bias and variance are measured together by the following quantity.

**Theorem 9.4.1 (Decomposition of the mean squared error)** The **mean squared error** of an estimator equals its variance plus the square of its bias:

$$
\mathrm E\big[(\hat x - x)^2\big] = \mathrm{Var}(\hat x) + b^2 . \tag{9.4.2}
$$

*Proof*　Write $m = \mathrm E[\hat x]$; then $\hat x - x = (\hat x - m) + (m - x)$. Squaring and taking expectations, the cross term is $2(m - x)\,\mathrm E[\hat x - m] = 0$, the first term is the variance and the second is the constant $b^2$.　∎

We met bias already in Section 9.1.6: an estimate of the variance that divides by n is biased, and one that divides by n − 1 is unbiased. Eq. (9.4.2) shows that both bias and variance contribute to the error. This section deals mainly with unbiased estimators, and among unbiased estimators, the smaller the variance the better.

### 9.4.3 The likelihood function and maximum likelihood estimation

How can a good estimator be constructed systematically? Early in the 20th century, the British statistician Ronald A. Fisher proposed a principle of general applicability. He put forward the idea in his first paper in 1912, began to use the word "likelihood" in 1921, and established a systematic theory in "On the mathematical foundations of theoretical statistics" in 1922.

The idea is this. Section 9.3 said that the likelihood p(z | x) is "how likely we are to see these data if the unknown is x". Now the data are in hand, and we regard p(z | x) as a function of x: for each candidate x we ask, "if this were the true value, how likely would we be to see the data we have seen?" The most reasonable estimate is the x that makes what has already happened most likely to happen.

**Definition 9.4.2 (Likelihood function and maximum likelihood estimate)** Let the measured values $z_1, \ldots, z_n$ be mutually independent given x, with densities $p(z_i \mid x)$. The function

$$
L(x) = p(z_1, \ldots, z_n \mid x) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(z_i \mid x) \tag{9.4.3}
$$

is called the **likelihood function**. The x that maximizes L(x) is called the **maximum likelihood estimate** of x and is written $\hat x_{\mathrm{ML}}$.

A product of many densities is awkward to differentiate, and it easily underflows to zero in a computer. The logarithm is monotonically increasing, so maximizing L is the same as maximizing ln L, and the logarithm turns the product into a sum:

$$
\hat x_{\mathrm{ML}} = \arg\max_x L(x) = \arg\max_x \sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln p(z_i \mid x) . \tag{9.4.4}
$$

arg max means "the argument at which the function that follows takes its maximum".

**Example 9.4.1** The ultrasonic sensor reads the same distance 5 times: {{ex9_4.zs}} (mm). The noise is Gaussian, with a known standard deviation of 10 mm. Find the maximum likelihood estimate of the true distance μ.

The density of each reading is $\mathcal N(z_i; \mu, \sigma^2)$; substituting into Eq. (9.4.4):

$$
\ln L(\mu) = -\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(z_i - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} - n\ln\big(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\big) . \tag{9.4.5}
$$

The second term does not depend on μ. The first term is a downward-opening parabola; differentiating with respect to μ and setting the derivative to zero gives $\sum_i (z_i - \mu)/\sigma^2 = 0$, so

$$
\hat\mu_{\mathrm{ML}} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n} z_i = \bar z . \tag{9.4.6}
$$

That is, the sample mean, {{ex9_4.zbar:.0f}} mm. Figure 9.4.1 shows what this means. In the upper panel, when the candidate value μ is 135 mm, all 5 readings lie on the edge of the bell curve, where every density is small and their product smaller still; when μ is the sample mean, the readings all lie in the middle part of the curve, and the product is largest. The lower panel shows how the product L(μ) varies with μ; it is itself a bell curve, with its peak at $\bar z$. Program 9.4.1 searches point by point for the maximum of ln L between 120 mm and 180 mm, and the result agrees with Eq. (9.4.6).

If σ is also unknown, the maximum can be sought over μ and σ together. Differentiating with respect to σ and setting the derivative to zero gives $\hat\sigma^2_{\mathrm{ML}} = \frac1n\sum_i (z_i - \bar z)^2$, with denominator n rather than n − 1. For Example 9.4.1, $\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{ML}}$ = {{ex9_4.sig_ml:.2f}} mm, whereas s from Eq. (9.1.16) is {{ex9_4.s_unb:.2f}} mm. By Theorem 9.1.5, the maximum likelihood estimate is biased here. This shows that the maximum likelihood principle does not guarantee unbiasedness; but the bias tends to zero as the number of measurements grows, and, under rather general conditions, for large n the maximum likelihood estimate is approximately unbiased and has the smallest variance. This is the theoretical justification for its wide use.

### 9.4.4 Under Gaussian noise, maximum likelihood is least squares

Eq. (9.4.5) already gives a hint: the log-likelihood under Gaussian noise is the negative of the sum of squared residuals (times a constant). Maximizing the likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the sum of squared residuals. We now extend this to general linear measurements.

Let the unknown be a vector x (for example the zero point and sensitivity in a calibration), and arrange m measured values into a vector z that depends linearly on x and carries Gaussian noise:

$$
z = Ax + v , \qquad v \sim \mathcal N(0, \Sigma_v) . \tag{9.4.7}
$$

A is a known m×n matrix (m > n), and $\Sigma_v$ is the covariance matrix of the noise. When the measurements are independent, $\Sigma_v = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \ldots, \sigma_m^2)$.

**Theorem 9.4.2 (Maximum likelihood estimate under Gaussian noise)** Under the model (9.4.7), let the columns of A be linearly independent. The maximum likelihood estimate of x is

$$
\hat x = (A^{\mathsf T}WA)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}W z , \qquad W = \Sigma_v^{-1} , \tag{9.4.8}
$$

it is unbiased, and its covariance matrix is

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(\hat x) = (A^{\mathsf T}WA)^{-1} . \tag{9.4.9}
$$

*Proof*　Given x, z ~ 𝒩(Ax, Σ_v). By Eq. (9.2.7),

$$
-\ln L(x) = \frac12 (z - Ax)^{\mathsf T}W(z - Ax) + \text{const} . \tag{9.4.10}
$$

So maximizing L is equivalent to minimizing the weighted sum of squared residuals $J(x) = (z - Ax)^{\mathsf T}W(z - Ax)$. W is a symmetric positive definite matrix and can be written $W = C^{\mathsf T}C$ (for example $C = \mathrm{diag}(1/\sigma_i)$ when the measurements are independent). With $\tilde A = CA$ and $\tilde z = Cz$, $J = \|\tilde z - \tilde Ax\|^2$, an ordinary least-squares problem. By the normal equations (2.5.4) of Section 2.5, $\tilde A^{\mathsf T}\tilde A\hat x = \tilde A^{\mathsf T}\tilde z$, that is, $A^{\mathsf T}WA\,\hat x = A^{\mathsf T}Wz$, which gives Eq. (9.4.8).

Unbiasedness: $\hat x = Lz$, where $L = (A^{\mathsf T}WA)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}W$ satisfies LA = I, so $\mathrm E[\hat x] = LAx + L\,\mathrm E[v] = x$. Covariance: by Theorem 9.2.3,

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(\hat x) = L\,\Sigma_v\,L^{\mathsf T} = (A^{\mathsf T}WA)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}W\,W^{-1}\,WA\,(A^{\mathsf T}WA)^{-1} = (A^{\mathsf T}WA)^{-1} .　∎
$$

Eq. (9.4.8) is called **weighted least squares**. "Weighting" means multiplying each equation by $1/\sigma_i$ and then doing ordinary least squares: equations of high accuracy are scaled up and those of low accuracy scaled down, and equations in different units are thereby brought to the same scale (each in units of its own standard deviation). When all measurements are equally accurate, $W = I/\sigma^2$, Eq. (9.4.8) is the ordinary least-squares solution of Section 2.5, and Eq. (9.4.9) reduces to $\sigma^2(A^{\mathsf T}A)^{-1}$. This answers the first question of Section 2.5: when the measurement errors are independent and Gaussian with equal variances, the least-squares solution is the maximum likelihood estimate.

Eq. (9.4.9) is a very useful by-product: together with the estimate, it gives the uncertainty of the estimate, and this depends only on A and the noise level, not on the measured values. So before doing an experiment, we can already use it to assess whether a measurement plan is good (for example, how the orientations should be chosen for the TCP calibration in the exercises of Section 2.5).

**Example 9.4.2** Back to the problem of Section 9.4.1: the laser reads $z_1$ = 152 mm (σ₁ = 3 mm), and the ultrasonic sensor $z_2$ = 143 mm (σ₂ = 10 mm). Find the maximum likelihood estimate of the distance to the face of the charger and its standard deviation, and compare with using only the laser and with the plain average.

Both sensors measure the same quantity, so $A = (1, 1)^{\mathsf T}$ and $W = \mathrm{diag}(1/\sigma_1^2, 1/\sigma_2^2)$. Substituting into Eqs. (9.4.8) and (9.4.9):

$$
\hat x = \frac{z_1/\sigma_1^2 + z_2/\sigma_2^2}{1/\sigma_1^2 + 1/\sigma_2^2} , \qquad \frac{1}{\sigma_{\hat x}^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma_1^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_2^2} . \tag{9.4.11}
$$

The weight of the laser is (1/9)/(1/9 + 1/100) ≈ {{ex9_4.w1:.4f}}, and that of the ultrasonic sensor {{ex9_4.w2:.4f}}. $\hat x$ ≈ {{ex9_4.xhat:.2f}} mm, with standard deviation $\sigma_{\hat x}$ ≈ {{ex9_4.sig_hat:.3f}} mm.

Eq. (9.4.11) has exactly the same form as Eqs. (9.3.9) and (9.3.10) of Section 9.3. This is no coincidence: Section 9.3 treated the odometry prior as "one measurement", combined with the lidar measurement by the same rule. The weights in the two examples are also exactly the same, because in both the ratio of the two standard deviations is 10 : 3.

Compare the different approaches (Figure 9.4.2): the ultrasonic sensor alone has a standard deviation of 10 mm; the laser alone, 3 mm; the plain average, by Theorem 9.1.2 and Eq. (9.1.18), has a standard deviation of $\sqrt{3^2 + 10^2}/2$ ≈ {{ex9_4.sig_avg:.2f}} mm, much worse than the laser alone; the weighted average has {{ex9_4.sig_hat:.2f}} mm, slightly better than the laser alone. Program 9.4.1 checks this with {{ex9_4.M:,}} simulations: the sample standard deviations of the three methods are {{ex9_4.mc_l:.3f}} mm, {{ex9_4.mc_avg:.3f}} mm and {{ex9_4.mc_w:.3f}} mm respectively.

This example teaches two engineering lessons. First, "averaging" a good sensor with a poor one can be worse than using the good one alone; fusion must be weighted by accuracy. Second, weighted fusion is always better than a single sensor, but when the two accuracies differ greatly, the gain is limited: here it improves only from 3 mm to {{ex9_4.sig_hat:.2f}} mm. The main value of adding a coarse sensor often lies not in better accuracy but in redundancy: when the laser is blocked or fails, the ultrasonic sensor still provides a rough reading, and it can be used to detect anomalies in the laser (when the difference between the two far exceeds their uncertainties, one of them must have gone wrong).

### 9.4.5 The Gauss–Markov theorem

Weighted least squares is the maximum likelihood estimate under Gaussian noise. What if the noise is not Gaussian? The following theorem shows that even if the distribution of the noise is unknown, as long as its covariance matrix is known, weighted least squares is still the best within a large class of estimators. This is the result of Gauss mentioned in Section 2.5.

**Theorem 9.4.3 (Gauss–Markov theorem)** In the model z = Ax + v, let E[v] = 0, let $\mathrm{Cov}(v) = \Sigma_v$ be positive definite, and let the columns of A be linearly independent; v need not be Gaussian. Then among all linear unbiased estimators of the form $\hat x = Lz$, the weighted least-squares estimate (9.4.8) has the smallest covariance matrix: for any linear unbiased estimator $Lz$,

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(Lz) - (A^{\mathsf T}WA)^{-1} \tag{9.4.12}
$$

is positive semidefinite.

*Proof*　Lz is unbiased, that is, E[Lz] = LAx = x for every x, so LA = I. Write $L_0 = (A^{\mathsf T}WA)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}W$ and $D = L - L_0$; then $DA = LA - L_0A = I - I = 0$. By Theorem 9.2.3,

$$
\mathrm{Cov}(Lz) = (L_0 + D)\Sigma_v(L_0 + D)^{\mathsf T} = L_0\Sigma_vL_0^{\mathsf T} + L_0\Sigma_vD^{\mathsf T} + D\Sigma_vL_0^{\mathsf T} + D\Sigma_vD^{\mathsf T} .
$$

The cross term $L_0\Sigma_vD^{\mathsf T} = (A^{\mathsf T}WA)^{-1}A^{\mathsf T}W\Sigma_vD^{\mathsf T} = (A^{\mathsf T}WA)^{-1}(DA)^{\mathsf T} = 0$, and the other cross term, its transpose, is zero too. By the proof of Theorem 9.4.2, the first term is $(A^{\mathsf T}WA)^{-1}$. So $\mathrm{Cov}(Lz) - (A^{\mathsf T}WA)^{-1} = D\Sigma_vD^{\mathsf T}$, which is positive semidefinite (for any a, $a^{\mathsf T}D\Sigma_vD^{\mathsf T}a = (D^{\mathsf T}a)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_v(D^{\mathsf T}a) \geq 0$).　∎

Comparing the "size" of covariance matrices by positive semidefiniteness means this: in any direction a, the variance along a, $a^{\mathsf T}\mathrm{Cov}\,a$, is no smaller than that of weighted least squares. In particular, no component has a smaller variance than the corresponding component of the weighted least-squares estimate. In Example 9.4.3 the variances of ordinary least squares are larger than those of weighted least squares, which is just one instance of this theorem; Program 9.4.1 checks that the eigenvalues of the difference of the two covariance matrices are all non-negative.

This theorem is usually called the **Gauss–Markov theorem**. Gauss proved it for uncorrelated errors in his *Theoria combinationis observationum erroribus minimis obnoxiae* (Theory of the combination of observations least subject to errors) of 1821–1823 (the observations may have different accuracies, handled by weights), no longer relying on the Gaussian error distribution he had assumed in 1809; Andrey Markov restated it in his textbook of probability theory in 1900, which is where the name comes from; and Alexander C. Aitken extended it in 1935 to the general case of correlated errors, that is, to Eq. (9.4.8) with a non-diagonal $\Sigma_v$. Such a "best linear unbiased estimator" is often abbreviated BLUE.

The limits of the theorem must be seen just as clearly: it compares only "linear" estimators. If the noise has very long tails (very large errors now and then), some nonlinear estimators can be far better than least squares. That is the situation we turn to next.

### 9.4.6 Outliers and robust estimation

Ultrasonic sensors have a typical failure: the sound wave is reflected once off a corner or an obstacle before it returns to the receiver, and the measured distance is much longer than the real one. This is called a multipath echo.

**Example 9.4.4** An ultrasonic sensor takes 9 successive readings of the charger face, which is 150 mm away: {{ex9_4.zr}} (mm). The last reading is a multipath echo. Compare the mean, the median and the Huber estimate.

The mean is {{ex9_4.mean_r:.1f}} mm; a single bad point has pulled it off by nearly 10 mm, far more than the noise of the sensor itself (about 2 mm). With the bad point removed, the mean of the other 8 is {{ex9_4.mean_clean:.1f}} mm. A reading that departs so clearly from the rest of the data is called an **outlier**.

The sum of squares is especially sensitive to outliers: the square of the 86 mm residual is 7,396, more than two hundred times the sum of the squares of the other 8 residuals, and least squares, to reduce this one term, is willing to move away from all the other points. From the point of view of probability, the reason is that the tails of the Gaussian distribution are very "thin": the probability of a deviation of more than 6σ is less than two in a billion, so in the eyes of the Gaussian model a reading more than 40σ away is all but impossible, and the estimate can only move towards it to "accommodate" it.

One remedy is to switch to a noise model with heavier tails. If the noise follows the Laplace distribution, whose density is proportional to $e^{-|r|/b}$, the log-likelihood is the negative of the sum of the absolute values of the residuals, and the maximum likelihood estimate minimizes $\sum_i |z_i - \mu|$; it is the **median** (the middle value of the sorted data; for an even number of values, the average of the two middle ones). The reason is this: each time μ moves a little to the right, every point to its left increases the sum, and every point to its right decreases it, by the same amount; only when there are equally many points on the left and on the right does the sum stop decreasing. The median is extremely insensitive to outliers: change 236 mm to 2,360 mm and the median is still {{ex9_4.med_r:.0f}} mm. The idea of fitting data by the sum of absolute values is even older than least squares; Roger Joseph Boscovich used it in 1757 and Laplace at the end of the 18th century, but the absolute value is not differentiable, which made computation inconvenient, and it was later displaced by least squares.

The price of the median is that, when the noise really is Gaussian, its variance is about 57% larger than that of the mean. Can we have both? In 1964 the Swiss statistician Peter J. Huber proposed counting small residuals by their squares and large residuals by their absolute values.

**Definition 9.4.3 (M-estimator and Huber function)** Choose a loss function ρ; the μ that minimizes $\sum_i \rho\big((z_i - \mu)/\sigma\big)$ is called an M-estimator ("M" standing for "maximum likelihood type"). The Huber function is

$$
\rho(r) = \begin{cases} r^2/2 , & |r| \leq k , \\ k|r| - k^2/2 , & |r| > k , \end{cases} \tag{9.4.13}
$$

with k commonly taken as {{ex9_4.k_h:.3f}}; then, under purely Gaussian noise, the variance of the estimate is only 5% larger than that of the mean.

**Robust estimation** refers in general to estimation methods that are insensitive to outliers, and the Huber estimate is the most basic of them. Figure 9.4.4 (right) shows the three loss functions: the quadratic function rises steeply on both sides, so a single large residual costs a great deal; the absolute value function consists of two straight lines; the Huber function is a parabola in the middle joined to straight lines on both sides.

The Huber estimate has no explicit formula; it is usually computed by iteratively reweighted least squares. Let ψ = ρ′; the condition for the minimum is $\sum_i \psi(r_i) = 0$. Write it as $\sum_i w_i r_i = 0$ with $w_i = \psi(r_i)/r_i$: when the residual is within ±k, $w_i = 1$, and outside it $w_i = k/|r_i| < 1$. Hence

$$
\mu = \frac{\sum_i w_i z_i}{\sum_i w_i} , \qquad w_i = \min\Big(1,\ \frac{k}{|r_i|}\Big) . \tag{9.4.14}
$$

Starting from the median, compute the residuals and weights from the current μ, update μ by Eq. (9.4.14), and repeat until convergence. Every step is a weighted least-squares problem, hence the name "iteratively reweighted". For Example 9.4.4, with σ = 2 mm and threshold kσ = {{ex9_4.c:.2f}} mm, the Huber estimate after convergence is {{ex9_4.hub:.2f}} mm, and the weight of the outlier is only {{ex9_4.w_out:.3f}}. Program 9.4.1 checks that the converged result satisfies $\sum_i\psi(r_i) = 0$.

When there are many outliers, perhaps more than half of the data, the methods above also fail. Then one commonly uses the random sample consensus algorithm (RANSAC) proposed by Martin A. Fischler and Robert C. Bolles in 1981: repeatedly draw at random the minimum number of points needed to fit the model, count how many points agree with it, and keep the model with which the most points agree. It will be used for visual pose estimation in Chapter 54.

### 9.4.7 Everyday examples

In diving and gymnastics competitions, it is common to "drop the highest and the lowest score and average the rest". This is a robust estimate called the trimmed mean: the bias or mistake of an individual judge has almost no effect on the result, and when all the judges are fair it loses very little accuracy compared with the mean of all scores.

Suppose you have two thermometers at home: an electronic thermometer accurate to 0.1 ℃, and a wall thermometer graduated only in 1 ℃. When measuring the room temperature, nobody would simply average the two readings; you would rely mainly on the electronic thermometer. But if the two differed by 5 ℃, you would suspect that one of them was broken. This is exactly the idea of the weighting in Eq. (9.4.11) and of "using redundancy to detect faults".

So far, the quantities being estimated have all been constant. An AGV measures while it travels; its position keeps changing, old measurements "expire" with time, and new measurements keep arriving. The next section combines the tools of this chapter to deal with this kind of estimation problem, in which the quantity changes with time.

**Engineer's notes**

- When fusing several sensors, make the weights inversely proportional to the variances. Measure the standard deviation of each sensor first, with the system at rest, rather than using the nominal value from the datasheet: the mounting, the cables and interference from the motors all change the actual noise.
- Always report the result of weighted least squares together with its covariance matrix from Eq. (9.4.9). A large correlation coefficient in the covariance matrix (such as the correlation between zero point and sensitivity in Example 9.4.3) indicates that the experimental design can be improved, for example by placing more calibration points at the two ends of the range.
- After fitting, check the residuals divided by their respective σ: most of them should lie within ±2. If they are large across the board, the noise has been underestimated or the model is wrong; if a few are particularly large, first check whether they are outliers.
- Outlier rejection in an automated program must have a threshold and must leave a record. Common thresholds are a residual larger than 3σ or a Mahalanobis distance larger than a certain value (Section 9.2); if data are deleted without a record, problems cannot be traced later.

**Exercises**

9.4.1 Three independent range sensors measure the same distance, with standard deviations of 2 mm, 4 mm and 4 mm and readings of 100 mm, 104 mm and 98 mm. Find the maximum likelihood estimate and its standard deviation.

9.4.2 A measurement follows the uniform distribution U(0, θ), with θ unknown. It is read n times, and the largest reading is $z_{\max}$. Prove that the maximum likelihood estimate of θ is $z_{\max}$, and show that it is biased (too small).

9.4.3 In Example 9.4.3, if the six calibration points are changed to 0, 0, 0, 100, 100, 100 N (three measurements at each end), compute the standard deviations of a and b with Eq. (9.4.9) (the noise standard deviation is still 0.05 + 0.002F mV), compare with the original plan, and explain why.

9.4.4 Prove that the median of the data $z_1, \ldots, z_n$ minimizes $\sum_i |z_i - \mu|$. When n is even, is the minimizer unique?

9.4.5 Implement the iteratively reweighted algorithm for the Huber estimate and verify it with the data of Example 9.4.4. Change the outlier from 236 mm to 160, 170, 200 and 500 mm in turn, compare how the mean, the median and the Huber estimate change, and explain the role of the threshold k.
