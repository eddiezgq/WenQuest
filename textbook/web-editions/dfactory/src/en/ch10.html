Over the last few lectures the plant stopped machines, paid overtime, switched suppliers and serviced the grinder for the robot plant’s rush order. The goods have finally gone out and the invoice has been issued. This lecture asks two questions: how much did the order actually make, and when will the money be back in the bank? The first is a question about gross margin; the second is about receivables and cash. The two answers often disagree: the books can show a profit while the bank account runs dry. Every number in this chapter follows the rules of the “Business and finance” page of the WenQuest digital factory, so your hand calculation and the platform use the same rules and formulas.

10.2 Revenue and cost: material, labour, overheads

An order’s revenue is price × quantity. Cost comes in three big blocks, which plants often call material, labour and overheads:

  • Material: the quantity of each part on the BOM × its unit price. For a WQR-105 the housing castings, gear forgings, steel bar, bearings, seals, bolts, oil and so on add up to $538.74 per unit.
  • Labour: the time of each operation × the workstation rate. The rate holds depreciation and wages; grinder GRD-01, for example, costs $20 an hour in depreciation and $26 in wages. Adding all the operations of a WQR-105, from sawing to packing, for every part, sub-assembly and the final assembly, gives $571.80 per unit. The digital factory books depreciation and wages together as “labour” (a conversion cost, machine plus people); in accounting, depreciation and energy belong to manufacturing overhead. This chapter follows the digital factory’s convention.
  • Overheads: here mainly energy (the energy part of the workstation rate, $6 an hour on GRD-01), $73.87 per unit.

There is also a class of unplanned money: scrap, rework, overtime, outsourcing, warranty and so on. It is not in the standard cost; Section 10.3 prices it line by line.

Cost worked out from standard quantities and standard times is the standard cost: $538.74 + $571.80 + $73.87 = $1,184.41 per unit. Sold at $1,650, each unit earns a gross margin of $465.59, a standard margin of 28.2%. The plant’s business objective is a gross margin of at least 25%.

Gross margin is not profit. It only subtracts the money spent directly on making the goods. Management salaries, selling costs, interest and tax are still to come. The margin must cover them before the plant makes a profit, which is why it has a target line and why a low margin is investigated.

10.3 Breaking down an order’s margin

Standard cost is what the order should cost; what it actually costs is often more. The digital factory splits everything spent on an order into seven lines, and each one can be opened to see where it came from:

\[\text{margin}=\text{revenue}-\text{material}-\text{labour}-\text{energy}-\text{scrap}-\text{idle power}-\text{rush, overtime, outsourcing}-\text{warranty},\qquad \text{margin \%}=\frac{\text{margin}}{\text{revenue}}\]

Three different hourly prices appear here; keep them apart. Labour in the standard cost is spread over each unit by the workstation rate ($26/h of wages on GRD-01); overtime is priced at the operator wage, $30 × 1.5; the service in Lecture 6 used the maintenance technician’s $45/h. Lecture 6 also put the grinder’s downtime at $200 of lost margin an hour. That is the platform’s setting for downtime loss: at standard margin one grinder hour covers the shafts of 1.54 units, worth about $716, but the setting is lower because lost time is partly made up later through overtime and buffers.

Figure 10-1 shows three things:

  • Material and labour are the big blocks. Labour is 35.2% of revenue and material 32.7%. The biggest line apart from material is labour: to earn more, look at operation times and workstation rates first.
  • The unplanned money adds up to $898.54 and pulls the margin from the standard 28.2% down to 25.5%, 2.7 percentage points lower, just above the 25% target. Overtime, at $540, is the largest part.
  • Some events look alarming but cost little. Twenty-four idle hours on the band saw cost $4.80; the $215 after-shift service of Lecture 6 is booked to the maintenance budget and is not allocated to orders. Judge money by the numbers, not by impressions.

If a finished order’s margin is more than 5 points below target (under 20%), the factory hub raises “order margin below target” and lists the largest lines. Who fixes a low margin? A low price goes to sales, dear materials to purchasing, excess hours to process engineering and the shop floor.

10.4 Accounts receivable and ageing

Once goods are delivered and invoiced but not yet paid for, the money is an account receivable. It belongs to the plant but is still in the customer’s pocket. Managing receivables starts with ageing: sorting them by how many days they are overdue.

“Usually pays late by” is each customer’s payment habit in the digital factory’s teaching simulator. The longer a receivable is overdue, the harder it is to collect; many plants set aside a bad-debt provision for anything over 60 days. Look at credit too: the robot plant owes $33,000, already above its $30,000 credit limit, and counting its next batch of 10 ($16,500, in production and not yet invoiced), its exposure is already $49,500, 165% of the limit. The digital factory counts open, uninvoiced orders in credit exposure, so the hub is already raising a second exception, “customer over credit limit”. Leave it alone and the debt keeps growing.

10.5 Days sales outstanding

Whether receivables are high depends on how much the plant sells. The usual measure is days sales outstanding (DSO): how many days of sales the current receivables represent. The digital factory calculates it as

\[\text{DSO}=\frac{\text{receivables outstanding}}{\text{invoiced in the last 30 days}\div 30}\]

The only invoices issued in the last 30 days are GreenValley’s $9,900 and Harbor Crane’s $13,200, $23,100 in all, or $770 a day. DSO = $62,700 ÷ $770 = 81.4 days, almost twice the 45-day target. The robot plant’s invoice is 52.6% of all receivables: once it is paid, $29,700 is left and DSO falls to 38.6 days, back within target.

DSO also converts into money: with $770 invoiced a day, every day off the DSO brings $770 back from customers’ pockets into the plant’s account. Cutting DSO from 45 days to 30 frees 15 × $770 = $11,550 of cash. Note that the denominator only covers the last 30 days of invoicing: when invoicing is uneven (a big invoice issued just over 30 days ago, say), DSO swings sharply, so watch its trend over several weeks.

10.6 Cash forecasting: profit is not cash

Margin answers “are we making money?”; cash answers “can we pay the bills?”. Wages, rent and suppliers have to be paid in cash, not in receivables. The “Receivables and payables” page of the digital factory forecasts cash flow over the next 8 weeks:

\[C_k=C_{k-1}+\text{cash in in week }k-\text{cash out in week }k-\text{fixed costs per week},\qquad C_0=\text{cash now}\]

Week k runs from day 7(k−1) to day 7k−1. Each receivable is expected on its due date plus the customer’s usual delay (anything already overdue falls in week 1); each supplier invoice is paid on its due date. Cash must stay above zero every week, preferably with a week of fixed costs to spare (this chapter uses $8,000 as the safety line).

Three remarks. The next three material batches are for new orders, and their sales come back only after 30-day terms plus late payment, beyond 8 weeks, so only the material payments appear. The weekly fixed costs are wages, rent and so on paid in cash; the “labour” in the margin table is the same wages allocated to orders by hours: one sum of money seen two ways, not to be counted twice. Depreciation is in the margin but costs no cash, so it is not in the cash forecast.

First, do nothing: the robot plant will not pay unless chased, so its $33,000 does not arrive within 8 weeks.

Yet the order clearly made $8,413. That is what people mean by “a profitable factory can still go bankrupt”: the materials and wages have been paid out, while the sales money is stuck with the customer. The longer the money is on the road, from paying for materials to collecting from customers, the more cash the plant has to advance; and the more orders it wins and the faster it grows, the more it has to advance. Many plants do not die of losses; they die when the cash runs out.

10.7 Three options: dunning, credit freeze, do nothing

For a “receivable overdue” exception the factory hub offers three options, each with the expected days until the money arrives and the lowest week of cash in the next 8:

Table 10-5 Three options for the robot plant’s receivable
OptionWhat it doesDays until $33,000 arrivesLowest cash in 8 weeksPrice
A Do nothing (control)Wait for the customernot within 8 weeksweek 8, −$6,624below the safety line in week 7, out of cash in week 8
B Send a dunning letterSales initiates; the plant manager or sales lead approvesabout 3week 8, $26,376almost none; the relationship stays friendly
C Credit freezeDun and freeze: its orders in production are not shipped or invoiced until the plant manager releases themabout 3week 8, $26,376 (the next batch’s delayed invoice and receipt not included)the next batch of 10 is held; the robot plant’s line may wait for parts; the relationship suffers

For the overdue invoice itself, B and C are the same: in the digital factory a customer who receives a dunning letter usually pays within 3 days. A freeze also delays the invoicing and collection of the next batch ($16,500): it is invoiced only when the plant manager releases it, and the 30-day terms restart from then. The 8-week forecast leaves this out because the batch has not been invoiced yet. What C adds is damage control: no new goods go out, so the debt stops growing. Its price is the relationship, and possibly the customer’s own production.

The hub has two rules here, and they disagree. “Receivable overdue” looks only at days overdue: 30 days or less, dun; more than 30, freeze. The robot plant is 15 days overdue, so this rule recommends B. “Customer over credit limit” looks at exposure: over the limit, freeze. The robot plant is at 165%, so this exception is raised too and recommends C. When two recommendations clash, a person has to weigh them. This chapter chooses dun first, and freeze if it is still unpaid before the next batch is invoiced: the invoice is not long overdue, so leave room for the relationship; the moment before the next invoice is the last chance to stop the debt growing.

Two points to note:

  • Doing nothing is not free. Its price is not in the margin table but in the cash table: in week 8 the account is negative, so the plant either borrows and pays interest or delays its suppliers and hurts its scorecard.
  • Sometimes it is better not to chase. The customer may have run into trouble but always kept its word and keeps ordering; or the sum may be small and chasing it may lose a big order. Then phone first, agree a payment date and put it into the cash forecast. A decision must weigh money and the relationship together.

10.9 Robot link and an everyday example

Robots: why the robot plant pays late too. The robot plant sells collaborative robots to system integrators, who build welding or palletising cells for end users. This business is usually paid by milestones. Take a $120,000 cell (illustrative): 30% on signing ($36,000), 60% after site acceptance ($72,000) and a 10% retention ($12,000) paid a year later. If the end user’s acceptance slips by two months, $72,000 arrives two months late; the integrator, short of cash, pays the robot plant late; and the robot plant, short of cash, pays us late. Cash shortages travel up the chain link by link. So when we set the robot plant’s credit limit and terms, we should look at how its own customers pay, not just at its reputation. We can also negotiate the other way: a down payment, or delivering and invoicing in smaller batches so that money comes back sooner.

Everyday example: a small shop that lets regulars buy on credit. A shop sells $3,000 a month at a 30% margin; rent and wages are $600 a month, so on paper it makes $300 a month. But half the goods go on credit to regular customers, who pay two months later, while the shop pays cash for its stock and opened with only $1,000. In month 1 its cash is already −$200; in month 2 it is $1,400 short and must borrow or run out of stock; only in month 3, when the regulars start paying, does it slowly recover. Profitable on paper, broke in the till: that is the difference between making money and having money. The shop’s DSO is 30 days: two months of credit sales, $3,000, divided by $100 of sales a day. The regulars take 60 days to pay, yet DSO is only 30 days, because half the sales are for cash.

10.10 Back to the problem

The robot plant’s order: revenue $33,000, cost $24,586.77, gross margin $8,413.23, or 25.5%, 2.7 points below the standard 28.2%, mainly because of $540 of overtime, $184 of grinder re-setting and $106 of scrap; it just holds the 25% target. Receivables now stand at $62,700, a DSO of 81.4 days, far above the 45-day target. Doing nothing leaves cash at −$6,624 in week 8. Send a dunning letter: the $33,000 comes back in about 3 days, the lowest cash in the next 8 weeks is still $26,376, and DSO falls to 38.6 days. “Receivable overdue” recommends dunning (only 15 days); “over credit limit” recommends a freeze (165% exposure). Weighing the two, dun first; if it is still unpaid when the next batch is ready to invoice, or once it is more than 30 days overdue, ask the plant manager to freeze the account. Next time the robot plant orders, negotiate a down payment or batch invoicing, and build the cost of overtime and alternative suppliers into the quote.

Chapter summary

  • Gross margin = revenue − material − labour − energy − scrap and rework − idle power − rush, overtime and outsourcing − warranty; cost from standard quantities and times is the standard cost, and the standard margin of a WQR-105 is 28.2%.
  • The margin breakdown shows where the money went and who should fix it: pricing goes to sales, materials to purchasing, hours to process engineering and the shop floor; judge by the numbers, not by impressions.
  • Receivables are read by ageing (not due, 1–30, 31–60, over 60 days overdue) and credit exposure; DSO = receivables outstanding ÷ average daily invoicing over the last 30 days.
  • Cash forecast: cash this week = cash last week + cash in − cash out − fixed costs; the lowest week matters most. Profit is not cash, and a profitable factory can still go under when its cash runs out.
  • An overdue receivable is judged by days overdue: within 30 days dun first, beyond 30 freeze; an over-limit customer gets a freeze recommendation. When the two disagree, a person weighs money and the relationship together.
