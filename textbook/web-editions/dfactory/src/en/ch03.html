Something happens in a plant every day: a machine stops, a material runs short, a dimension drifts, an order is going to be late. The hard part is not knowing that something is wrong; it is answering three questions at once: why did it happen, who else does it hit, and what is the cheapest fix? In the WenQuest digital factory these questions are answered by the factory hub. Rules find exceptions in the data, a causal chain traces up to the cause and down to the impact, and a what-if simulation fast-forwards each option to price its money, delivery and risk. This chapter walks through the whole method with one grinder breakdown. You will see that estimates made from experience are often wrong, and not by a little.

3.1 The problem: the grinder stopped. Which order will be late?

At the start of term the robot plant ordered 20 extra WQR-105 joint reducers, due on Thursday (the customer, quantity and due date are this lecture’s illustrative setting). Their shafts are being machined. At 10:00 on Monday, cylindrical grinder GRD-01 suddenly stops: a spindle fault, waiting for a spare part, with the repair estimated at 24 hours. GRD-01 is the plant's bottleneck. There is only one, and no other machine can grind for it.

Table 3-1 Work queued for the grinder at the breakdown (digital-factory teaching scenario, in order)
OrderUnitsGrinding timeDueContract penalty
Summit Mixers88 × 39 min = 5.2 hWednesday$200 per working day
Robot plant (rush, this lecture’s setting)2020 × 39 min = 13 hThursday (illustrative)$200 per working day (illustrative)
Harbor Crane1212 × 39 min = 7.8 hin 12 working daysnone

Why 39 minutes per unit? Each WQR-105 has three shafts that go through the grinder: pinion shaft SH-101 takes 15 minutes, intermediate shaft SH-201 and output shaft SH-301 take 12 each, 39 in all. These figures come from the digital factory's routings. The shop works one shift, Monday to Friday 08:00–17:00, 9 hours a day. After grinding, assembly, run-in and final inspection take one more working day before the order ships.

The shop supervisor's first reaction: “Down 24 hours, that's about three shifts. The robot order will be three days late, $600 in penalties. Get maintenance to expedite!” This chapter answers: Which order will this breakdown actually delay, by how many days, at what penalty? Which is cheapest: overtime, expediting the repair or outsourcing? Was the supervisor right?

3.2 Rules: finding exceptions in the data

How does the hub know something is wrong? Through rules. A rule is a judgement written as a program: “if the data meet this condition, raise an exception.” In the digital factory the rules check the whole plant every 20 seconds. After this breakdown several rules fire:

Table 3-2 Rules fired by one breakdown (the rule catalogue is in the digital factory's ontology/rules/)
RuleConditionWhat it reports this time
P1 Machine down / faultmachine state is down or faultGRD-01 down, about 1,440 minutes to go
P5 WIP backlogmore than 6 parts queued at a bottleneck120 shafts queued at the grinder
S1 Order may be latedashboard forecast later than due daterobot plant's order forecast late
R3 Delay triggers a penaltyforecast late and the contract has a penalty; critical from $500the expected penalty

Rules have three strengths: they don't forget, don't tire and judge consistently. They also have a limit: a rule only says whether something is an exception, not why or what to do. These four exceptions are really one event. If the hub just pushed four messages at you, you would have to piece them together yourself. So the next step is to connect them.

Writing rules means choosing thresholds. Too loose and exceptions pour in until nobody looks; too tight and real trouble goes unreported. The digital factory keeps thresholds in the rule catalogue (such as P5's “6 parts” and R3's “$500”), where instructors and students can read and change them.

3.3 The causal chain: up to the cause, down to the impact

Everything in a plant is connected: operations are queued on machines, operations belong to work orders, work orders serve sales orders, and sales orders belong to customers. The hub stores these links as a network, the factory ontology. When an exception appears, it walks the network in two directions:

  • Up to the cause: why will the order be late? Because its work orders have unfinished operations. Why unfinished? They are queued on GRD-01. Why is GRD-01 idle? A spindle fault, waiting for parts.
  • Down to the impact: GRD-01 stops → the grinding operation stops → the three groups of shaft work orders queued there are pushed back → three orders finish later → which customers will not get their goods, and what penalties apply.

Look at the colours in Figure 3-1. All three groups of work orders are pushed back, but only the robot plant's order will be late. Summit's work is at the front of the queue: it is ground first after the repair, finishes at 15:12 on Tuesday and still ships on Wednesday. Harbor's due date is far away. Being affected is not the same as being late. The impact has to be worked out order by order; a breakdown does not mean everything is lost.

Every link needs evidence. In the hub's causal chain each link states what it rests on: a bus message, a document, a rule. A link without evidence is a guess. In Lab 15 you first draw this chain yourself, then compare it with the hub's.

3.4 Impact: downtime is not lost production time

The supervisor said “24 hours down, three days late”: 24 hours ÷ 9 hours a day ≈ 2.7 days, rounded to 3. What went wrong? The repair goes on overnight, but the shop does not work at night anyway. What is really lost is only the part of the downtime that overlaps the shifts:

\[L=\bigl|\,[\,t_s,\;t_s+D\,)\;\cap\;\text{shifts}\,\bigr|\]

ts is when the stop begins and D is its length. This stop runs from 10:00 Monday to 10:00 Tuesday: 7 hours lost on Monday (10:00–17:00) and 2 on Tuesday (08:00–10:00), so L = 9 hours, not 24.

Does losing 9 hours make the order 9 hours late? No again. It depends on how much slack the order had. Without the breakdown the grinder would do Summit first (5.2 h) and then the robot plant (13 h), 18.2 hours in all: 7 on Monday, 9 on Tuesday and 2.2 on Wednesday, finishing at 10:12 on Wednesday. The robot order ships on Thursday, so its shafts must be done by the end of Wednesday (the regular shift ends at 17:00; finishing that evening on overtime also counts). Counted in regular hours, the slack is S = 6.8 hours.

\[\text{shortfall}=\max(0,\;L-S)=9-6.8=2.2\ \text{h}\]

Short by 2.2 hours, the shafts are finished only at 10:12 on Thursday, the order ships on Friday: 1 day late, a $200 penalty. Not three days and $600: the supervisor’s figure is three times the real one ($600 against $200).

Three steps to the impact: ① find the production time really lost, L (the overlap of downtime and shifts); ② find each order's slack S (production time between its planned finish and its latest finish); ③ only orders with L − S above zero are late; convert to working days and penalties. Order matters: orders at the front of the queue recover first and use little slack; those at the back also carry the work ahead of them.

The other way round: if the grinder stops at 15:00 on Friday for 20 hours, into Saturday morning, the only production lost is Friday's 2 hours, since the weekend is not worked. The same “20 hours down” can have impacts several times apart.

3.5 Three remedies: overtime, expediting, outsourcing

There are three common ways to make up 2.2 hours. Each costs money, and the digital factory's parameters say how much:

  • B Overtime after the repair: once the grinder is fixed, run it longer each day. Overtime cost = hours × machine rate × 1.5. GRD-01's rate is $20 depreciation + $26 labour + $6 energy = $52 per hour, so overtime is $78 an hour. The digital factory allows at most 4 hours of overtime a day.
  • C Expedite the repair: pay $300 to rush the repair (express spare parts, a service engineer from the maker) and the remaining downtime is halved.
  • D Outsource: send some shafts to another shop for grinding. The outsourcing price is the plant's own hourly cost × 1.3, plus $50 freight per batch, and the parts come back after 24 hours. One unit's three shafts cost 39/60 × $52 × 1.3 = $43.94.

First by hand. Short by 2.2 hours:

\[\text{overtime}:\;2.2\times 78=\$171.6\qquad \text{outsourcing}:\;n\ge\frac{2.2}{0.65}=3.4\Rightarrow n=4,\;\;4\times 43.94+50=\$225.76\]

And expediting? The remaining 24 hours are halved to 12, and the grinder is back at 22:00 Monday. Of the 12 hours of downtime saved, 10 fall at night; the extra production time is only 2 hours on Tuesday morning. L drops from 9 to 7 hours, still 7 − 6.8 = 0.2 hours, or 12 minutes, short. After paying $300 the robot order is still 1 day late, $500 in all. To save the date with expediting you would also need 12 minutes of overtime, $315.60 in total.

That is the supervisor's second mistake: he assumed a faster repair would save the delivery. The repair is faster, but most of the time it gains is at night, when it is of no use.

Which is cheaper, overtime or outsourcing, depends on how far behind you are. An hour of grinding outsourced costs $43.94 ÷ 0.65 = $67.60, less than $78 of overtime, but each batch adds $50 of freight. A small shortfall favours overtime, a large one outsourcing. The break-even point:

\[78\,h = 67.6\,h + 50\;\Rightarrow\; h = 4.8\ \text{h}\]

This time the shortfall is 2.2 hours, below the break-even point, so overtime is cheaper.

3.6 What-if simulation: fast-forward each option

Hand calculation is quick, but it quietly assumes that there is enough overtime available, that outsourced parts come back in time and that the orders ahead are unaffected. In a real shop these assumptions break all the time. So the hub does not stop at a hand calculation; it simulates. It takes a snapshot of the plant, makes a fast-forward copy, applies one option and lets the copy run minute by minute on the real shift calendar to see when each order finishes. Simulation does not touch the real shop, so the options can be tried one after another.

This chapter uses a small simulation program (the grinder only, with the same rules as the hub) to run the four options:

Table 3-3 Simulation results of four options (down from 10:00 Monday for 24 hours)
OptionRobot order's shafts doneDeliveryExtra cost
A Do nothingThu 10:121 day latepenalty $200
B Overtime after repair (2 h a day, the hub’s default; cap 4 h; stop when done)Wed 17:12on time2.2 h overtime = $171.60
C Expedite the repairThu 08:121 day late$300 + $200 penalty = $500
D Outsource the shafts of 4 unitsWed 16:36on time$225.76

This time simulation and hand calculation agree: 2.2 hours short, overtime at $171.60 is cheapest, and expediting wastes money. The hub ranks options first by delivery, then by extra cost, then by overtime hours. On that order, B comes first.

When do hand calculation and simulation disagree? Figure 3-3 shows how the cost of each option changes as the downtime grows from 2 to 48 hours.

  • The curves have steps and plateaus. From 7 to 22 hours down, the repair finishes during the night and L stays at 7 hours: a longer stop loses no production. Doing nothing keeps costing $200 a little longer, up to 25 hours: L grows from 7 to 10 hours, the robot order is still only 1 day late, and Summit's slack (10.8 regular hours) still holds. The rule of thumb “late by as long as it is down” cannot see either plateau.
  • From 26 hours down, Summit cannot be saved either. Summit is first in the queue with 10.8 regular hours of slack; once L exceeds that, Summit is late too.
  • At 30 hours down, overtime cannot save the orders. By hand: L = 15, the robot order is 8.2 hours short, so 8.2 × 78 = $639.60 of overtime should do. The simulation says otherwise: the grinder is back only at 16:00 Tuesday, which leaves 1 regular hour plus 4 hours of overtime that day; even with the full 4 hours every day, Summit and the robot plant are each 1 day late, $1,024 in all. The hand calculation forgot the daily cap and forgot that Summit is ahead in the queue. Outsourcing 13 units saves the robot plant but not Summit ($821.22); only expediting saves Summit, with the robot plant 1 day late ($500); doing nothing leaves both 1 day late ($400).
  • The ranking rule has a blind spot. At 26–30 hours down, the hub's ranking (first the delay of the latest order, then money) recommends doing nothing ($400): like expediting, its latest order is 1 day late, and it is $100 cheaper. Yet it makes two orders late, while expediting makes only one. The rule looks at the latest order, not at how many are late. From 31 hours down, doing nothing makes the robot plant 2 days late, so expediting ($500) ranks first, and it is the only option that saves Summit. Which order to save, and how many late orders are acceptable, is then a decision for people. Lab 15's discussion question 2 asks about exactly this ranking.

The hub's default options are not exactly this chapter's options. For a machine-down exception the command centre offers four by default: do nothing, overtime after the repair, reassign to a similar machine, and expedite the repair. The default overtime is a single day (the snapshot's day, or the next working day if the shift is over), plant-wide, 120 minutes, billed for every busy machine. This breakdown happens at 10:00 Monday, so the default overtime lands on Monday, while the grinder is still under repair, and is useless. Outsourcing is not in the default list at all; it appears only for exceptions such as “order may be late”, with a default of 3 parts, one operation and 1 day. So options B and D of this chapter must be set up by hand in the what-if workbench: overtime on GRD-01 for Tuesday and Wednesday, and the number of units to outsource. That is what Lab 15 task 2 means by asking on which day overtime actually helps. This chapter's model also simplifies one thing: overtime stops once the robot order is done and only the minutes used are paid; the hub bills the scheduled minutes, so 2 hours scheduled on both Tuesday and Wednesday cost $312.

That is why we simulate: experience gives a direction, hand calculation gives an order of magnitude, and simulation brings in shifts, caps and queue order to give numbers good enough to decide on. Simulations can be wrong too: the snapshot may be inexact, cycle times vary, something else may happen. So afterwards there is a review: compare the actual outcome with the simulation and ask how far apart they are and why.

3.7 Three dimensions of an option: money, delivery, risk

Comparing options on money alone is a mistake; so is comparing on delivery alone. The hub lays out each option in three dimensions:

Table 3-4 Three dimensions of the four options
OptionMoneyDeliveryRisk
A Do nothing$200robot plant 1 day latethe penalty is small, but the robot plant's assembly line may stop, and a key customer's trust is hard to win back
B Overtime$171.60on timeneeds a skilled grinder operator willing to stay (Chapter 7); the grinder is already overdue for service, so extra hours raise the risk (Chapter 6)
C Expedite the repair$5001 day latethe money does not save the date; if the part arrives late, things get worse
D Outsource$225.76on timeoutsourced parts need full inspection; if the other shop is a day late, it was all for nothing

Three remarks:

  • Money counts only extra cash. Overtime, expediting, outsourcing and penalties. The margin lost while the bottleneck stands (Chapter 6's $200 an hour) is not counted again, because options B and D make the work up; the comparison is how much extra it costs to make it up.
  • Doing nothing costs only $200. Why not choose it? Because delivery comes before money. For the robot plant, one day late may mean its assembly line stops for a day, which costs far more than $200. The penalty is only the part that can be written into a contract.
  • Remedies can move trouble onto someone else. If the robot order is moved to the front of the queue, it is ground by 14:00 Wednesday and ships on time; but Summit is pushed back, finishes at 10:12 Thursday, ships 2 days late and pays $400. Reordering did not remove the loss; it shifted it to another customer, and at a higher price.

3.8 In the digital factory: Lab 15

The factory hub of the WenQuest digital factory is exactly what this chapter describes: rules check the plant every 20 seconds, every link of a causal chain has evidence, and simulation fast-forwards from a snapshot with the same code as the simulated shop. Lab 15, “Root cause, what-if simulation and decisions for shop-floor anomalies”, uses comparison mode: you trace, simulate and decide yourself first, and see the hub's recommendation only after you submit.

  1. Trace it yourself (task 1): the instructor injects a breakdown (suggested: GRD-01, 1,200–2,400 minutes). Without opening the hub, use the home page, shop terminals and order pages to find out which machine stopped, which work orders are queued, which orders they belong to and how many days late they will be. The platform scores you against the system's causal chain.
  2. Simulate at least three options (task 2): in the what-if workbench try do nothing, overtime, expediting and outsourcing, save each as a scenario and compare delivery, cost and overtime. Think: why is there no “reassign” option for the grinder? On which day does overtime actually help?
  3. Choose and justify (task 3): cover delivery, cost and risk. After you submit, comparison mode ends; read the hub's recommendation and the AI's analysis and compare them with your choice.
  4. Adopt and execute (task 4): the option becomes an action that runs only after the instructor (plant manager) approves it: overtime changes the shift calendar, expediting shortens the stop, outsourcing removes parts from the shop's queue; the actions are also written to ERPNext (an urgent purchase, for example, creates a purchase request).
  5. Review (task 5): when the work orders are done, the platform compares simulation and reality and reports the deviation.

Three notes. The digital factory's closed-loop simulation tracks only one shaft, output shaft SH-301, so the work orders and hours you see in the lab are smaller than in this chapter. On the platform the two-week scenario's main-line order shows the customer as Harbor Crane (the robot plant joins in round 30), so the numbers differ; check them with the same method. The time of the breakdown and the queue in the lab also differ from this chapter, so the conclusions may differ too (for example whether expediting saves the date at 20 hours down); go by the simulation. The lab mark is 60 points scored by the platform plus 40 for the report.

3.9 Robot link and an everyday example

Robots: where does one day late lead? Each collaborative robot from the robot plant uses WQR-105 units in the two large joints of its base, so 20 reducers make 10 robots (illustrative values). If we ship a day late, its assembly line builds 10 fewer robots, and its own customers wait too. A causal chain does not stop at the factory gate; it runs on down the supply chain. Robots have the same kind of tracing inside them. When a controller reports “joint 3 overcurrent”, it traces up to the cause (an overload? a worn reducer? a collision?) and down to the impact (this robot stops, the line's cycle time drops, today's output falls). That is what fault diagnosis in a modern robot controller does.

Everyday example: the takeaway is late. Where is it stuck? A delivery order goes through the restaurant accepting it (2 minutes), cooking (12), waiting for a rider (5) and the ride (11); the app promises 30 minutes. At the lunch peak the kitchen takes 25 minutes to cook, 13 more than usual, and the food arrives after 43 minutes. The rider arrives early and simply waits for the food. This chain has no slack, so the kitchen's extra 13 minutes all turn into lateness. Calling the rider does not help: the kitchen is where it is stuck, and the rider is only downstream. The app's updated arrival time is the platform running a causal chain and a simulation in the background.

3.10 Back to the problem

GRD-01 stops at 10:00 Monday for 24 hours. The production time really lost is 9 hours, not 24. The robot order had 6.8 hours of slack and is 2.2 hours short: doing nothing makes it 1 day late with a $200 penalty, while Summit and Harbor stay on time. The supervisor's “three days late, $600” is three times the real figure ($600 against $200), and the expediting he wanted would cost $300 without saving the date. Recommendation: after the repair, run the grinder 2 hours of overtime on Tuesday and 12 minutes more on Wednesday (2.2 hours in all) for $171.60; the robot order's shafts are done on Wednesday and it ships on time on Thursday. The fallback is to outsource the shafts of 4 units ($225.76). Before choosing overtime, confirm that a skilled grinder operator is available, and remember that the grinder is overdue for service (Chapter 6), so weigh the failure risk of those extra hours too. In the hub the planner raises this action; overtime needs the plant manager's approval, and only then does the shift calendar change.

Summary

  • A rule turns “the data meet a condition” into an exception; it never forgets or tires, but it only says that something is wrong, not why or what to do. Thresholds need judgement: too loose floods people with alerts, too tight misses real trouble.
  • A causal chain follows the links of the factory ontology in two directions: up to the cause and down to the impact (machine → operation → work order → sales order → customer). Every link needs evidence.
  • Impact must be calculated: the production time really lost, L, is the overlap of downtime and shifts; whether an order is late depends on L minus its slack S. Being affected is not the same as being late.
  • Overtime = hours × rate × 1.5; outsourcing = units × price + freight; expediting has a fixed fee whose effect depends on whether the saved downtime falls in the day or at night. Small shortfalls favour overtime, large ones outsourcing.
  • Simulation brings in shifts, caps and queue order; estimates from experience are often wrong. Compare options on money, delivery and risk; the hub ranks by delivery first, then money, then overtime.
