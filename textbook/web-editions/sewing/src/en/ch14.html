Chapter 13 explained how motors, solenoids and sensors each work. This chapter arranges them into the working cycle of an electronically controlled lockstitch machine: presser foot down, start backtack, soft start, sewing, end backtack, deceleration, thread trimming, needle positioning, thread wiping, presser-foot lift. Every electrically controlled action has to line up with the mechanical timing chart of Chapter 8: the feed direction must be switched while the feed dog is below the throat plate, and the trimming moving knife must enter while the needle-thread loop is spread wide and cut when the take-up lever reaches its highest point. Every actuator has a delay, and that delay varies with supply voltage, temperature and wear, so every action has an upper speed limit. This is the theme that runs through the whole chapter.

14.1 Opening: the half-second after the pedal is released

At the end of a seam the operator presses the pedal back with the heel, and within half a second the machine runs through a whole sequence: the main shaft drops from several thousand r/min to a few hundred, turns one more stitch, a click under the throat plate cuts the threads, the shaft stops at the needle-up position (take-up lever near its highest point), a thin rod sweeps in front of the needle to wipe the needle-thread end aside, and the presser foot lifts. The operator pulls the work out, places the next piece and presses the pedal forward with the ball of the foot; the presser foot drops, the machine first sews a few backtack stitches, then a few slow stitches, then accelerates to the set speed.

This sequence is executed step by step by a state machine in the controller (Fig. 14-1). Boxes are states; arrows are trigger conditions: pedal position, stitch count, the angle the main shaft has reached, an actuator's in-position signal. The advantage of a state machine is that what happens at each step, and when the next step begins, is written down explicitly; if a step hangs, the controller knows where it stopped and which fault to report.

The bottom of Fig. 14-1 is the time budget for stopping and trimming from 4000 r/min (worked example): about 146 ms to decelerate to trimming speed, about 231 ms for the trimming stitch, only a few milliseconds for needle positioning — about 0.38 s in total, or roughly half a second once thread wiping and presser-foot lift are added. An operator trims thousands of times a day; multiplied out, this half-second amounts to tens of minutes, so trimming speed is itself a design target for the control system.

14.2 The main-shaft speed profile

Main-shaft speed follows the pedal, but the controller does not let it follow the pedal freely; it imposes several limits (Fig. 14-2).

Soft start　After trimming, only a needle-thread end a few centimetres long remains in the needle eye, and the bobbin thread has just been cut too. If the first few stitches of a seam run too fast, one pull of the take-up lever can draw the needle-thread end out of the eye; and since the bobbin thread has not yet been pulled tight, it easily tangles into a bird's nest under the fabric. The controller therefore sews the first few stitches at low speed. When there is a start backtack, the backtack itself is speed-limited (Section 14.3), and the soft start applies after the backtack. One manufacturer's manual for an electronic-feed lockstitch machine gives several soft-start modes; one of them is 400 sti/min for the first 3 stitches and 4000 sti/min from the 4th stitch on (a manufacturer’s instruction manual).

Acceleration and deceleration　The servo motor's peak torque determines how fast the main shaft can at most accelerate and decelerate. In the Chapter 13 worked example, the angular acceleration the peak torque can produce is about 4400 rad/s². Real controllers usually keep a margin; this chapter's worked examples use 60%, so accelerating from 400 r/min to 4000 r/min takes about 0.14 s, roughly four or five stitches. (4400 rad/s² is the deceleration value, where friction helps braking; during acceleration friction works against the motor, so the capability is somewhat lower.) If acceleration is too aggressive, the inertia torque of the needle-bar mechanism adds to the motor torque and the machine head shakes.

Slowing to trimming speed　Before trimming, the main shaft must first drop to a very low speed and sew the last stitch at that speed. Why it must slow down is calculated in Section 14.4. The lower the trimming speed, the more reliable the trim, but the slower the last stitch: stopping and trimming from 4000 r/min with a trimming speed of 200, 300 or 500 r/min takes about 0.50, 0.38 or 0.29 s respectively.

How long a seam takes　Putting all this together, the 40-stitch seam on the left of Fig. 14-2 (about 10 cm at 2.5 mm stitch length) takes about 1.36 s from first stitch to standstill (excluding backtacks). Less than 30% of that is spent at maximum speed, and the 3 soft-start stitches alone take 0.45 s. The more short seams there are, the larger the share of soft start and stop-and-trim; then raising the maximum speed achieves almost nothing, and only shortening acceleration, deceleration and trimming time helps.

14.3 Automatic backtack

Sewing a few stitches backwards at the start and end of a seam to lock the stitches is called backtacking (reverse stitching). On a lockstitch machine with mechanical feed, a reverse-feed solenoid moves the stitch-length regulating mechanism so that the feed direction reverses; on a machine with electronic feed, the feed stepper motor changes direction directly.

Switching the feed direction is a matter of timing. While the feed dog is above the throat plate it is pushing the fabric; if the direction is switched at that moment, that stitch is neither a forward nor a reverse stitch, and the backtack stitches will not line up. In the Chapter 6 worked example the feed dog drops below the throat plate at 114.2° and rises above it at 325.8°; the 211.6° in between is the “idle angle”, and the switch must be completed within it (Fig. 14-3). The controller issues the command at 114.2°; with an actuator delay t and main-shaft speed n, the shaft has turned 6nt degrees by the time the switch is complete. Keeping a 10° margin, the upper speed limit is

n_{max}=\frac{211.6-10}{6\,t}\quad\text{(r/min, t in seconds)}

The reverse-feed solenoid has to push links and springs, so its delay is longer than that of the trimming solenoid. With a delay of 18 ms (illustrative value) the maximum backtack speed is about 1870 r/min; with 20 ms, about 1680 r/min. The stepper motor of an electronic feed can reverse in a few milliseconds; at 5 ms the limit exceeds 6000 r/min, and switching delay is no longer the constraint. Yet the machine in the manual cited above has electronic feed and its default start-backtack speed is still 1800 sti/min (adjustable), which shows that backtack speed is limited by other factors as well: the backtack stitches must coincide with the forward stitches, the stitches must be pulled tight, and the fabric must have time to be gripped by the presser foot and feed dog.

Even when switched within the idle angle, backtack stitches do not necessarily coincide with the forward ones. One reason is clearance in the mechanism: when the feed direction reverses, the clearance shifts from one side to the other, so the first reverse stitch is slightly short. Another is the elasticity of the fabric. The control remedy is “backtack compensation”: adjusting the stitch length or switching moment separately for the first and last backtack stitch, which is especially easy with electronic feed.

14.4 Trimming sequence

Construction of the trimmer　The thread trimmer of a lockstitch machine sits under the throat plate beside the rotary hook (Fig. 14-4). A common arrangement has a stationary fixed knife and a moving knife mounted on an oscillating shaft that swings back and forth; there are also rotary scissors in which both blades move. The moving knife is driven in one of two ways. In one, it is driven by a trimming cam on the shaft: normally the cam roller is disengaged from the cam groove, and for trimming a solenoid pushes the roller into the groove so the moving knife makes one stroke out and back with the shaft — the pattern sewers of Chapter 15 work this way. In the other, a solenoid (or motor) drives the moving knife directly through links, as in the patent cited below. The timing problems differ. With cam drive, the moment the knife enters is set by the cam profile; the solenoid only has to push the roller in before the groove entry comes round — a little early does no harm, but late means missing the groove or the roller striking its edge. With direct drive, the knife-entry moment is simply the command moment plus the delay. The timing model in this section applies to the latter.

The trimming process　Following the description in a patent for a lockstitch thread-trimming device (thread-trimming device patent), one trim proceeds in several steps. The last stitch is sewn as usual: the hook point catches the needle-thread loop at 206° and carries it around the bobbin case, and the loop grows larger and larger. When the needle has risen to about 10 mm above the throat plate (about 291° in this book’s needle-bar model), the controller energises the trimming solenoid; after the pull-in delay the moving knife swings into its catching position, its tip passing over the bobbin thread and under the thread on the supply side of the needle-thread loop. The other side of the loop (the needle thread leading to the fabric) and the bobbin thread fall into the knife’s hook notch. After the loop has passed around the bobbin case and slipped off the hook (341°), the take-up lever draws in thread and pulls the loop up; at this moment the tension assembly opens, so the thread taken up by the lever is drawn from the spool side. The moving knife returns, carrying the fabric-side needle thread and the bobbin thread towards the fixed knife, and cuts them as the take-up lever reaches its highest point (55° of the next revolution). The supply-side needle thread is not cut; it stays threaded through the needle eye as the thread end for starting the next seam, and only very short thread ends remain under the fabric.

An illustrative criterion　Drawing this process on the timing chart of Chapter 8 (Fig. 14-5) gives the conditions the control must satisfy. The criteria used in this chapter are illustrative values:

  • The moving knife must enter the loop between 300° and 330° (illustrative value). Too early, and the loop has not yet been carried around the bobbin case into the knife’s path, so the knife cannot catch the fabric-side needle thread: the needle thread is not cut, only the bobbin thread. Too late, and the loop has already slipped off and been pulled away by the take-up lever: again no cut.
  • The cut occurs at the take-up lever’s highest point (55°). The main shaft must pass this point before stopping, and must stop before the needle point enters the fabric (101.7°); with a 5° margin, that gives 55°–96.7°. This is the limit for “being able to stop”: at 96.7° the needle point is only a millimetre or two above the fabric, and real machines set the needle-up position near the early end of this range (Chapter 13).
  • The tension assembly must be open while the take-up lever draws in thread and the moving knife returns, covering at least 335° to 55° of the next revolution. The thread taken up by the lever is then drawn from the spool side, leaving a long enough thread end beyond the needle eye. If the tension assembly does not open, the lever can only draw thread from the thread-end side, the remaining end is too short, and the thread easily unthreads when the next seam starts.
  • After the main shaft has stopped, the wiper sweeps in front of the needle and moves the thread end beyond the eye up onto the presser foot. Otherwise the end stays under the presser foot and easily tangles into a bird's nest under the fabric when the next seam starts.

How far ahead to issue the command　From energising to reaching its end position, the trimming solenoid goes through the pull-in delay described in Chapter 13. For a directly driven moving knife, if the controller issues the command at shaft angle φ_c and the delay is t, the knife actually enters at φ_c + 6nt. In the worked example, with a trimming speed of 300 r/min and a delay of 6–10 ms, a command at 290° gives actual entry between 301° and 308°, inside the window. At 700 r/min the same command angle delays entry to 315°–332°, outside the window, and the command angle must be advanced to before 288°; the entry range also widens from 7° to 17°.

Upper limit of trimming speed　The command angle can be advanced automatically with speed, so the average delay is not the problem; the variation in delay is. When the delay varies between t_min and t_max, the actual entry angle varies over a range of width 6n(t_max − t_min), which must fit into the 30° window:

n \le \frac{30}{6\,(t_{max}-t_{min})}

The other limit comes from stopping: after the cut the shaft must stop within 41.7° (from 55° to 96.7°). With the servo stopping at its maximum deceleration of about 4400 rad/s², the trimming speed may not exceed about 770 r/min (worked example). Fig. 14-6 plots both limits together. With a delay of 6–10 ms the first limit is 1250 r/min, so the stopping limit governs; when a low supply voltage or a worn spring changes the delay to 5–15 ms, the first limit drops to 500 r/min.

So to make trimming both fast and reliable, the first thing is to make the solenoid delay stable: a regulated supply, boost (over-excitation) and Zener-diode freewheeling (Chapter 13). The value of these measures is not just that they make the solenoid faster, but above all that they make its delay more consistent. All numbers in the figure are illustrative values chosen to explain the principle; real machines trim at speeds far below sewing speed, set by a parameter. 14.5 Electronic stitch length and electronic feed

A lockstitch machine with mechanical feed sets the feed amount with a stitch-length regulator (a rotatable rocker or eccentric mechanism); the operator turns the stitch-length dial and presses the reverse lever. Electronic feed hands this rocker over to a stepper motor (Fig. 14-7a): the stepper turns through an angle and the feed amount and direction change accordingly, with the stitch length entered directly on the panel. The machine in the manual cited earlier has a feed motor with an encoder and two feed-dog height versions, for light and heavy materials (a manufacturer’s instruction manual); another manufacturer’s direct-drive lockstitch machine hands both the horizontal and the vertical motion of the feed dog to motors and calls this “digital feed” (a manufacturer’s product catalogue).

Changing stitch length, like changing direction, must be completed in the idle angle while the feed dog is below the throat plate, and takes effect from the next stitch. This makes possible several things mechanical feed cannot do: changing stitch length section by section within a seam under program control, with denser stitches at the start and at corners; setting the stitch count to the repeat of a striped or checked fabric so that stripes and checks match; and backtacking with the stepper motor, which switches fast and makes compensation easy. When the feed-dog lift is also motor-driven, the feed-dog trajectory can be changed to suit the fabric: quantities such as feed-dog height and feed timing, discussed in Chapter 6, which once required taking the machine apart to adjust, become parameters.

14.6 Electronic tension assembly and presser-foot pressure

Electronic tension assembly　As Chapter 7 explained, needle-thread tension comes from the tension assembly: two tension discs grip the needle thread, and the tension equals the pretension plus 2μ times the clamping force. An electronic tension assembly produces the clamping force with a proportional solenoid or a voice-coil motor; the larger the current, the tighter the grip (Fig. 14-7b). A voice-coil motor’s force is proportional to current and very linear, but its stroke is small and it is expensive; an ordinary solenoid’s force is roughly proportional to the square of the current and also depends on the gap at the tension discs, so it must be calibrated against a current–tension curve. In the worked example, with a pretension of 20 cN, a friction coefficient of 0.2 and a clamping force of 4 N per ampere, raising the current from 0 to 1 A raises the needle-thread tension from 20 cN to about 180 cN (illustrative values).

Once tension becomes a number in the program, it can be switched by seam section: higher for topstitching, lower for concealed seams; released automatically during trimming (Section 14.4); reduced for the first few stitches of a seam to prevent unthreading. One manufacturer’s direct-drive lockstitch machine calls this digital control of needle-thread tension: the tension is set on the panel and stored with the sewing program (a manufacturer’s product catalogue).

Presser-foot pressure　Presser-foot pressure used to be set by turning the screw on the presser-foot spring; now it can be adjusted by a stepper motor or electromagnetic actuator. As Chapter 6 explained, if the pressure is too low the presser foot drags the upper ply and the plies shift relative to each other; if too high, light fabrics pucker and knits are stretched. Under electronic control, presser-foot pressure, like tension, can be set by seam section or changed automatically with fabric thickness — the case study of Section 14.7.

14.7 Case study: one lockstitch machine adapting automatically to different fabrics

The technical problem　Along one seam of a garment the fabric thickness is not uniform. The most typical case is crossing a seam: on reaching another seam that has already been sewn, the fabric suddenly goes from two plies to four or even six, and then quickly back to two. When thickness changes, several parameters ought to change with it: the needle needs more force to penetrate the thick section, and at too high a speed it breaks easily; in the thick plies the needle and thread meet more friction, the needle is easily deflected and the loop does not form well (Chapter 5), so stitches are skipped easily; each stitch consumes more thread, so tension should be adjusted; and the presser foot is pushed up, its pressure changes, and the feed easily slips or the plies shift. A skilled operator eases off the pedal before the cross seam and helps the fabric along by hand; an ordinary operator often breaks the needle, breaks the thread or skips stitches right there.

Analysis of the principle　An electronically controlled lockstitch machine has two kinds of signals that can sense a change in fabric thickness (what follows describes possible approaches). One is presser-foot height: a displacement sensor (a Hall element, for example) on the presser bar responds immediately when the fabric thickens and the foot is pushed up; since a cross seam often reaches the presser foot before it reaches the needle, there is a little time to prepare. The other is the main-shaft servo current: penetrating thick plies increases the resisting torque and the current rises with it, as follows from the torque–current relationship in Chapter 13. By combining these two signals with settings such as fabric type and stitch length, the controller can adjust automatically when thickness changes: lower the speed, raise the presser-foot pressure, change the needle-thread tension and, if necessary, adjust the feed amount.

Solutions and data　The first verifiable example is in a product catalogue: one manufacturer’s direct-drive lockstitch machine has an automatic mode that increases or decreases presser-foot pressure when it detects a change in the number of fabric plies (a manufacturer’s product catalogue). Further, according to company material quoted in a 2025 brokerage research report, a model launched in 2024 by a domestic manufacturer detects changes in fabric thickness 32,000 times per second, and a 2025 model is advertised as dynamically adjusting thread tension, speed and presser-foot pressure so that the pressing force and the feeding force are matched in real time, solving needle breakage, thread breakage and skipped stitches at cross seams (brokerage research report, 2025 (in Chinese)). The latter is the manufacturer’s promotional wording; the detection method and adjustment rules have not been published, and this section only explains, from first principles, how it could be implemented.

Discussion　This case shows the fundamental change electronic control brings: stitch length, tension and pressure were once “settings”, adjusted and then left alone; now they are “control variables” that can be changed at any moment, and so can be adjusted in closed loop from sensor signals. The difficulty lies in the adjustment rules: how much tension and how much pressure should change for a given change in thickness depends on the fabric, the thread and the needle, and requires a large amount of test data — one reason manufacturers attach the “AI” label. Chapter 31 returns to this topic.

14.8 Design notes and limits of the model

The key point of this chapter: every action of an electronically controlled lockstitch machine must line up with the mechanical timing chart, and actuator delays give every action an upper speed limit. The backtack switch has to fit into the feed dog’s idle angle; the trimming knife’s entry has to fit into the window in which the loop is spread wide. The command angle can be advanced according to speed and the average delay, but the range of delay variation can only be accommodated by lowering the speed — so making actuator delay stable matters more than making it faster. Soft start, tension release during trimming and wiping after trimming all serve to let the next seam start cleanly. Electronic feed and electronic tension assemblies turn stitch length, tension and pressure into numbers in the program, which can be set by seam section or adjusted automatically from sensor signals.

The models in this chapter have clear limits. The knife-entry window of 300°–330°, the tension-release range and the stopping margin are illustrative values taken from the Chapter 8 timing chart and the qualitative description in the patent; on a real machine they are determined by the trimming mechanism (cam profile or linkage) and must be checked against the service manual. The two limits on trimming speed serve only to show the influencing factors; real machines trim at much lower speeds. The acceleration and deceleration of the speed profile are taken as 60% of the servo’s capability; in practice they depend on the controller’s parameters. Backtack switching considers only delay, not clearance and elasticity in the mechanism. The current–tension relationship of the electronic tension assembly is illustrative.

Shop-floor notes

  • If trimming cuts the bobbin thread but not the needle thread, the moving knife is most likely entering too early or too late: on cam-driven trimmers check the position of the trimming cam, on direct-drive trimmers check the command angle; in both cases check the solenoid voltage, and then whether the trimming speed has been raised.
  • If the thread unthreads when the next seam starts after a trim, check that the tension release operates and that the needle-thread end left is long enough; then check whether the needle stops too early (take-up lever not yet at its highest point).
  • If a bird's nest forms under the fabric at the start of a seam, check that the wiper operates and that soft start has not been switched off.
  • If backtack stitches do not line up with the forward stitches, first check whether the backtack speed is too high, then adjust with the backtack compensation parameters.
  • If the needle breaks or stitches skip at thick cross seams, first lower the speed and change to a suitable needle; on machines with automatic presser-foot pressure and tension adjustment, check that the presser-foot height sensor is working.
