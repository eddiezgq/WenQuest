This is an optional chapter (★). The earlier parts of the book are about machines and Chapter 29 covers five typical kinds of factory; this chapter sits between the two and deals with how production is organised on the shop floor. Operations, times, wages and equipment costs are worked-example values that the author will later replace with factory data.

However well a sewing machine is designed, on the shop floor it is just one of dozens of machines. A garment passes through dozens of operations, dozens of operators each do one part, and the cut parts flow between them. Which operation goes to whom, how much work each person gets and how many machines of which kinds are provided directly determine how many pieces the line makes per hour and how much labour each piece costs. Chapter 29 applies these methods to shirts, T-shirts and small-batch quick response, and Chapter 12 covers hanger systems and flexible cells; rather than repeat those worked examples, this chapter explains the methods themselves: the stages a garment goes through from sample to bulk production; how to break it down into operations and measure standard minute values; how to assign operations to operators and calculate the balance efficiency; what lean production sets out to eliminate; and how to provision equipment for a given output and how many automatic machines one person can tend. The example running through the chapter is a casual-trousers line. The chapter also answers a question tied to the main thread of this book: how much does a sewing machine’s maximum speed really affect shop-floor efficiency?

28.1 Opening: from sample to bulk production

Once a garment comes into a factory it travels roughly two stretches of road (Fig. 28-1). The first is technical preparation: the designer produces the style and the paper patterns, the sample maker sews a sample, and it is fitted and revised; once approved, the technical department writes the specification sheet (also called the tech pack), containing a size chart for every size, a bill of fabrics and trims, and the sewing requirements for each part — for example, how many millimetres the topstitching sits from the edge, how many stitches per 3 cm, and which stitch type to use; then a pre-production sample is made from the bulk fabric for the customer to approve. Meanwhile the industrial engineers (usually called IE) break the garment down into operations, estimate each operation’s standard minute value and lay out the production line — the main subject of this chapter.

The second stretch is bulk production: marker making and cutting, turning fabric into stacks of cut parts; sewing, turning the cut parts into garments; finishing, including buttonholing and button sewing, pressing, thread trimming and folding; and finally inspection and packing. Of the four stages, sewing has the most operations, the longest times and the most people. In this chapter’s casual-trousers example, sewing accounts for about four-fifths of all labour time (lower part of Fig. 28-1; in this chapter’s example buttonholing, button sewing and thread trimming/inspection are also placed on the sewing line). Shop-floor management therefore concentrates its effort on sewing, and the sewing machines described in the earlier parts of the book all work in this stage.

28.2 Marker making and cutting

Before sewing, the fabric must first become cut parts. Marker making means laying out all the pattern pieces of one or several garments on a sheet of paper as wide as the fabric, packed as tightly as possible; the finished layout is called the marker. Because the pieces are irregular in shape, there are gaps however tightly they are packed; the ratio of the total area of the pieces to the area of the marker is called the fabric utilisation. Fabric often accounts for more than half of a garment’s manufacturing cost, so raising utilisation by one or two percentage points saves far more money than shaving a few seconds off sewing times.

How many garments are placed on one marker has a big effect on utilisation (Fig. 28-2, left). With only one garment, the gaps between pieces cannot be filled; with several garments and a mix of large and small sizes, the pieces of the small sizes can be tucked into the gaps between those of the large sizes, and utilisation rises. In the worked example in the figure it rises from 78% for one garment to 86% for six (illustrative values). But a marker cannot be infinitely long: it is limited by the length of the cutting table, and a very long lay is also hard to spread flat.

For cutting, the fabric is spread layer upon layer on the cutting table, up to several dozen plies, and a single cut produces, from every ply, the garments on the marker. How many plies one lay can have is limited by the stroke and accuracy of the cutting knife and depends on fabric thickness: with too many plies, the parts in the top and bottom plies lose their shape. An order usually has several sizes, each in a different quantity, and must be divided into several lays for cutting; this is the cut plan. Worked example: an order for S 200, M 400, L 400 and XL 200 pieces, using a six-garment marker with a 1:2:2:1 size ratio, needs 200 plies for every size; with at most 80 plies per lay, it is split into three lays of 80, 80 and 40 plies (Fig. 28-2, right). A real cut plan must consider marker length, the ply limit, the labour time for each new lay and the size ratio together, and several alternatives are usually compared. Today’s automatic cutters hold the plies down by vacuum and cut with a reciprocating knife, and automatic marker-making software can compare many layouts in a few minutes, but the logic of cut planning is unchanged.

28.3 Operation breakdown

Before the cut parts enter the sewing floor, two questions must be answered: which operations does this garment need, and in what order? Dividing a garment, in processing order, into individual operations is called operation breakdown. How finely to divide depends on the production system: a long progressive line uses a fine breakdown, where an operation may be just “sew one straight seam”; cell-based production uses a coarser breakdown, so that one person can do several operations in succession. For each operation, the breakdown must state the machine (lockstitch, overlock, bartacker…), the stitch type and stitch length and the quality requirements, and also its predecessors — the operations that must be finished before it can be done.

Drawing the operations and their precedence relations gives the operation flow chart (Fig. 28-3). In this chapter the casual trousers are broken down into 23 operations. Three components — back panels, front panels and fly — are each made first: the back panels need overlocking, darts, back-pocket welting, pocket-bag stitching and bartacking; the front panels need overlocking, slant pockets and topstitching; the fly needs overlocking, zip setting and topstitching. Once the three components are ready they come together for assembly: joining front and back rise, joining the side seams, topstitching the side seams, joining the inseam, attaching the waistband, finishing the waistband ends, attaching belt loops, hemming the legs, buttonholing, button sewing and finally thread trimming and inspection. The flow chart tells us two things: which operations can be done in parallel (the three components are independent of each other) and which must come later (the waistband cannot be attached before the side seams are joined). When operations are assigned in the next section, the precedence relations are the constraints.

The time of each operation in the figure is this chapter’s worked-example value; the total is 13.20 min/piece. For a sense of scale, a published operation list for basic five-pocket jeans has 30 operations totalling 11.65 min (Online Clothing Study); an operation list for chino trousers breaks the work down more finely, into 83 operations totalling 36.86 min, including many helper and pressing operations (ORDNUR). For the same kind of product, differences in style and in how finely the operations are divided can make times differ several-fold, so factories generally use their own measured data.

28.4 Standard minute value

Time study, rating and allowances　The standard minute value (SAM, also called SMV) is the time a skilled operator needs to complete an operation by the prescribed method at normal pace, plus the necessary allowances. As Chapter 29 explains, it is the common yardstick a factory uses to calculate output, set headcount and price labour. The most common way to determine it is stopwatch time study, in three steps (Fig. 28-4, left).

Step 1, observation. Choose a skilled operator, have them work by the prescribed method, time several to a dozen or more cycles and take the average. Worked example: “topstitch side seams” is timed 10 times, averaging 0.48 min.

Step 2, rating. The operator being timed may be faster or slower than “normal pace”, and the time-study engineer rates their pace from experience, with normal pace = 100%. This operator works briskly and is rated at 110%; multiplying the observed time by the rating factor gives the basic time, 0.48 × 1.10 = 0.528 min. Rating is the step in time study that depends most on experience; time-study engineers need training and often calibrate their judgement against videos of standard motions.

Step 3, allowances. No operator can work non-stop all day: they need to drink, go to the toilet and get tired; the machine needs thread changes, bobbin changes and thread-break handling. These times are added as percentages of the basic time, called allowances. Values commonly found in published sources are: personal allowance 5%, fatigue allowance 4%, machine allowance 5% for lockstitch and chainstitch machines and 6% for overlock machines and bartackers (Apparel Resources). The worked example uses 14%, giving a standard minute value of 0.528 × 1.14 ≈ 0.60 min, i.e. 36 s.

Besides stopwatch time study there is a family of methods called predetermined motion time systems: an operation is broken down into basic motions such as “reach, grasp, align, sew 30 cm, set aside”, each with a standard time measured in advance, and the operation time is obtained by looking these up and adding them. No operator has to perform the operation first, so times can be estimated before a new style goes into production; there are several complete systems internationally, and the apparel industry has its own versions (such as GSD).

Machine time and manual time　The time of a sewing operation divides into the time the machine is sewing and the time the operator spends picking up, aligning and setting aside the parts. Published sources often state that sewing operators spend about 80% of their time picking up parts, positioning them on the machine, guiding the fabric under the needle and setting the parts aside (Apparel Resources); this figure counts the time spent guiding fabric while the machine runs as manual time. If one looks only at the share of the operation time during which the machine actually runs, it varies greatly between operations, from roughly 10–20% to about 70%. Either way, it shows one thing: a large part of the operation time is not determined by the machine.

So how much does a sewing machine’s maximum speed affect the operation time? Chapter 14 worked out the speed profile of a seam: a slow start for the first 3 stitches, acceleration to maximum speed, deceleration near the end, the last stitch sewn at trimming speed, then trimming and needle positioning. The same model can be used to calculate seams of different stitch counts (Fig. 28-4, right). “Topstitch side seams” involves two seams of about 100 cm each; at a stitch length of 3 mm each has about 333 stitches. At a maximum speed of 4000 r/min one seam takes about 5.75 s, at 5000 r/min about 4.80 s; for the two seams together this saves 1.9 s, only 5% of the 36 s operation. For a short seam of 40 stitches, raising the speed from 4000 to 6000 r/min saves only 0.10 s, because the slow start and the trimming stop take just as long as before. Moreover, a long seam requires the operator to guide the fabric, and the operator may not keep the pedal fully down all the time. This confirms the conclusion of Chapter 14: for most garment operations, raising the maximum speed further yields very little; shortening acceleration, deceleration and trimming time, and making pick-up and alignment easier (for example by adding guides, folders and automatic feeding devices), has a greater effect on the operation time.

28.5 Line balancing

Takt time and balance efficiency　With the standard minute value of every operation known, the line can be laid out. First the takt time — how often, on average, one piece must be completed — is calculated from the target output:

\text{takt time}=\frac{60}{\text{target output (pieces/h)}}\ \text{(min/piece)},\qquad N_0=\left\lceil\frac{\sum \text{SAM}}{\text{takt time}}\right\rceil

N₀ is the theoretical minimum number of operators, and ⌈ ⌉ denotes rounding up. The target output of the casual-trousers line is set at 120 pieces/h, so the takt time is 0.5 min; with a total work content of 13.20 min, at least 27 operators are needed in theory.

Next the operations are assigned to operators. The time assigned to each operator is their load. The output of the whole line is determined by the most heavily loaded operator: until they finish a piece, everyone downstream has to wait. The load of the most heavily loaded operator is called the line’s cycle time (i.e. the bottleneck time), and actual output = 60 ÷ cycle time. How evenly the work is spread is measured by the balance efficiency:

\text{balance efficiency}=\frac{\sum \text{SAM}}{N\times \text{cycle time}}

N is the actual number of operators. If every operator’s load exactly equals the cycle time, the balance efficiency is 100%; 1 − balance efficiency is called the balance loss, i.e. the share of the line’s total labour time spent waiting.

Why one operation per group does not work　The simplest assignment is one operation per group, with extra operators on operations whose time exceeds the takt time: 2 operators on the 0.9-min “slant pockets”, 3 on the 1.2-min “back-pocket welting”. But headcounts must be whole numbers: the 0.55-min “topstitch fly” exceeds the takt time only slightly yet still needs 2 operators, each with only 0.275 min of work, so they wait half the time; and the 0.2-min buttonholing still occupies a whole operator. With this assignment the trousers line needs 38 operators and the balance efficiency is only 69.5% (Fig. 28-5, left).

Shared groups　The improvement is to combine several adjacent operations into a group shared by several operators. For example, the three operations “overlock front panels, overlock back panels, sew back darts” total 1.30 min; with 3 operators each carries 0.433 min. Operators within a group can divide the work by pieces: one overlocks the front panels and then some of the back panels, and whoever is free helps with the next operation. In this way 0.3 of an operator’s work no longer occupies a whole person, and the odd fractions of time are pooled into whole operators. Two shop-floor rules apply when forming groups: a group must not have too many operators, or coordination within it breaks down (this chapter allows at most 4); and a group must not use too many types of machine, because every operator in the group must be able to use all of them and the machines must be placed together (this chapter allows at most 2, not counting manual work such as thread trimming). Precedence relations must also be respected: the group containing an operation must come after, or be the same as, the groups containing all its predecessors.

What is the best grouping? A common approach is the ranked positional weight method: the “positional weight” of an operation is its own time plus the times of all operations that follow it; an operation with a large weight has a lot of work queued behind it and should be scheduled early. The operations are arranged in a sequence in descending order of positional weight (while respecting precedence), and this sequence is then cut into segments, one group per segment (in this chapter, at most 4 operations per group). Where to cut can be decided by dynamic programming, which compares all possible cuts and picks the one with the fewest operators and the shortest cycle time. Grouped this way, the trousers line needs only 29 operators with a balance efficiency of 91.0% (Fig. 28-5, right), just 2 more than the theoretical minimum. The order given by positional weights is not necessarily the best: swapping a few operations without violating precedence — for example moving “make belt loops” forward into a group with some of the overlocking operations — achieves 28 operators and a balance efficiency of 94.3%. When the takt time changes　When the target output changes, the takt time changes, and a grouping that suited the old takt time may no longer suit the new one. For a target of 150 pieces/h and a takt time of 0.4 min, automatic grouping needs 36 operators at 91.7% balance efficiency; for 100 pieces/h, 25 operators at 91.8%. Because the way operation times “round up” against the takt time has changed, the groups must be re-formed; this is also why the line must be rebalanced after a style change.

Relation to Chapter 29　The balance above is a theoretical value based on standard minute values. On a real floor, different operators do the same operation at different speeds, the same operator is faster or slower at different times of day, and some work in progress is needed between operations as a buffer — otherwise, if one operator slows down for a moment, everyone downstream stops. The three ways of organising production discussed in Chapter 29 deal with this variation differently: the progressive bundle system moves a bundle at a time, has a lot of WIP that absorbs the variation, and has long throughput times; hanger single-piece flow limits how many pieces hang in front of each workstation, keeping WIP low, and can dispatch work automatically to whichever station is free — in effect rebalancing the line continuously while it runs (Chapter 12); and in a U-shaped cell the relay zones let operators adjust the division of work themselves, so faster operators naturally do a little more. Virtual lab 29-1 in Chapter 29 compares them by simulation. The balance calculation of this chapter is the common starting point of all these methods.

28.6 Lean production

Lean production originates in the Toyota Production System; the name “lean production” was coined by American researchers around 1990 to summarise it, and its core is identifying and eliminating waste. Waste is any activity that does not add value to the product. Seven kinds are commonly cited in garment factories: overproduction (making more than the order or making it far too early), waiting (operators waiting for work or for machines), transport (moving cut parts back and forth between workstations), over-processing (sewing an extra line of stitching beyond what is required), inventory (WIP on the line and in the warehouse), unnecessary motion (reaching too far, searching for things) and rework (faulty pieces that must be unpicked and redone). A sewing machine adds value to a garment only during the few seconds it is sewing; the rest of the time may all be waste.

Several common practices relate to the sewing floor as follows.

WIP and flow　Chapter 29 uses Little’s law to show that the more WIP there is, the longer a garment spends on the line. WIP also hides problems: if one workstation makes a faulty batch, it may not be discovered until hundreds of pieces later. Lean production calls for small batches or even single-piece flow; once WIP is reduced, problems surface immediately, which forces people to solve them.

Standard work　The best way of doing each operation is fixed: where the parts are placed, which hand picks them up, how they are aligned and where finished pieces go. This is written up as a work instruction with pictures or video, and new operators learn from it. Standard minute values are measured on the basis of standard work; if the method changes, the time must be measured again.

Quick changeover　At a style change, the time spent adjusting machines and changing thread, presser feet and guides is lost line time. The remedy is to do in advance whatever preparation can be done before the line stops (thread and attachments ready), and to shorten what must be done with the line stopped (quick couplings, recalling electronic stitch-length and electronic differential-feed settings from a program, Chapter 14; on programmable pattern sewers, changing the clamp changes the pattern, Chapter 15). As calculated in Section 29.5 of Chapter 29, in small-batch quick response, line stoppage for style changes is the biggest single loss.

Built-in quality　Instead of relying on final inspection to pick out defects, quality is assured at every operation: first-piece inspection, self-inspection by operators with checking by the next operation, and stopping to solve a problem on the spot when it is found. Thread-break detection and stitch monitoring on sewing machines (Chapter 26) follow the same idea.

Visual management and 5S　The state of the floor is made visible at a glance: planned and actual output of each workstation is displayed at the head of the line, and a light comes on when a machine is in fault; tools and materials have fixed places. 5S stands for sort, set in order, shine, standardise and sustain; their Japanese romanised forms (seiri, seiton, seisō, seiketsu, shitsuke) all begin with S. It means clearing away what is not needed, giving what is needed a fixed place, keeping things clean, and making all this a habit. It may look trivial, but it is the most direct way to reduce “searching for things” and “unnecessary motion”.

28.7 Equipment provisioning and the machine-to-operator ratio

How many machines of each type　Once the line is balanced, we know which machines each group uses and how many operators it has, so the number of machines of each type can be calculated. Every operator in a group must have access to every type of machine in the group, but not necessarily one each: if a group of 3 operators shares 1.2 min of lockstitch operations at a takt time of 0.5 min, ⌈1.2 ÷ 0.5⌉ = 3 lockstitch machines are needed; if the same group also has a 0.2-min bartacking operation, one bartacker is enough, used by the three in turn. With the grouping of Fig. 28-5 (right) and a target of 120 pieces/h, the 29-operator trousers line needs 20 lockstitch machines, 8 overlock machines, 3 bartackers and 1 each of chainstitch machine, buttonholer and button sewer — 34 machines in all. The ratio of machines to operators (machines/operator) is called the machine-to-operator ratio, here 1.17 (some factories write it the other way round, as operators per machine). In addition, 5%–10% spare machines should be kept so that a broken machine can be replaced at once and a single machine failure does not stop the whole line; at a style change, spare machines can also be set up for the new style in advance.

A machine-to-operator ratio above 1 is normal: when a group has two machine types, an operator can switch between two machines just by turning round. A ratio that is too high means many machines stand idle and the money spent on them is tied up on the floor; one that is too low means the division of work is too coarse and operators queue for machines.

How many automatic machines one person can tend　Automatic machines such as programmable pattern sewers, template sewing machines and automatic pocket-welting machines have one feature in common: the operator loads the parts, the machine sews a complete cycle by itself, and the operator need not stand by during that time (Chapters 12 and 15). So one person can tend several machines: load the first, walk to the second and load it, then the third… and when the operator gets back to the first, it has just finished. If each machine takes a min to unload and load, walking between machines takes w min, the machine sews automatically for t min, and one person tends n machines, the cycle time is

C=\max\big[\,n(a+w),\ a+t\,\big]

The first term is the time for the operator to make one round, the second is one machine’s cycle time, and whichever is longer governs. When n is small, the operator gets round quickly and has to wait for the machines to finish; when n is large, finished machines have to wait for the operator to load them. The number of machines at which the two are exactly equal is n* = (a + t) ÷ (a + w).

Worked example (Fig. 28-6): a = 0.3 min, w = 0.05 min, t = 0.9 min, n* = 1.2 ÷ 0.35 ≈ 3.43. Tending 3 machines, the cycle time is 1.2 min, the machines never stop, and the operator waits 13% of the time; tending 4 machines, the cycle time is 1.4 min, the operator never stops, and each machine waits 14% of the time. Which is cheaper depends on whether people or machines cost more. With wages of 0.5 yuan/min and equipment (depreciation, maintenance, electricity) at 0.08 yuan/min per machine, the cost per piece = (wage + n × equipment rate) × C ÷ n, which is 0.296 yuan for 3 machines and 0.287 yuan for 4, so 4 is slightly cheaper. If the equipment were more expensive, the cheapest number of machines would shift downwards.

There is one point that is easy to get wrong: when the machines become faster, one person can tend fewer of them, not more. If the automatic sewing time is cut to 0.6 min (for example by raising the speed of the pattern sewer), n* falls to 2.57 and the cheapest number of machines changes from 4 to 3. This is because, with faster machines, the operator’s loading and walking time becomes more prominent. So when the speed of automatic machines is raised, ways should also be found to shorten loading time — for example using two clamps alternately, one sewing on the machine while the other is loaded outside it. 28.8 Case: improving the balance of a casual-trousers line

The technical problem　This is a generic case constructed from first principles; all figures are worked-example values. A factory’s casual-trousers line has 26 operators and a planned output of 120 pieces/h, but never achieves it; even on standard minute values it can only make about 86 pieces. The floor manager’s first reaction was to add operators; the industrial engineers first carried out a time study and a line-balance analysis.

Analysis　The original assignment had been set by experience: essentially one operation per group, with an extra operator wherever things seemed busy (Fig. 28-7, left). The balance chart made the problem obvious at once: “set zip” and “join inseam” each take 0.70 min and each has only 1 operator, so they drag the cycle time up to 0.70 min and the whole line can make only 60 ÷ 0.70 ≈ 85.7 pieces/h; at the other end, bartacking the back-pocket openings and making belt loops take 0.25 min each, buttonholing plus button sewing 0.4 min, and these operators wait half the time. The balance efficiency is 72.5%. Extra operators can only help at the bottleneck, but once each bottleneck operation gets one more operator, the next bottleneck appears immediately: attaching the waistband has 2 operators at 0.65 min each, and there are five further operations of 0.6 min, all exceeding the 0.5-min takt time.

Improvement　Step 1: regroup with the same equipment. Using the method of Section 28.5, adjacent operations are combined into groups and the odd fractions of time are pooled: with the same 26 operators, the cycle time falls to 0.55 min, output rises to 109 pieces/h, and the balance efficiency is 92.3% (Fig. 28-7, centre). With almost no equipment investment (only rearranging workstations and training), output rises by 27%. Reaching 120 pieces/h with the same equipment would need 29 operators.

Step 2: look at the operations that take longest and are hardest to split. “Back-pocket welting” at 1.20 min and “attach waistband” at 1.30 min are the two most time-consuming operations on the trousers, both relying on manual positioning and repeated adjustment by the operator. Switching back-pocket welting to an automatic pocket-welting machine — the operator only places the panel and pocket bag, and the machine sews and cuts the pocket opening automatically — cuts the manual time to about 0.45 min; switching waistband attaching to a waistband machine with a folder, which folds and feeds the waistband automatically, cuts it to about 0.80 min (both worked-example values). The total work content falls from 13.20 min to 11.95 min, and after regrouping, 26 operators achieve 120 pieces/h at a balance efficiency of 91.9% (Fig. 28-7, right). The number of machines falls from 34 (the same-equipment, 29-operator scheme) to 31, including 3 special machines of the two types and 6 fewer lockstitch machines. With further reordering of the operations, 25 operators can also achieve 120 pieces/h (a task in Virtual lab 28-1).

The business case　Is buying the special machines worthwhile? Compared with “same equipment, increase to 29 operators”, step 2 saves 3 operators. With labour at 0.5 yuan/min (including social insurance and overheads; worked-example value), 25 days a month and 8 hours a day, 3 operators cost about 18,000 yuan a month. If buying 3 special machines, after deducting the 6 lockstitch machines no longer needed, means a net extra investment of 200,000 yuan (worked-example value), it pays back in about 11 months — not counting the more consistent quality the special machines bring. Conversely, if this line changes styles frequently so that the special machines often go unused, or if the styles have no back pockets at all, the sums do not work out. Equipment investment must therefore be considered together with the product mix and order batch sizes — which is exactly why the equipment of the five kinds of factory in Chapter 29 differs so much.

After the improvement, follow-up is still needed: is actual output close to the theoretical value, have operators adapted to the new groups, what is the failure rate of the special machines? These data feed into the next round of time study and balancing, for continuous improvement.

28.9 Limits of the model

The operations and times, allowance percentages, wages and equipment rates in this chapter are all worked-example values. The line-balancing model assumes that the operations in a group can be split by pieces in any proportion and that operators within a group are equally capable; output is calculated from standard minute values, without considering speed variation between and within operators, thread-break stoppages, WIP buffers or learning, so actual output must be multiplied by the floor efficiency and is usually well below the theoretical value. Automatic grouping compares only one operation sequence, the one given by positional weights; changing the sequence may give a better grouping. “At most 4 operators, 4 operations and 2 machine types per group” are rules set for illustration. The multi-machine model assumes fixed loading, walking and automatic sewing times. The time for one seam uses the speed profile of Chapter 14 and assumes the operator always presses up to maximum speed. Fabric utilisation and cut planning are illustrative. All of these need to be calibrated with measured factory data.

Shop-floor notes

  • If output will not rise, first draw the balance chart to find the bottleneck, then decide whether to add operators or redistribute the work; an operator added in the wrong place just adds one more person waiting for work.
  • After a style change or a change of sewing method, re-measure the standard minute values and rebalance the line.
  • Combine small, fragmentary operations for one operator, and let several operators share large, time-consuming ones; machines in the same group should be placed together, with few machine types.
  • In multi-machine tending, either the operator or the machines will always be waiting; choose the number of machines by comparing their costs. When machines are speeded up, also find ways to shorten loading time.
  • Work out equipment investments together with the product mix and batch sizes; special machines save money only on styles that can use them.
