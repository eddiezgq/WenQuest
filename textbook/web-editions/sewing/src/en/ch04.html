Chapter 4The Thread Take-up Mechanism

Interactive resources in this chapter3D modelIndustrial lockstitch machineVirtual labTake-up mechanism and thread supply

The needle mechanism of Chapter 3 carries the needle thread below the fabric; the rotary hook of Chapter 5 catches the loop and carries it around the bobbin case. Both consume needle thread, and very unevenly: within one stitch there are moments when no thread is needed at all, moments when nearly eighty millimetres are needed at once, and a little later all the surplus has to be pulled back up. The part that handles this is the take-up lever, rocking up and down without pause at the front of the machine head. This chapter first explains what the take-up mechanism has to do and what forms it takes. A worked example of a take-up mechanism is then used to calculate and plot the design rule “the lever must pay out enough thread for the loop, but not so much that it cannot be taken back up”. Finally you will adjust a take-up yourself in the virtual lab.

4.1 Opening: how much thread does one stitch use?

There are several faults on the shop floor for which the mechanic suspects the take-up first: frequent thread breaks after switching to a heavier fabric; loose loops of needle thread on the underside of the fabric (called “floating” or “looping underneath”); stitches that are tight one moment and loose the next; and needle thread that snaps audibly at high speed. Their causes differ, but they all come back to one question: at every instant of a stitch, how much needle thread is needed, and how much is the take-up mechanism supplying?

Start with the total. A thread manufacturer’s consumption guide states that a 301 lockstitch uses roughly 2.5 cm of thread per 1 cm of seam, split equally between needle and bobbin thread; this assumes 7 stitches per centimetre, and the shop should add another 10%–15% for wastage (a thread manufacturer’s consumption guide). Converted, only about two millimetres of needle thread actually stays in the fabric per stitch. Yet while that stitch is being formed, the loop is enlarged by the hook and carried around the entire bobbin case, and at that moment the needle side needs almost eighty millimetres of thread (worked example in this chapter). All that thread must first be “borrowed” from the take-up lever and, once the loop has left the bobbin case, “returned”, leaving in the fabric only the few millimetres actually used. The take-up mechanism is the mechanism that lends and recovers thread according to the main-shaft angle.

4.2 The task of the take-up and the needle-thread path

Fig. 4-1 shows the needle-thread path from the thread stand to the needle. The thread comes down from the cone and first passes through the tension assembly, where two tension discs pressed together by a spring put tension on the needle thread. It then wraps around the check spring, a fine steel-wire spring that takes up slack when the thread is loose and is pulled against its stop when the thread is tight. Next it passes the thread guide into the take-up lever eye, then runs down via the lower guide point, the face-plate guide and the needle-bar thread guide, and finally through the needle eye. As the take-up lever eye moves up and down, the combined length of the two spans thread guide–eye and eye–lower guide point changes; this change is the thread paid out or taken back by the take-up mechanism.

Within each stitch, the take-up mechanism performs three tasks in turn.

The first is paying out. After the needle has pierced the fabric, more and more needle thread lies below the fabric; once the hook has caught the loop, the loop is drawn larger and larger until it passes around the bobbin case. Throughout this period the take-up lever must keep descending to let thread down, and it must do so in time: if it pays out too little, the loop cannot open and the hook has to force it, the needle-thread tension rises sharply, and the result is at best a tightened previous stitch and uneven stitching, at worst a thread break.

The second is taking up. When the loop has slipped off the bobbin case, a large loop of surplus thread hangs below the fabric. The take-up lever must rise quickly to draw this loop back and pull the interlacing point of needle and bobbin thread into the middle of the fabric thickness, setting the stitch (Chapter 2, Section 2.4).

The third is drawing thread. After the stitch is set, the take-up lever continues a little further upward. The stitch in the fabric is now fixed, so the lever can only pull thread from the other end: the needle thread slides through the tension assembly, and the length needed for the next stitch is drawn off. This is the moment at which the tension assembly acts, and it determines how tightly the stitch is set.

Of the three, paying out is the hardest, because the amount required is dictated by the motion of the needle and the hook and the take-up lever can only follow. The heart of take-up design is therefore to make the lever’s thread supply curve “envelop” the thread demand curve of the needle and loop throughout the pay-out phase; this is the subject of Section 4.5.

The check spring is the buffer on this path. At times the take-up lever pays out thread faster than the needle consumes it. If nothing controls the surplus, it hangs loosely in front of the head, where the needle may pierce it or it may catch on other parts; the check spring takes up this slack. One manufacturer’s service manual specifies that the check-spring motion should be completed as the needle point enters the fabric, with a stroke of about 7 mm, and that while the loop is passing around the hook and is at its largest, the check spring should move only about 1 mm (a manufacturer’s service manual). These two rules tie the check spring to the motions of needle and hook: its stroke must match the thread the lever lets down in advance before the needle point enters the fabric, and at the moment the loop is largest there must be hardly any slack left in the thread path.

4.3 Types of take-up mechanism

Classified by how the take-up lever is driven, take-up mechanisms take a few basic forms (Fig. 4-2).

Cam take-up　A grooved cam sits on the arm shaft; a roller at one end of the take-up lever runs in the groove, and as the cam turns it rocks the lever. Its advantage is that the supply curve can be designed almost at will: dwells and fast sections wherever you like can be built into the groove shape. Its drawback is the clearance between roller and groove, which causes impact, wear and noise at high speed. The cam type is simple and cheap and is used mainly in slow-running domestic sewing machines (technical notes from a sewing-machine parts manufacturer).

Link take-up　The take-up lever is itself the coupler of a four-bar linkage: one end is mounted on the take-up crank pin of the arm-shaft crank, the middle is pinned to a rocker whose other end pivots on the housing, and the thread eye is at the front end of the lever. The mechanism has only revolute joints and no sliding grooves, so it has low wear, low noise, long life and can run at high speed; this is why industrial lockstitch machines almost universally use the link type (abstract of a study on the kinematics of industrial sewing-machine take-up mechanisms (in Chinese)). The price is that the supply curve must be chosen from among the coupler curves of a four-bar linkage rather than designed freely, so it usually pays out extra thread to be sure there is enough. The worked example in this chapter is a link take-up.

Sliding-lever take-up　One end of the take-up lever is driven by a crank pin, and the lever body passes through a swinging slider block, so the lever rocks while sliding in the block. The path of its eye is flatter and more elongated than that of a four-bar linkage; the design is compact and is also used on high-speed machines.

Rotary take-up　A uniformly rotating, specially shaped take-up disc replaces the reciprocating lever. The thread runs around the edge of the disc between two fixed guide points; when a lobe of the disc turns towards the thread it pushes the thread out, and as it turns away it lets the thread back. Because there is only uniform rotation and no reciprocating impact, it suits high speeds. A 1953 US patent describes such a disc, whose two opposite edges respectively set the stitch and slacken the needle thread (US 2,636,464). A 1996 patent notes that an ordinary take-up lever pays out far more thread than the hook actually needs, and that the excess makes stitches uneven and causes thread breaks at high speed; it reduces the excess by varying the rotation radii of the two thread-holding points on the disc edge (US 5,555,830).

Two other approaches are also common. On overlock and coverstitch machines, the needle-thread take-up is mostly achieved by a guide mounted on the needle bar working with fixed guides on the housing, while the looper threads have their own take-up cams or take-up plates (Chapters 9 and 10). In recent years some manufacturers have also driven the take-up mechanism of high-speed lockstitch machines directly with a stepper motor, so that the amount and timing of thread supply can be set to suit the fabric, and so that the take-up lever no longer carries lubricating oil out of the housing (Light Industry Machinery, 2021, No. 4 (in Chinese)); this belongs to the electrical control of Part V.

4.4 Kinematic analysis of the link take-up

Coordinates and dimensions　Looking along the arm shaft from the left end of the head (Fig. 4-1), take the arm-shaft centre O as origin, the z axis horizontal to the right and the y axis vertically upward, units mm. The take-up crank pin P has crank radius r; the rocker’s frame pivot is Q and the rocker length is b; on the take-up lever, the distance from P to the rocker pivot M is a; the thread eye E lies on the lever at distance c from P, offset from line PM by angle α. The main-shaft angle φ is again zero when the needle bar is at its highest point, and the phase difference between the take-up crank pin and the needle-bar crank pin is δ (δ = 0 means the take-up pin is exactly opposite the needle-bar crank pin). The worked-example dimensions are listed in Table 4-1. They are a set of values optimised for teaching and do not correspond to any particular machine model.

Position solution　The position of the crank pin follows directly from the main-shaft angle:

\[P = \big(-r\sin(\varphi+\delta),\; -r\cos(\varphi+\delta)\big)\]

Point M lies at distance a from P and distance b from Q, so M is an intersection of two circles centred at P and Q. Let d = |PQ|, let u be the unit vector along PQ, and let n be u rotated 90° anticlockwise. Then

\[p=\frac{a^{2}-b^{2}+d^{2}}{2d},\qquad h=\sqrt{a^{2}-p^{2}},\qquad M = P + p\,\mathbf{u} + h\,\mathbf{n}\]

The two intersections of the circles correspond to the two assembly modes of the mechanism; the worked example takes the +h branch. If a² − p² < 0 at some angle, the circles do not intersect and the mechanism cannot be assembled at that angle, which the design must avoid. The eye position is obtained by rotating the direction PM through α and extending it to length c:

\[E = P + \frac{c}{a}\,R(\alpha)\,(M-P)\]

R(α) denotes the rotation matrix for angle α. Computing E every 1° gives the path of the eye over one revolution (Fig. 4-3, left). How well the four-bar linkage transmits force is measured by the transmission angle μ, the angle between the take-up lever and the rocker at M:

\[\cos\mu=\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}-d^{2}}{2ab}\]

The closer μ is to 90°, the more easily the rocker is driven; if μ is too small or too large, only a small part of the driving force goes into moving the rocker, and the joints are heavily loaded and wear quickly. In engineering practice μ is generally kept at no less than about 40°.

Worked-example results　For φ = 0°, P = (2.45, −20.55), giving M = (45.01, −12.03), E = (66.16, −4.52) and a transmission angle of 43.6°; for φ = 150°, E = (46.30, −12.93) and the transmission angle is 114.0°. Over one revolution the transmission angle stays between 41.0° and 114.8°. Geometrically the eye is highest at φ ≈ 39° and lowest at 288°, a vertical difference of 38.4 mm (Fig. 4-3).

What matters for the thread path, however, is not the height of the eye but the combined length of the two spans thread guide–eye and eye–lower guide point:

\[L(\varphi)=|AE|+|EB|\]

When L is largest the take-up lever has drawn the thread tightest; when it is smallest the most thread has been let down. In the worked example L is largest at φ = 55° (210.2 mm) and smallest at 293° (128.6 mm), a difference of 81.6 mm: this is the most thread the take-up lever can pay out in one stitch. These angles differ by about 16° and 5° from the geometric highest and lowest points of the eye, because neither guide point lies directly below the eye. Taking up from 293° to 55° takes only about 120°, while paying out from 55° to 293° takes about 240°: slow pay-out and fast take-up, which is exactly the rhythm the task requires.

4.5 Thread supply and thread demand

Thread supply　Measured from the moment of tightest take-up, the thread let down by the take-up lever is

\[S(\varphi)=L_{\max}-L(\varphi)\]

For example, at φ = 150°, L = 189.4 mm and S = 210.2 − 189.4 = 20.8 mm.

Thread demand　Now consider how much thread the needle and hook side requires. The zero is again the needle bar at its highest point, and the thread is treated as inextensible.

Before loop catch there is only one change on the needle side: once the needle eye passes through the fabric surface, the thread below the fabric forms two strands, “down and back”, each as long as the depth of the eye below the fabric surface. Let the needle-bar displacement be x(φ) (Chapter 3), and let the eye be a height e₀ above the fabric surface when the bar is at its highest (in the worked example the eye is 20 mm above the throat plate and the fabric thickness is t = 1.5 mm, so e₀ = 18.5 mm). Then

\[D(\varphi)=2\max\big(0,\;x(\varphi)-e_{0}\big)\]

While the eye is still above the fabric surface, whatever length the descending needle bar adds to the thread above is taken away from the span between eye and fabric surface; the two cancel, so D = 0 during this time. In the worked example the needle point enters the fabric at φ ≈ 102° (Chapter 3), the eye enters the fabric at φ ≈ 109°, and at bottom dead centre D = 2 × (31 − 18.5) = 25 mm.

After loop catch, the thread below the fabric becomes a loop pulled by the hook point, with a complex shape. This chapter estimates it with a simplified geometric model (Fig. 4-4): viewed along the hook axis, the loop is stretched between the fabric hole F, the hook point H and the arc of bobbin-case rim the hook point has already swept, plus the needle eye while it is still below the fabric; its length is taken as the perimeter C(φ) of the convex hull of these points. This choice is consistent with the pre-catch formula: the two “down and back” strands before catch are exactly the perimeter of the “convex hull” of the two points fabric hole and needle eye; and while the eye is above the fabric surface, whatever length the needle-bar motion adds above is taken from the span between eye and fabric hole, so it does not affect the demand. In this model, therefore, from the eye entering the fabric until the hook point reaches its lowest position, the demand equals this perimeter:

\[D(\varphi)=C(\varphi),\qquad 109^{\circ}\le\varphi\le 296^{\circ}\]

The loop is largest when the hook point reaches its lowest position (φ ≈ 296°). After that the loop, held open by the bobbin case, slides towards the far side of the case and the take-up lever begins to take up; at cast-off (about 341°) the loop leaves the bobbin case, and the thread required drops to the length actually used by this stitch:

\[q \approx s + t\]

Here s is the stitch length and t the fabric thickness: the interlacing point lies at mid-thickness, so the needle thread must travel one stitch length per stitch and also go down through half the thickness and back up again. With s = 3 mm and t = 1.5 mm in the worked example, q ≈ 4.5 mm. Under the conditions of the thread manufacturer’s data (7 stitches per centimetre, s ≈ 1.43 mm), this formula reproduces the needle-thread consumption of 1.25 times the seam length if the fabric is about 0.36 mm thick, i.e. a lightweight fabric; the order of magnitude agrees.

At loop catch D = 21.0 mm; when the hook point has turned 88° (φ = 250°), D = 55.0 mm; with the hook point at its lowest, D = 79.6 mm.

Thread surplus and three criteria　Plot S and D on the same graph (Fig. 4-5). Their difference

\[\Delta(\varphi)=S(\varphi)-D(\varphi)\]

is called the thread surplus. Δ > 0 means the take-up lever has let down more thread than is needed, and the excess must be held by the check spring; Δ < 0 means too little has been let down, and thread can only be dragged from elsewhere. Fig. 4-5 should be read in three phases, in time order.

Phase one runs from tightest take-up (55°) to loop catch (206°) and has two sub-phases. Before the eye enters the fabric (109°), the needle side uses no thread at all, and all the thread let down by the lever must be held by the check spring, so 0 ≤ Δ ≤ check-spring stroke. In the worked example the surplus is about 7.6 mm when the needle point enters the fabric (102°) and reaches its maximum of 9.2 mm when the eye enters; the check-spring stroke is taken as 10 mm. After the eye enters the fabric, the needle starts using thread and the surplus gradually falls, reaching a minimum of +1.1 mm around 152°. The criterion here is that the surplus must not become appreciably negative; this chapter uses a shortfall of no more than 1 mm. A small shortfall can be absorbed by the elastic stretch of the thread; any more, and needle thread is pulled out of the tension assembly while the needle is still in the fabric, the thread tension jumps and the loop has difficulty opening. After bottom dead centre the rising needle gives thread back while the lever is still paying out, so the surplus grows rapidly, reaching 23.8 mm at loop catch: this thread is the loop bulging out above the needle eye, which is exactly what the hook point must catch.

Phase two runs from loop catch to cast-off (206°–341°). This is the most critical phase, and the criterion is Δ ≥ 0, with a margin of 1–2 mm. In the worked example the surplus is about 24 mm at loop catch and then falls gradually as the loop grows, reaching its minimum of 1.9 mm when the hook point reaches its lowest position (296°). This is the tightest moment of the whole cycle, which is precisely why the service manual specifies the check-spring motion at this moment.

Phase three runs from cast-off to the next tightest take-up (341° → 55°). The loop slips off, the demand drops abruptly to q, and the surplus suddenly becomes large; the take-up lever must recover all of it before the needle of the next stitch pierces the fabric. Approaching 55°, S → 0 while this stitch still uses q = 4.5 mm more, so the surplus approaches −4.5 mm: this is the moment at which the take-up lever draws the next stitch’s thread from the tension assembly (“drawing thread” in Section 4.2).

A good take-up mechanism satisfies all three phases within one revolution and does not pay out too much extra thread in phase two. The more excess it pays out, the looser the loop, the more easily it twists and is caught a second time by the hook point, and the more uneven the stitches. The worked example pays out up to about 24 mm of extra thread while the loop passes around the case; this is just the problem identified by the patent cited in Section 4.3: a link take-up lever pays out far more thread than the hook needs.

4.6 Adjustment and design notes

Adjust the thread guide when changing fabric　With thicker fabric, the two strands below the fabric get longer and the fabric hole is further from the bobbin case, so the loop is larger and the whole demand curve rises. In the worked example, changing the fabric thickness from 1.5 mm to 3 mm changes the minimum margin from loop catch to cast-off from +1.9 mm to −0.9 mm, and after the eye enters the fabric there is a 1.9 mm shortfall: the loop does not get enough thread. The thread guide can then be moved along its adjusting slot in the “more pay-out” direction: the incoming thread then runs more nearly in line with the eye’s motion, so for the same travel of the eye the path length changes more and more thread is let down. A sewing-machine instruction manual puts it this way: for heavy materials move the thread guide to the left to increase the amount of thread drawn by the take-up lever; for light materials move it to the right to reduce it (a manufacturer’s instruction manual). In the worked example, moving the thread guide 12 mm down its slot (along the 285° direction in this chapter’s coordinates; the manual’s “left” and “right” are as seen by the operator facing the machine, and only the idea is borrowed here) restores the minimum margin to 1.4 mm, reduces the shortfall to 0.6 mm, and leaves a surplus of 8.6 mm before the eye enters the fabric, still within the check-spring stroke. If the check-spring stroke has been set too small, it must be adjusted together with the thread guide; in that same manual these two adjustments are, appropriately, described side by side.

Loop-catch timing affects the demand curve　The shape of the demand curve is set by the moment of loop catch. If the hook point arrives early (say at 198°), the thread let down by the lever is used by the loop straight away: the excess while passing around the case falls from 23.8 mm to 18.0 mm and the minimum margin is still 1.7 mm. If it arrives late (214°), the lever has already begun taking up when the loop is largest, and the minimum margin is only 0.7 mm; delayed to 216° it is just 0.1 mm, while the excess actually grows to over 30 mm. So whenever the loop-catch timing (Chapter 5) is changed, the take-up must be re-checked as well.

Link versus cam　Designing the supply curve with the cam option in the virtual lab, the supply can be made to follow the demand closely, cutting the excess while passing around the case from 24 mm to under 8 mm. But the closer the fit, the steeper the curve: at 5000 r/min the maximum needle-thread acceleration of the worked-example link take-up is about 30 km/s², whereas a cam curve from the lab that meets the above criteria reaches about 290 km/s², nearly ten times as much. The higher the thread acceleration, the higher the dynamic tension the thread experiences at the guides and in the tension assembly. This is why high-speed industrial lockstitch machines accept a little extra pay-out in exchange for the smooth link take-up; to be both smooth and economical with thread, a rotary take-up disc or a motor-driven take-up is needed.

Design sequence　First estimate the demand curve from the needle-bar motion, the loop-catch timing, the bobbin-case size and the heaviest fabric, and fix the maximum supply and the split of time between take-up and pay-out. Next choose the four-bar dimensions so that the eye path is gentle during pay-out and steep during take-up, while ensuring that the mechanism can be assembled throughout the revolution and the transmission angle stays above about 40°. Then position the thread guide and the lower guide point, check the surplus for different fabric thicknesses against the three criteria of Section 4.5, and leave an adjustment range for the thread guide. Finally set the check-spring stroke and force. The take-up lever is a part that oscillates at high speed, so it should be as light as possible, and attention must be paid to sealing against oil at the slot it passes through in the housing (Chapter 8).

4.7 Limits of the model

This chapter treats the thread as an inextensible string, simplifies the loop shape to a convex hull in a plane, and assumes that after the hook point passes its lowest position the loop shrinks along a smooth curve until cast-off. All of these are simplifications made to bring out clearly the relationship between supply and demand. Real thread is elastic, there is friction at every guide along the path, and at high speed the inertia of the thread cannot be neglected, so the check-spring stroke actually required is usually smaller than in the worked example (10 mm); the service manual gives about 7 mm. The worked example’s surplus of about 7.6 mm as the needle point enters the fabric agrees broadly with the manual’s “motion completed as the needle point enters the fabric, stroke about 7 mm”; because the model only starts counting needle thread consumption once the eye enters the fabric, it asks the check spring to hold a little more. In three dimensions the loop must also clear the thickness of the bobbin case, and the loop shape of a rotary hook depends on the detailed shape of the hook point and the bobbin-case holder; Chapter 5 returns to this. The most reliable source of a demand curve is measurement: mark the needle thread and record its movement during one stitch with a high-speed camera, or fit a tension sensor at a guide point to measure the thread tension at each instant (Chapter 7).

Shop-floor notes

  • If thread breaks or stitches will not set after changing to a heavy fabric, check the thread-guide position and the check-spring stroke first, then the tension assembly; the two should be adjusted together.
  • Floating thread and loose loops under the fabric usually mean the take-up lever takes up too late or pays out too much; check whether the check spring has lost its springiness and whether the thread guide has been moved to the “more pay-out” side.
  • After adjusting the loop-catch timing or replacing the hook, sew a test seam in both a light and a heavy fabric to confirm that the take-up still supplies enough thread.
  • Turn the handwheel by hand and watch the check spring: it should just complete its stroke as the needle point enters the fabric, and move only about 1 mm when the loop is passing around the bobbin case and is at its largest (follow the service manual for the specific model).
