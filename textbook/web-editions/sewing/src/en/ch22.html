This chapter draws on the needle-positioning model and solenoid delays of Chapter 13, the trimming sequence of Chapter 14, production test and burn-in in Chapter 18, MQTT reporting in Chapter 26, and the WQ-SC platform of Chapter 19 (event table, state machine, hardware abstraction layer). The statistics need only the basic ideas of the binomial and normal distributions.

Chapter 19 assembled the WQ-SC platform from modules in the parts library, and Chapters 20 and 21 built four control prototypes on it: lockstitch, overlock, pattern sewer and a scaled flat knitter. A prototype that moves and sews is still a long way from an industrial product, which must behave the same way on thousands of machines, over years, through millions of trims. That distance is not covered in one flash of inspiration; it is covered round after round of “change a little, test it all again”. The parts library makes configuration fast, the platform makes building fast, and the firmware framework makes changes fast. Once all three are fast, testing becomes the bottleneck. If every firmware change needs an experienced technician to sit at the machine and sew for half a day, iteration cannot be quick — and whatever he cannot detect will reach the customer anyway.

So this chapter is about automating the tests: every change is tested automatically, every result is tied to a requirement number and stored in the digital factory’s historian, and the decision to move a prototype up to the next maturity level is made from those data. One example runs through the chapter: a firmware optimisation that looked like a clear win made trimming fail now and then at high speed. Hand trials could not find it; the nightly hardware-in-the-loop run caught it the same night.

22.1 Opening: a trimming failure that appears only at high speed

The firmware of the WQ-SC/LS lockstitch prototype had reached v1.3.2, and every requirement was met. An engineer wanted to make the stop-and-trim faster. Chapter 14 worked out that stopping and trimming from 4000 r/min takes about 0.38 s: the spindle first decelerates at 60 % of the servo’s capability to 300 r/min, finishing before the trimming stitch begins (0°); the trimming stitch is then sewn at 300 r/min, the trimmer solenoid is fired at 290°, and the threads are cut at 55° of the next revolution. His change was simple (v1.4.0): plan the deceleration at 100 % of the servo’s capability, and instead of finishing it before the trimming stitch, move its end to 180° of that stitch, so that deceleration and the trimming stitch overlap. On paper the time from 4000 r/min to the cut drops from 377 ms to 219 ms, about 0.16 s saved per trim. As Chapter 14 noted, an operator trims thousands of times a day; that is ten-odd minutes a day.

He tried it on the prototype — a few dozen stops each from 3000, 3500 and 4000 r/min, every trim clean — and ran the trim speed sweep in software-in-the-loop (SIL): all passed. The change was merged. That night the trim speed sweep on the hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) rig turned red: of 250 trims from 5000 r/min, 43 failed to cut the needle thread.

The cause lay in a curve he had not thought about (Fig. 22-1). A servo motor’s deceleration capability is not constant. Below the base speed it is limited by current, about 4400 rad/s² (Chapter 13); above the base speed the motor enters its constant-power region, the available torque falls as speed rises, and the base speed itself moves with the mains voltage. v1.3.2 used only 60 % of the capability and the margin swallowed this effect. v1.4.0 used all of it and pushed the end of the deceleration into the middle of the trimming stitch, so when starting from high speed the spindle fell behind the reference curve and had not yet reached 300 r/min when the solenoid was fired at 290°. The trimmer solenoid’s delay is 6–10 ms and the knife enters at 290° + 6nt (Chapter 14); if the speed at firing exceeds about 667 r/min, a 10 ms delay puts the knife entry after 330° and the thread is not cut. In the worked example, with normal mains voltage the speed at firing from 5000 r/min is about 514 r/min and the trim still succeeds; with the voltage 10 % low it is about 1150 r/min and the trim must fail. The voltage fluctuates all the time, so the failures are intermittent: up to 4500 r/min the failure rate is the same as before the change (about 0.012 %, from the tail of the solenoid delay itself), at 4750 r/min about 1.2 %, at 5000 r/min about 17 %.

Why could hand trials not find it? There are three reasons, and each is a problem this chapter sets out to solve. First, the operating conditions are not covered: people habitually sew at 3000–4000 r/min, rarely floor the pedal to 5000 r/min and then let go, and never deliberately test at low mains voltage; 50 trims at 3000–4000 r/min have only about a 0.6 % chance of showing one failure. Second, too few trials: even at 4750 r/min, with a 1.2 % failure rate, 50 trims have about an even chance of showing nothing (Section 22.6). Third, the model is not good enough: SIL uses the ideal motor of Chapter 13 with no constant-power region, so the SIL sweep passed; the motor emulator on the HIL rig uses the torque–speed curve measured on the prototype, and that exposed it. Together these are the starting point of automated testing: let a machine test everything that must be tested, every night, under specified conditions, enough times, with realistic enough models.

22.2 The test pyramid

Group the tests by how far they are from the real machine. The lower the level, the faster, cheaper and more frequent the tests, and the more of them there are; the higher the level, the closer to reality, but the slower and dearer. Drawn out, it is a pyramid (Fig. 22-2). The WQ-SC platform has five levels, matching the right-hand leg of the V-model in Chapter 18.

Unit tests exercise single firmware functions: converting encoder counts to angles with 0.1° resolution, parsing and looking up the event table, which state the state machine moves to for each input, encoding fault codes. The firmware framework of Chapter 19 hides all hardware access behind the HAL, so these functions compile and run on a PC; hundreds of cases finish in a minute. Unit tests find logic errors: the wrap from 359.9° to 0°, out-of-range parameters, a missing state transition. They cannot see timing or physics: the v1.4.0 deceleration planner computed exactly what it was meant to, and its unit test passed.

Software-in-the-loop (SIL) connects the control part of the firmware to a machine model and runs it on a PC in simulated time. The machine models are those of the virtual labs throughout this book: the spindle servo and solenoids of Chapter 13, the trimming sequence of Chapter 14, X–Y interpolation of Chapter 15, needle selection of Chapter 17. A SIL run takes minutes and allows large parameter sweeps: inertia ±20 %, friction ±30 %, solenoid delay 6–10 ms. Its limits are the model’s limits: physics that is not in the model (a constant-power region, interrupt jitter, a real solenoid) is invisible to SIL.

Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) connects the real WQ-SC controller, real driver boards and solenoids to a real-time simulator, which computes the motion of the spindle and mechanisms from measured parameters and feeds encoder signals back to the controller. The firmware runs on the real chip, with real interrupts, DMA and CAN FD bus, so real-time problems, the real behaviour of the drive and the real solenoid delays are all tested; faults that are rare in practice can be produced on purpose — encoder loss, overcurrent, voltage dips. A HIL round takes a few hours and runs at night.

The machine test bench mounts a real head on the universal bench (Section 22.4), loads it with a load motor, watches the stitches with a camera and runs for long periods. It tests what is not in any model at all: stitch quality, backtack alignment, mechanical play, temperature rise, noise. A round takes a day or two and runs weekly or for each release candidate.

End-of-line (EOL) test is done on every unit as it leaves the line (Chapter 18). Its object differs from the other four: they validate the design and assume the prototype under test is good; EOL checks whether this unit was assembled correctly and its components are sound, assuming the design has already been validated.

The point of the levels is that a defect should be found at the lowest level that can find it. The same defect found in a unit test is fixed in minutes; found on HIL, it costs a night’s wait and a bisection; found on the machine bench, the head comes apart; found by a customer, it means repairs, claims and reputation. So whenever a higher level catches a defect, ask after fixing it: can a test one level lower catch it? The case in Section 22.9 ends by doing exactly that.

22.3 Tracing requirements to tests to results

22.4 A universal test bench

Automated testing first needs a test bench that a program can command and that measures for itself. Chapter 19’s idea was “one platform, four machines”; the test bench follows suit: one set of instruments, a change of adaptor plate, four prototypes tested (Fig. 22-3).

The bench has three parts. On the left, the test PC runs the Python test scripts, sends commands through the prototype’s gateway and reads back what the prototype itself reports, reads the “true” values from the bench modules, judges, writes the report and publishes the results on the digital-factory bus. In the middle is the prototype under test (DUT, device under test): gateway, WQ-SC controller, drive layer and the head (a real head on the machine bench, replaced by the simulator on HIL). On the right are the bench modules, which measure independently of the prototype. This matters: if the stop error were read only from the prototype’s own encoder, a misaligned encoder would make the prototype and the test agree on the same wrong answer.

What each module does:

  • Load motor and dynamometer: a servo motor coupled to the spindle at the handwheel end by a jaw coupling (existing library entry A-CPL-JAW). Under program control it applies a load torque (simulating penetration of heavy fabric, or added inertia) and measures spindle torque and speed.
  • Encoder signal emulator: on HIL it replaces the spindle encoder, generating A, B and Z signals from the simulated angle (2500 lines, 10 000 counts/rev after quadrature, as in Chapter 19), and on command injects missing pulses, loss of signal or jitter.
  • HIL real-time simulator: computes spindle motion from the measured torque–speed curve, inertia and friction, with a step below 1 ms. Its model is one step more “real” than SIL’s because its parameters come from measurements on the prototype (that is how the constant-power region of Section 22.1 entered).
  • Data acquisition and current probes: solenoid coil current, DC-link voltage, encoder angle and the firmware’s firing signal, sampled together; the firing signal is brought out on a debug pin of the controller.
  • Stroke sensor: measures the travel of the solenoid plunger or the moving knife, giving the real pull-in and release instants; subtracting the firing instant gives the delay, converted to angle at the running speed (REQ-SOL-01).
  • Stitch camera: photographs the fabric and locates needle penetrations to compute backtack offset, skipped stitches and thread-tail length; it can also judge whether a trim cut (whether a long length of needle thread is pulled out).
  • Temperature logger: motor, heat sink, solenoid coils and ambient, for the 4 h temperature-rise test.

Like the control modules of Chapter 19, these modules belong in the parts library, so that “configuring a test bench” can also be done by parameter selection with an automatic BOM. None of them is in the library yet; Table 22-4 gives proposed IDs and key parameters.

Change-over One bench tests four prototypes thanks to three standardised things. Mechanically, each prototype has an adaptor plate that fixes the head or table to the same set of mounting holes on the bench, and the spindle (or the knitter’s carriage drive shaft) is connected to the load motor through the same kind of coupling; the pattern sewer’s X–Y table adds two linear scales or a laser displacement sensor to measure the actual position, while the knitter uses the encoder emulator for carriage position and the stroke sensor on a selector. Electrically, the bench and prototype use the same set of numbered connectors, with the wiring table of Chapter 19 deciding which pin carries what. In software, each prototype has one test configuration file stating its wiring table, HAL drivers and which cases to run; the test scripts themselves do not change. A change-over means swapping the adaptor plate and the configuration file: under half an hour (illustrative).

Shop-floor notes

  • Calibrate the bench’s own measuring chain regularly: check the encoder emulator and stroke sensor against an indexing plate of known angle, and the dynamometer with weights on a lever arm. If the bench is wrong, every conclusion is wrong.
  • “True” values must come from independent sensors, not only from what the prototype reports. Record both; their difference is itself a useful indicator.
  • Each bench has its own serial number and “firmware version” (the version of the test scripts), recorded with every result.

22.5 Test scripts: driving it with Python and pytest

The test scripts are written in Python and organised with pytest, for plain reasons: the syntax is easy for engineers and students to read; pytest fixtures are made for set-up and tear-down jobs such as “connect to the prototype, de-energise it afterwards”; parametrisation turns one case into a set of speeds in one line; and the results can be written in a format that CI servers understand. The WenQuest Digital Factory tests itself the same way: pytest -q tests runs the unit tests, and a separate closed-loop script walks through the whole factory following a lab exercise, both run automatically by CI after each push (Section 5 of the digital-factory README).

Fixtures: connect to the prototype, publish the results The conftest.py below defines two fixtures. dut connects to the prototype under test through the gateway’s web API and reads its serial number, firmware version and parameter version; it offers a cmd() method that sends a command, waits for it to finish and returns what the prototype and the bench measured. report connects to the digital factory’s MQTT broker and publishes each result, in the standard envelope, to the …/measurement topic. The gateway endpoints (/api/v1/info, /api/v1/cmd) are illustrative; the gateway definition in Chapter 19 governs.

# tests/conftest.py — connect to the prototype under test (DUT), publish results to the digital factory
import datetime as dt, json, os, uuid
import pytest, requests
import paho.mqtt.client as mqtt

GW    = os.environ.get("WQ_DUT", "http://192.168.10.21")   # prototype gateway (example address)
BUS   = os.environ.get("WQ_BUS", "bus.lab.local")          # digital-factory MQTT broker
TOPIC = "wq/sewing/lab/tb-01/measurement"                  # factory/area/unit/category

def pytest_configure(config):
    config.addinivalue_line("markers", "req(id): requirement verified by this test")

class Dut:
    def __init__(self, base):
        self.base = base
        info = requests.get(base + "/api/v1/info", timeout=5).json()
        self.serial, self.fw, self.param = info["serial"], info["fw"], info["param_ver"]

    def cmd(self, name, **kw):
        """Send one command, wait until the prototype has done it, return the measurements (dict)."""
        r = requests.post(self.base + "/api/v1/cmd", json={"cmd": name, **kw}, timeout=300)
        r.raise_for_status()
        return r.json()

@pytest.fixture(scope="session")
def dut():
    d = Dut(GW)
    d.cmd("reset_faults")
    yield d
    d.cmd("safe")                      # pass or fail, de-energise the actuators afterwards

@pytest.fixture(scope="session")
def report(dut):
    cli = mqtt.Client(mqtt.CallbackAPIVersion.VERSION2, client_id="tb-01-pytest")
    cli.connect(BUS)
    cli.loop_start()
    run = "run-" + uuid.uuid4().hex[:8]          # one correlation id per test run

    def send(req, case, value, unit, limit, ok, **extra):
        ts = dt.datetime.now(dt.timezone.utc).isoformat(timespec="milliseconds")
        msg = {"v": 1, "id": uuid.uuid4().hex, "ts": ts.replace("+00:00", "Z"),
               "type": "test.result", "source": "tb-01/pytest", "mode": "prod", "corr": run,
               "data": {"req": req, "case": case, "dut_serial": dut.serial,
                        "fw": dut.fw, "param_ver": dut.param,
                        "value": value, "unit": unit, "limit": limit,
                        "result": "pass" if ok else "fail", **extra}}
        cli.publish(TOPIC, json.dumps(msg, ensure_ascii=False), qos=1).wait_for_publish()
    yield send
    cli.loop_stop()
    cli.disconnect()

The outer envelope (v, id, ts, type, source, mode, corr, data) follows the WenQuest Digital Factory format (Chapter 26); test.result is a data type added for test results under the specification’s “add, never alter” rule, and the area lab and unit tb-01 in the topic are this chapter’s example names. QoS 1 is used and the receiver de-duplicates by message id (Chapter 26).

Test cases Below is an excerpt of the WQ-SC/LS acceptance cases. Each case does three things: runs the prototype a number of times under specified conditions, computes statistics, and compares them with the criterion. The result is published first, pass or fail, and then judged with assert.

# tests/test_ls_acceptance.py — WQ-SC/LS acceptance cases (excerpt)
import statistics as st
import pytest
from stats import r_lower, max_fail        # the two functions of Section 22.6

SPEEDS = [3000, 3500, 4000, 4500, 4750, 5000]
TRIMS = {}                                 # (trials, failures) per speed, judged together at the end

@pytest.mark.req("REQ-POS-01")
def test_stop_position(dut, report):
    """Stopping: 50 stops, max error ≤ 1°, Cpk ≥ 1.33."""
    e = [abs(dut.cmd("run_and_stop", rpm=3000, stitches=20)["stop_err_deg"]) for _ in range(50)]
    mu, sd, mx = st.mean(e), st.stdev(e), max(e)
    cpk = (1.0 - mu) / (3 * sd)            # upper limit only: one-sided Cpk
    ok = mx <= 1.0 and cpk >= 1.33
    report("REQ-POS-01", "test_stop_position", mx, "deg", 1.0, ok, cpk=round(cpk, 2), n=50)
    assert ok, f"max {mx:.2f}°, Cpk {cpk:.2f}"

@pytest.mark.req("REQ-TRM-01")
@pytest.mark.parametrize("rpm", SPEEDS)
def test_trim_point(dut, report, rpm):
    """Trimming: 250 stop-and-trims from rpm; count failures (camera judges whether both threads were cut)."""
    n = 250
    fails = sum(not dut.cmd("sew_and_trim", rpm=rpm, stitches=20)["trim_ok"] for _ in range(n))
    TRIMS[rpm] = (n, fails)
    report("REQ-TRM-01", f"test_trim_point[{rpm}]", fails, "count", None, fails <= 1, n=n)
    assert fails <= 1, f"{rpm} r/min: {fails}/{n} trims did not cut"   # 2 or more at one speed: alarm

@pytest.mark.req("REQ-TRM-01")
def test_trim_overall(report):
    """All speeds together: 95 % lower confidence bound on reliability ≥ 99.5 %."""
    n = sum(v[0] for v in TRIMS.values())
    x = sum(v[1] for v in TRIMS.values())
    rl = r_lower(n, x, 0.95)
    report("REQ-TRM-01", "test_trim_overall", rl, "ratio", 0.995, rl >= 0.995, n=n, fails=x)
    assert rl >= 0.995, f"{x}/{n} failed, bound {rl:.4f} (at most {max_fail(n)} allowed in {n})"

@pytest.mark.req("REQ-XY-01")
@pytest.mark.parametrize("pattern", ["circle_r20.wqp", "zigzag_3mm.wqp", "logo_dense.wqp"])
def test_xy_following(dut, report, pattern):
    r = dut.cmd("run_pattern", file=pattern, rpm=2500)        # bench linear scales measure actual position
    ok = r["follow_err_mm"] <= 0.05 and r["move_in_fabric_mm"] == 0
    report("REQ-XY-01", f"test_xy_following[{pattern}]", r["follow_err_mm"], "mm", 0.05, ok)
    assert ok

A few remarks. First, parametrisation turns one case into a set: test_trim_point runs once for each of six speeds and each is listed separately, so the speed that misbehaves is obvious; the regression of Section 22.1 was caught by the 4750 and 5000 points, and a test at 4000 r/min alone would have let it through. Second, the criteria live in the test and match the requirement table: two or more failures at one speed raise an alarm; the total is judged by the binomial lower confidence bound (Section 22.6). Third, the stop test uses both the maximum absolute error and Cpk: judged on a single stop, the “faster stop” change in v1.3.2 (position gain 200 → 300 s⁻¹) gives about 0.91° on HIL and passes; only the statistics of 50 stops reveal a maximum above 1° and a Cpk of about 0.3 (Section 22.6). Fourth, the scripts know only the gateway’s commands and measurements, not the machine type: the same test_stop_position tests the overlock prototype with a different configuration file.

The lockstitch prototype’s unit tests target firmware functions written in C. They can be compiled and run on a PC with a C unit-test framework, or the firmware can be compiled into a host library whose functions Python calls, so that pytest manages the cases and reports of all five levels. SIL cases are also written in pytest; the only difference is that the dut fixture connects not to a gateway but to a “firmware + model” simulation process with the same command and reply format. The same case can then run first on SIL and unchanged on HIL and the machine bench, and the results of the three levels can be compared directly — Section 22.9 makes use of this.

22.6 How many tests are enough

Trimming success must be ≥ 99.5 %. Two hundred trials, all successful — is that enough? No. Two hundred successes only show that the success rate is unlikely to be very low; they do not show that it is at least 99.5 %. To answer “is it enough”, state the question statistically: demonstrate, at confidence C, that the reliability is at least R.

22.6.1 Zero-failure demonstration test

If the true reliability were exactly R, the probability of n successes in n trials would be Rⁿ. Require this to be at most 1 − C — that is, “if the reliability were only R, we would be very unlikely to see n successes in a row” — and

\[ R^{\,n}\le 1-C \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n\ \ge\ \frac{\ln(1-C)}{\ln R} \]

For R = 99.5 % and C = 95 %, n ≥ ln 0.05 / ln 0.995 = 597.6, so 598 trials. In other words, 598 stop-and-trims from one speed without a single failure are needed to say with 95 % confidence that trimming success is at least 99.5 %. Table 22-5 lists some common cases. An easy approximation: at C = 95 %, n ≈ 3/(1 − R) — with zero failures, test about three times the reciprocal of the failure rate.

22.6.2 Lower confidence bound with failures

With x failures in n trials, the point estimate of reliability is 1 − x/n, but the judgement must use its lower confidence bound. Let the failure probability be p; the number of failures is binomial. The one-sided upper bound pU is the value of p that makes the probability of “no more than x failures” exactly 1 − C:

\[ \sum_{k=0}^{x}\binom{n}{k}p_U^{\,k}(1-p_U)^{\,n-k}=1-C,\qquad R_L=1-p_U \]

This is the Clopper–Pearson method; pU can also be computed directly as a quantile of the beta distribution, pU = Beta⁻¹(C; x+1, n−x). In Python it takes two lines:

# stats.py — Section 22.6
from math import ceil, log
from scipy.stats import beta

def n_zero(R, C):              # trials for a zero-failure demonstration
    return ceil(log(1 - C) / log(R))

def r_lower(n, x, C=0.95):     # one-sided lower bound on reliability, x failures in n
    return 0.0 if x >= n else 1 - beta.ppf(C, x + 1, n - x)

def max_fail(n, R=0.995, C=0.95):   # most failures allowed in n trials
    x = -1
    while r_lower(n, x + 1, C) >= R:
        x += 1
    return x

print(n_zero(0.995, 0.95))                     # 598
print([round(r_lower(1000, x), 4) for x in range(4)])   # [0.997, 0.9953, 0.9937, 0.9923]

For 1000 trims, the bound is 99.70 % with no failure and 99.53 % with one, both meeting 99.5 %; with two failures the point estimate is 99.8 % but the bound only 99.37 %, which fails (Fig. 22-4, middle). Hence “at most 1 failure in 1000 at one speed” in Table 22-2. The 1500-trim speed sweep of Section 22.5 allows at most 2 failures.

22.6.3 How many tests catch a regression

The question above was “how many tests prove it is good”. CI also asks “if it has become bad, how many tests will notice”. If a change raises the failure probability to p, the chance of seeing at least one failure in n trials is 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ. To be 95 % sure of catching a regression with p = 0.5 %, about 598 trials are again needed — the same number as the zero-failure demonstration. That is no coincidence: both questions are the same equation. The regression of Section 22.1 has p ≈ 1.2 % at 4750 r/min, so 250 trims give about a 96 % chance of seeing it; at 5000 r/min p ≈ 17 %, and a handful of trims suffice. This illustrates a principle of test design: spend the limited trials where problems are most likely. The same 250 trims at 4000 r/min would show nothing; at top speed they are almost certain to catch it.

22.6.4 Variables data: mean, 3σ and Cpk

Stop error is a continuous quantity; there is no need merely to count passes. Let the mean of 50 stop errors be μ and the standard deviation σ. With only an upper limit, USL = 1°, use the one-sided process capability index

\[ C_{pk}=\frac{\mathrm{USL}-\mu}{3\sigma} \]

Cpk ≥ 1.33 means the limit is at least 4σ from the mean. In the worked example (Fig. 22-4 right; HIL inertia 6.3×10⁻⁴ kg·m², small random variations in friction and positioning speed from stop to stop, plus 0.03° measurement noise): with position gain Kp = 200 s⁻¹, mean 0.17°, σ 0.026°, Cpk ≈ 10.7 — a large margin; with Kp = 300 s⁻¹, mean 0.92°, σ 0.079°, maximum 1.13°, Cpk ≈ 0.34 — a failure. A single stop of 0.91° looks “acceptable”; a little statistics shows it sitting on the line. Variables criteria need fewer trials than attributes criteria: 50 stops show the distribution clearly, whereas proving 99.5 % by pass/fail needs a thousand. So wherever a continuous value can be measured, record it, not just ✓ or ✗.

Cpk assumes the error is roughly normal and the process stable. When friction makes the stop error “stick” (Chapter 13) the distribution is not normal, and with few samples σ is a rough estimate. Judge on the maximum as well, repeat on different benches and prototypes, and do not decide on one Cpk figure alone.

22.7 Continuous integration: every firmware change is tested automatically

Continuous integration (CI) means that every commit is built and tested automatically, and a change that fails may not be merged into the main line. It turns the pyramid of Section 22.2 into a pipeline (Fig. 22-5). The digital factory in the WenQuest repository has one ready to look at: whenever a push touches factory/, it runs “test → server rehearsal (install a real ERPNext and walk the full closed loop) → build images → deploy to the server”; a failure stops the later steps, and the failure logs are collected into annotations automatically. Sewing-machine firmware CI has the same structure, with the “rehearsal” replaced by the HIL rig and “deploy” by releasing a firmware package.

Three pipelines On every push: build the firmware (cross-compiled) and the host version, run unit tests and SIL, about 12 min; no merge on failure — developers know the result in the time it takes to fetch a drink. Nightly: flash the latest main-line build into the HIL rig’s controller and run the HIL regression cases within a 4 h budget; results go to the bus and the dashboard, and a failure triggers automatic bisection. Weekly or per release candidate: about two days on the machine bench, each of the four prototypes running 4 h at top speed, 1000 real trims and camera checks of backtack and stitches; only a full pass is released, and the firmware and parameter version numbers are reported to the digital factory with each prototype. Below is an illustrative pipeline configuration (in GitHub Actions syntax; the HIL job runs on the PC attached to the rig):

# .github/workflows/firmware.yml (illustrative)
name: WQ-SC firmware
on:
  push: {branches: [main]}
  pull_request:
  schedule: [{cron: "0 18 * * *"}]        # 18:00 UTC = 02:00 Beijing time
jobs:
  quick:                                  # every push: about 12 min
    runs-on: ubuntu-latest
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
      - run: make firmware host           # cross-compiled firmware + host build
      - run: python -m pytest -q tests/unit tests/sil
  nightly-hil:                            # nightly: 4 h budget
    if: github.event_name == 'schedule'
    runs-on: [self-hosted, hil-01]        # the PC attached to the HIL rig
    timeout-minutes: 250
    steps:
      - uses: actions/checkout@v4
      - run: make firmware && tools/flash.sh build/wqsc.bin
      - run: python -m pytest tests/hil -m nightly --junitxml=hil.xml
      - if: failure()
        run: tools/bisect_hil.sh "$(cat .last_green)" "$GITHUB_SHA"

The test-time budget A night has only a few hours, there are only one or two rigs, and the number of cases keeps growing. Nightly cases must therefore be chosen by “risk × defects caught ÷ time”. Table 22-6 shows how WQ-SC/LS spends its 240 nightly minutes (illustrative). Some principles: functions that changes often touch (trimming, stopping) get more time; safety-related tests (fault injection) run every night; slow tests that rarely change (a 2 h soak) move to the weekend; for one requirement, prefer one speed sweep to four long runs at fixed speeds.

A counter-example: if the nightly trimming case were “1000 trims at 4000 r/min” (55 min) and the stop case “one stop” (2 min), the total would be even shorter, yet neither the regression of Section 22.1 nor that of v1.3.2 would be caught — the first appears only at high speed, the second gives 0.91° on a single stop and just “passes”. The case set itself must be tested. One way is to plant known defects in the firmware on purpose and see whether the set catches them (the idea behind mutation testing); Task 2 of Virtual lab 22-1 is such an exercise.

Locating the failing commit automatically: bisection When HIL fails, last night was green and tonight is red, and twenty or thirty commits may have been merged in between. Trying them one by one is too slow. Bisection tries the middle one; if it is good, look later, if bad, look earlier; k rounds find the first bad commit among 2k, so 23 commits need 5 rounds. git has this built in:

# tools/bisect_hil.sh GOOD BAD — re-run only the one failing case
git bisect start "$2" "$1"
git bisect run sh -c '
  make firmware || exit 125                         # skip commits that do not build
  tools/flash.sh build/wqsc.bin || exit 125
  python -m pytest -q tests/hil -k "trim_point and 5000"'
git bisect log > bisect.log; git bisect reset

Bisection re-runs only the failing case, and should use its most sensitive condition. With 250 trims at 5000 r/min, a good commit almost never fails and a bad one fails 43 times on average, so each round is unambiguous. At 4750 r/min a bad commit shows itself with only about 96 % probability, so bisection may misjudge a round and blame an innocent commit. A bisection test must satisfy two conditions: a bad commit always fails and a good one always passes (statistically) — which brings us back to the arithmetic of Section 22.6.3.

Design notes

  • Keep the test configuration (case sets, criteria, bench versions) in version control with the firmware and review them together; changing a criterion leaves a record just like changing code.
  • A flaky test (passing and failing on the same version) is worse than no test: it teaches people to ignore red. When you find one, establish whether the bench, the model or the firmware is at fault, then fix or quarantine it — never “just run it again”.
  • A nightly report needs only three things: which requirements turned red, the indicators that moved most since last night, and the bisection verdict. Details are a click away.

22.8 From prototype to industrial grade: maturity driven by test data

When is a prototype “done”? Left to gut feeling, it stays at “nearly” for ever. The electronics industry commonly manages this with a ladder of maturity levels: P0 and P1 prototypes, then engineering validation (EVT), design validation (DVT), production validation (PVT), and finally volume production. This chapter writes every level’s exit conditions as numbers that automated tests can judge (Table 22-7, Fig. 22-6), so that review meetings look only at data.

A few of the numbers deserve comment. From EVT to DVT, trimming validation rises from “99.5 %/95 %” to “99.9 %/90 %”, the trials from about a thousand to 2302 with no failure, and they must be repeated on several units at several voltages and temperatures. DVT asks whether the design itself has margin, so differences between units and between environments must be included. PVT shifts the focus from design to manufacture: the design is frozen, and the question is whether a batch built by the production process is consistent; EOL first-pass yield is the most direct indicator. The number of units grows from level to level because the statistics demand ever more trials than one unit can run, and because only several units show unit-to-unit variation.

End-of-line test In volume production every control box and every machine passes EOL as it leaves the line. Its items are chosen from the acceptance cases that are quick and find assembly defects: encoder reading and zero, 10 stops, 20 trims (looking for any failure to cut, not proving 99.5 % — that is design validation’s job), solenoid coil current waveform (a wrongly fitted or shorted coil shows at a glance), pedal, firmware version check and parameter download, plus the electrical safety tests of Chapter 18 (protective-earth continuity, insulation resistance, withstand voltage) and burn-in. Each unit takes 3–5 min (illustrative). EOL scripts use the same framework as development, only a different case set, so any development case can become an EOL item directly.

Data go to the digital factory by serial number Every EOL result is published on the bus in the format of Section 22.5, to a topic such as wq/sewing/eol/eol-01/measurement, with serial number and firmware and parameter versions, into the historian and the quality system (Chapter 27). For any machine, over its whole life, one can then look up its stop error at shipment, how many trims it was tested with, and which firmware it carried.

Field data flow back Once machines in customers’ workshops are connected (Chapter 26), their status and event reports include trim counts, fault codes, and firmware and parameter versions. Matching these with the factory data by version, batch and serial number shows what the laboratory cannot: that machines on one firmware version log noticeably more “trim did not cut” repairs in plants with an unsteady supply, say, or that machines with one batch of solenoids pull in more slowly after two years. Each such finding adds a REQ (or tightens a criterion) in the traceability matrix and a case that reproduces it (often a fault-injection scenario or an operating condition on HIL), tested automatically at the next firmware change. Chapter 27 calls this the quality loop; for testing it means the case set grows up together with the product.

22.9 Case: a firmware optimisation makes trimming fail intermittently at high speed

Technical problem Back to Section 22.1. This is a case written from first principles; its numbers come from this chapter’s model (figsrc/ch22_model.py, the same as Virtual lab 22-1) and are illustrative. The v1.4.0 “fast trim” change was merged on a Tuesday afternoon: deceleration planned at 100 % of servo capability, its end moved from 0° to 180° of the trimming stitch. The push pipeline was green in about 12 min: the unit test checked the deceleration planner’s arithmetic, and the SIL trim sweep (3000–5000 r/min, 200 per point) had no failures.

Detection In the 02:00 HIL regression run the trim sweep turned red: 43 failures in 250 at the 5000 r/min point, and failures at 4750 r/min too — far beyond the limit of 2 in 1500; every other case was green (Fig. 22-7, left). The results on the bus included the speed at firing for every trim, and the picture was immediate: the failed trims had been fired at 700–1200 r/min, where about 300 r/min was intended.

Localisation Last night was green, and 23 commits had been merged since. CI bisected automatically, re-running only the case “250 trims at 5000 r/min”: commit 11 passed, 17 failed, 14 failed, 12 and 13 passed; after round 5 it pointed to commit 14, a3f9c1e (“trim: plan deceleration at full capability, end inside the trimming stitch”). Each round took about 3 min to build and flash and about 13 min for 250 trims, about 80 min in all. When the engineers arrived in the morning, a report was waiting: which requirement, which case, which commit, and the speed records of the failures.

Cause The right of Fig. 22-7 shows the speed during the trimming stitch when stopping from 5000 r/min. v1.3.2 decelerates at 60 % capability with its reference ending at 0° of the trimming stitch; the spindle settles at 300 r/min by about 95°, nearly 200° before firing at 290°. v1.4.0 plans at 100 % capability and moves the end to 180°, but in the constant-power region the spindle cannot decelerate that fast and falls further and further behind: at firing it is still at about 514 r/min with normal mains voltage, about 850 r/min with the voltage 5 % low, and about 1150 r/min with it 10 % low. With n = 850 r/min and t = 8 ms the knife enters at 290° + 6nt = 331°, after the window. Why did SIL miss it? SIL’s motor is the ideal motor of Chapter 13, with 4400 rad/s² of deceleration everywhere; in that world the v1.4.0 plan is perfectly feasible and the failure rate is the same as before (about 0.011 %). The HIL simulator’s torque–speed curve was measured on the prototype: above the base speed it enters the constant-power region, and the base speed moves with the DC-link voltage. That is where the problem came from.

Fix (v1.4.1) Two changes. First, plan the deceleration on the motor’s actual torque–speed curve, using only 90 % of it, with the base speed taken at the low-voltage limit (0.9 times). Second, add a safeguard: at 270° of the trimming stitch, check the measured speed, and if it exceeds 400 r/min, trim one stitch later — better one stitch slower than an uncut thread. After the fix, stopping from 5000 r/min at any voltage brings the speed at firing back to 300 r/min; stopping from 4000 r/min to the cut takes about 0.23 s, still about 0.15 s faster than v1.3.2’s 0.38 s, so most of the gain is kept.

Regression v1.4.1 re-ran all nightly cases on HIL: trim sweep 0/1500. On the machine bench, 1000 real trims from 5000 r/min: no failure, lower bound 99.70 % (C = 95 %). Two further steps matter just as much: port the HIL torque–speed curve (with its voltage factor) into the SIL model, so that SIL catches this class of problem in minutes from now on; and add “speed at trim firing” as a new variables indicator in the REQ-TRM-01 cases, trended in the nightly report — it shows trouble coming long before “cut or not cut” does.

Discussion The case hides a second, latent problem. When v1.3.2 raised the position gain from 200 to 300 s⁻¹, the nightly stop case stopped only once (0.91°, pass) and SIL used only nominal parameters (about 0.8°, pass), so it rode along into v1.4.1. As soon as the stop case became 50 stops with statistics (HIL-POS-50, only 4 more minutes a night), or SIL added an inertia ±20 % sweep (overshoot about 1.3° at +20 % inertia), it showed at once; v1.4.2 set Kp to 220 s⁻¹ and the 50-stop Cpk returned to about 7. Taken together, the two regressions show that the value of automated testing lies not in running a lot, but in running the right cases, under the right conditions, the right number of times, with the right criteria — every time. 22.10 Limits of the model

The numbers in this chapter come from a few simplified models whose purpose is to show cause, effect and orders of magnitude in test design. The trimming model attributes failure only to knife entry after 330° (or before 300°), ignoring loop shape, knife wear and thread type; the solenoid delay is normal, N(8, 0.667) ms, matching Chapter 14’s 6–10 ms, whereas the real distribution may be skewed with a longer tail — and the tail is exactly what decides failure rates of the order of 0.5 %. The servo’s constant-power region is represented by “capability proportional to nb/n”, the base speed varies with mains voltage with a 5 % standard deviation, and the speed loop is a first-order lag of 3 ms — all illustrative; real torque–speed curves must be measured on a dynamometer. The stop model is the continuous-time model of Chapter 13 with random friction and measurement noise; Cpk assumes normality and stability.

On the statistics: the zero-failure formula and the Clopper–Pearson bound assume independent trials with a constant failure probability. In reality, 1000 consecutive trims on one unit see the thread, knife and temperature change, and failures may come in clusters; 99.5 % demonstrated on one unit is not 99.5 % on every unit, which is why DVT uses several units and environments. The case counts, durations and costs of the pyramid levels, the time split in Table 22-6 and the maturity exit conditions are illustrative values chosen to explain the method; a real project must set them from its own requirements, number of rigs and risks. The gateway API, topic names (areas lab, eol) and the test.result data type are examples; the definitions in Chapter 19 and the digital-factory specification govern. Finally, testing can only show that no problem was found under the conditions tested; it cannot show that there is no problem. Had the regression of Section 22.1 appeared only above 5200 r/min with the sweep stopping at 5000 r/min, it would have slipped through. The operating range of the cases must cover the whole range in the requirements, with some margin.

Shop-floor notes

  • “Hand trials found nothing” does not mean there is nothing: ask which speeds, how many times, at what voltage, and compare with Table 22-5.
  • If trimming is sometimes good and sometimes not, first see whether it happens only when stopping from high speed; check the firmware version and deceleration parameters, then the mains voltage and solenoid voltage (Chapters 13 and 14).
  • Field repair reports must record the serial number and the firmware and parameter versions, so they can be matched with end-of-line data.
  • After each firmware upgrade, run the EOL case set on at least one machine before rolling it out widely.

    AnswerNo failure: n ≥ ln 0.1 / ln 0.99 = 229.1, so 230 trials; one failure allowed: about 388 (found by trying successive n with r_lower from Section 22.6).
  2. A trimming test of 1500 trials has 3 failures. What are the point estimate and the 95 % lower confidence bound? Is REQ-TRM-01 met?
    AnswerPoint estimate 99.80 %; lower bound about 99.48 %, just under 99.5 % — not met. At most 2 failures are allowed in 1500.
  3. A change might raise the trimming failure rate to 1 %. To detect it each night with 99 % probability, how many trims are needed at least? At 3 s per trim cycle, how many minutes is that?
    Answern ≥ ln 0.01 / ln 0.99 ≈ 458.2, so 459 trims; about 23 min.
  4. Fifty stop errors have mean 0.62° and standard deviation 0.09°, upper limit 1°. Find Cpk and say whether it passes; with the same standard deviation, what is the largest mean that gives Cpk = 1.33?
    AnswerCpk = (1 − 0.62)/(3 × 0.09) ≈ 1.41, pass; mean ≤ 1 − 4 × 0.09 = 0.64°.
  5. Forty commits were merged between two nights and the HIL trimming case failed. At most how many bisection rounds are needed? With 3 min per round to build and flash and 13 min to run the case, how long at most? If the bisection case detected a bad commit only 90 % of the time, what could go wrong, and how would you avoid it?
    Answer⌈log₂40⌉ = 6 rounds, about 96 min. With insufficient detection a bad commit may be judged “good”, and bisection will blame a later, innocent commit; choose the most sensitive condition (such as top speed), increase the trials until detection is close to 100 %, or re-check the bisection verdict once.
  6. (Discussion) Section 22.9 says “port the HIL torque–speed curve into the SIL model”. Once that is done, can the nightly HIL trim sweep be dropped? Argue from the defect types each level of Section 22.2 can find.
    AnswerNo. With the curve added, SIL catches the same class of planning problem in minutes, which is good; but HIL also tests real-time behaviour (interrupt latency, bus timing) and the delays of the real drive and real solenoids, which are not in the SIL model. A sensible split is for SIL to stop most problems first and HIL to confirm with fewer trials, giving the time saved to other cases.
