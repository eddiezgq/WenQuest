The mechanisms described in Chapters 3 to 8 all hang on one main shaft and are coordinated by shaft angle through cams, links and eccentrics. Electronically controlled sewing machines have not removed these mechanisms: the needle bar, take-up lever and rotary hook are still driven by the main shaft. What has changed is that the main shaft itself is driven by a servo motor, while actions such as thread trimming, reverse stitching, presser-foot lift, stitch length and needle-thread tension, which used to be set by hand, foot and mechanical adjustment, have been handed over to solenoids, pneumatic cylinders and stepper motors, directed by the controller according to the main-shaft angle. This chapter explains how these “components” work, how fast they are and how to select them, laying the foundation for the electrical control in Chapters 14 to 17. How particular machines orchestrate them into a sewing cycle is left to the later chapters.

Two questions run through this chapter. The first is how the main shaft stops accurately at the needle-up position; this is the most typical task of the servo motor and the starting point for thread trimming in Chapter 14 and the programmable pattern sewer in Chapter 15. The second is how many degrees of main-shaft rotation an actuator’s delay corresponds to: a solenoid takes a few milliseconds to pull in, during which the main shaft has already turned through more than ten degrees, so the controller must issue its command early.

13.1 Opening: what is inside an electronically controlled lockstitch machine

Look under the table of an electronically controlled lockstitch machine, and the most conspicuous items are the servo motor at the rear of the machine head and the control box under the table. Fig. 13-1 divides the components into three groups: on the left, sensors, which feed information such as shaft angle, pedal position and thread breaks into the control box; in the middle, the control box, containing the main controller, servo drive, stepper drives, solenoid drivers and power supply; on the right, actuators, which turn the controller’s commands into motion.

Take one manufacturer’s direct-drive high-speed lockstitch machine as an example: a 400 W AC servo motor is coupled directly to the main shaft (direct drive), with a maximum speed of 5000 sti/min; feeding is done by a motor-driven “digital feed” mechanism, needle-thread tension and presser-foot pressure are both under program control, and the rated power consumption is 520 VA (a manufacturer’s catalogue). On a lockstitch machine of twenty years ago the main shaft was belt-driven by a clutch motor under the table, stitch length was set with the stitch regulator and tension by turning the tension nut; today all of these can be set on the control panel and changed by the program.

13.2 The main-shaft servo motor

From clutch motor to direct-drive servo　The old clutch motor ran continuously at its rated speed. Pressing the pedal engaged a friction clutch between it and the pulley; the further the pedal was pressed, the tighter the engagement and the higher the speed. Releasing the pedal applied a brake pad to stop the pulley. It wasted power and ran hot, and the stopping position was pure chance: an extra positioner was needed to stop at the needle-up position. A servo motor turns only when needed, and both its speed and position are decided by the controller. Direct drive also eliminates the belt: the motor rotor sits directly on the main shaft, with no belt elasticity or slip, so needle positioning is more accurate and the machine head is quieter.

The permanent-magnet synchronous motor　Almost all servo motors used on sewing machines are permanent-magnet synchronous motors (PMSM): permanent magnets are mounted on the rotor and a three-phase winding is wound on the stator. The stator currents produce a rotating magnetic field that drags the rotor round in synchronism. The torque the motor delivers depends on the relative position of the stator current and the rotor field: for a given current, torque is greatest when the field produced by the current is perpendicular to the rotor field. The drive must therefore know the rotor angle at every instant (read from the encoder) and distribute current among the three phases according to that angle. The three phase currents are transformed into two components for control: i_d along the direction of the rotor field and i_q perpendicular to it. For a motor with magnets mounted on the rotor surface, when i_d is held at zero the torque is simply proportional to i_q (motors with magnets embedded in the rotor have an additional reluctance torque, and at high speed a negative i_d is used for field weakening; these are not covered here):

T = k_t\, i_q

This is vector control. For the controller, it turns a three-phase AC motor into a simple motor whose “torque equals a constant times current”, and all the speed and position control that follows is built on this.

Three control loops　A servo drive contains three nested control loops (Fig. 13-2). The innermost is the current loop: it makes the actual i_q follow the torque command, is usually computed about every 0.1 ms, and is the fastest of the three. In the middle is the velocity loop: it compares the speed command with the actual speed and calculates the torque command with proportional-plus-integral (PI) action. The outermost is the position loop: it compares the target angle with the actual angle and calculates the speed command. The output of each outer loop is the command for the inner loop, and the further in a loop is, the faster it is.

The proportional gain of the velocity loop K_v has the unit N·m·s/rad, meaning “how much torque to apply for a speed error of 1 rad/s”. Its ratio to the moment of inertia J of the main shaft referred to the motor shaft, K_v/J, is roughly the bandwidth of the velocity loop: the higher the bandwidth, the faster the speed is pulled back when it departs from the command. The worked example in this chapter takes J = 6×10⁻⁴ kg·m², including the motor rotor, main shaft, handwheel and the referred needle bar and take-up mechanism; K_v = 0.2 N·m·s/rad, giving a velocity-loop bandwidth of about 330 rad/s. With a peak motor torque of 2.5 N·m, accelerating from rest to 5000 r/min takes at least J·ω / T = 6×10⁻⁴ × 524 ÷ 2.5 ≈ 0.13 s (all illustrative values).

The load on a sewing-machine main shaft has one peculiarity: it changes within every revolution. As calculated in Chapter 8, the needle bar and take-up lever accelerate twice and decelerate twice per revolution; their inertial torques vary periodically with shaft angle and grow with speed. The velocity loop must suppress this periodic disturbance; otherwise the main-shaft speed would surge and sag within each revolution, and the timing of feeding and thread take-up would wobble with it.

13.3 Needle positioning

When a seam is finished and the pedal released, the main shaft must stop at the needle-up position: the needle above the fabric and the take-up lever near its highest point, so that the presser foot can be lifted and the fabric pulled out, and the next seam can start cleanly. For thread trimming, the main shaft must first turn through the trimming angle range at a specified low speed and then stop at needle-up. Both require the main shaft to stop accurately in a very short time, typically tens of milliseconds up to a little over one hundred.

Needle positioning has two stages. First the velocity loop slows the main shaft from sewing speed to a very low positioning speed, for example 200 r/min. When the encoder shows that the shaft is only a certain angle short of the target (45° in this chapter’s example), control switches to the position loop, which covers the remaining angle and stops the shaft at the target angle. The output of the position loop is limited to the positioning speed, so while the error is large the shaft approaches at constant positioning speed, and it only begins to decelerate once the error is small.

Worked example　Fig. 13-3 shows four parameter sets simulated with this chapter’s model. The model includes the main-shaft inertia, 0.15 N·m of Coulomb friction and a little viscous friction; the current loop is simplified to a 0.5 ms first-order lag, and the peak torque is 2.5 N·m. The standard parameters are K_p = 200 s⁻¹, K_v = 0.2 N·m·s/rad and integral time T_i = 12 ms. The shaft overshoots the target by about 0.1°, is pulled back to around −0.5°, sticks there briefly because of friction, and is then pushed to the target by the integral action within about 0.1 s; counting from the switch to the position loop, it enters the ±0.5° tolerance band in 38 ms.

Raise the position gain to 500 s⁻¹ and the shaft overshoots by about 1.8°, swings back and forth once more and stops after 50 ms. The reason is that the position loop demands too violent a deceleration for the velocity loop to follow: the velocity-loop bandwidth is only about 330 rad/s, and the position gain is now larger than that. The rule of thumb is to stiffen the velocity loop first and then add the position loop; the position gain is generally set to between a quarter and a half of the velocity-loop bandwidth K_v/J, and the standard 200 s⁻¹ is already near the upper limit. Another limit comes from the motor itself. When the position loop first starts decelerating, the deceleration it demands is about K_p times the positioning speed, and this cannot exceed the deceleration the peak torque can produce, (T_max + T_f)/J, about 4400 rad/s² in the example. With K_p = 500 the torque sits at its peak for about 5 ms; the response is then no longer governed by the gain, and the overshoot grows by about another 0.5°.

Double the inertia without changing the parameters and the result resembles that of too high a gain: an overshoot of about 2.2° and 67 ms to settle. Fit a heavier handwheel, or add some other device to the machine head, and the velocity-loop bandwidth is halved; parameters that used to suit no longer do. Here the torque also hits its peak (the deceleration capability is halved), so simply increasing K_v is not enough; K_p must also be reduced somewhat. Every servo drive has an “inertia identification” or “load inertia ratio” parameter, which must be reset when the machine head is changed. One of the tasks in Virtual lab 13-1 is to retune the parameters after the inertia has been doubled.

Remove the velocity-loop integral (T_i = 0) and the shaft stops about 0.2° short of the target and stays there. This is a steady-state error caused by Coulomb friction: when the shaft has stopped, its speed is zero and only the proportional term of the velocity loop remains, so the torque output is K_v × K_p × e; as long as this torque is less than the friction torque T_f, the shaft cannot be moved. Without integral action, therefore, the shaft may stop anywhere within

|e| \le \frac{T_f}{K_v K_p}

which in the example is 0.15 ÷ (0.2 × 200) rad ≈ 0.21°. Where in this range it stops depends on how the shaft arrived. The integral term slowly accumulates this small error into torque until it can move the shaft; that is why the velocity loop uses PI action. The pause near −0.5° in the standard-parameter curve, followed by a slow push to the target by the integral, has the same explanation.

What happens if the needle-up stop is inaccurate? According to the timing chart of Chapter 8, the take-up lever reaches its highest point at 55° and the needle point does not enter the fabric until 101.7°; the needle-up position is set between these two. Stop too early, and the take-up lever has not yet reached its top. After trimming, only a very short thread end is left in the needle eye; when the next seam starts, the take-up lever continues to rise and may pull the thread end out of the eye, so the seam unthreads at the start. For this reason some machines reverse the main shaft slightly after trimming to bring the needle to its highest point. Stop too late, and the needle has already begun its descent and is too close to the fabric, so it easily catches the fabric as it is pulled out. On real machines the needle-up position is a settable range of shaft angle (Fig. 13-4b); stopping anywhere in that range counts as acceptable.

13.4 Encoders and sensors

Incremental encoders　An encoder is fitted at the rear of the servo motor. The most common, the incremental encoder, is a code disc with evenly spaced transparent slits and two sets of photoelectric detectors, A and B, offset by a quarter of the slit pitch (Fig. 13-4a). As the disc turns, A and B output two square waves 90° apart: A leading B means forward rotation, B leading A means reverse. Both rising and falling edges of both signals are counted, giving 4 counts per cycle; this is called quadrature (×4) decoding. An encoder with 1024 lines per revolution gives 4096 counts per revolution after ×4 decoding, a resolution of 360° ÷ 4096 ≈ 0.088°, which is ample for needle positioning. The disc also carries an index track with just one slit per revolution; after power-up the controller turns the shaft once to find the index and then knows the absolute shaft angle. A PMSM, however, needs to know the approximate rotor position before it can turn at all, even before the index has been found, so encoders of this type also carry three coarse-resolution pole-position signals (U, V, W); some machines instead use an absolute encoder or a magnetic encoder, which knows the angle as soon as power is applied.

With the absolute angle known, “needle-up” and “needle-down” are simply two intervals of shaft angle (Fig. 13-4b). The controller can stop when the angle enters the needle-up interval, can leave the needle in the fabric in the needle-down interval (used when pivoting at corners), and can issue trimming and wiping commands at particular angles within a revolution. Old-style positioners produced these two signals with two magnets mounted on the handwheel and Hall switches; the principle is the same, but the settings cannot be changed at will.

Low-speed speed measurement　Speed is calculated from the angle, by one of two methods. The M method counts the pulses in a fixed time interval (for example 1 ms); the count times a constant gives the speed. Its error is plus or minus one count, equivalent to 60 ÷ (4096 × 0.001) ≈ 14.6 r/min, so the lower the speed, the larger the relative error—about 7% at 200 r/min. The T method uses a high-frequency clock (for example 10 MHz) to measure the time between two counts; its error is plus or minus one clock period, and the higher the speed, the fewer clock ticks between two counts and the larger the relative error; at 200 r/min it is only about 0.14%. Fig. 13-4c plots the relative error of both methods; the curves cross at about 1500 r/min. Needle positioning is done at a few tens to two hundred r/min, exactly where the M method is worst, so servo drives use the T method at low speed, or the combined M/T method.

Other sensors　The pedal contains a position sensor (a Hall element or potentiometer): pressing forward with the ball of the foot sews, faster the further it is pressed; a light press back with the heel lifts the presser foot, and pressing the heel fully back trims the thread. Thread-break detection is done in several ways: by detecting whether the check spring swings with every stitch, or by passing the needle thread round a small wheel and checking with a photoelectric sensor whether it turns; if the needle thread breaks, the check spring stops swinging and the wheel stops turning, and the controller can stop the machine within a few stitches. Bobbin-thread remaining detection shines a light beam onto the side of the bobbin and raises an alarm when the thread is nearly used up and a reflective mark on the bobbin side is exposed; some machines simply estimate it from the stitch count. Fabric thickness is sensed by a displacement sensor on the presser bar, and fabric edges by photoelectric sensors, the latter being used most in automatic units (Chapter 12).

13.5 Stepper motors

A stepper motor’s rotor turns step by step as the currents in the stator windings are switched. The most common two-phase hybrid stepper motor has a step angle of 1.8°, i.e. 200 steps per revolution. Its greatest advantage is that it can position without an encoder: as long as it keeps up with every step, it turns as many steps as pulses are sent. Electronic feed, presser-foot pressure adjustment and the X–Y tables of embroidery machines and programmable pattern sewers (Chapters 15 and 16) use stepper motors extensively; some electronic tension assemblies use a stepper motor, but most use a proportional solenoid or voice-coil motor, where the current sets the clamping force.

Microstepping　If the two phase windings are simply switched on and off in turn (full-step drive), the rotor jumps 1.8° at a time and moves in jerks, with heavy vibration and noise at low speed. Microstepping drives the currents in phases A and B through small increments following sine and cosine (Fig. 13-5, left), so the direction of the combined field rotates continuously and the rotor comes to rest at positions between two full steps. With 16 microsteps there are 3200 steps per revolution, 0.1125° per step. Note that microstepping makes motion smoother but does not raise positioning accuracy in the same proportion: under a large load torque, the lag angle of the rotor behind the field direction changes, so the actual angle of each microstep becomes uneven.

Torque–frequency characteristic and lost steps　The windings have inductance, so the current cannot change instantly. The higher the step frequency, the shorter each step, the less time the current has to reach its set value, and the lower the torque (Fig. 13-5, right). If the load torque exceeds the torque available at that moment, the rotor cannot keep up with the field and loses steps: the controller believes the motor has arrived, but it has in fact fallen a few steps short, and the controller does not know it. On a sewing machine, lost steps in the feed mean inaccurate stitch length; lost steps on an embroidery frame mean the whole design is misregistered. Raising the drive voltage builds up the current faster and pushes the curve to the right, which is why stepper drives often use a supply voltage much higher than the motor’s rated voltage and limit the current by chopping. Stepper motors also have resonances at low frequency, where vibration becomes especially strong at certain frequencies and can even cause lost steps; microstepping and added damping both reduce this. When selecting a motor, leave an adequate margin between the load-torque curve and the motor’s torque–frequency curve; where demands are high, simply add an encoder to the stepper motor to make a closed-loop stepper.

13.6 Solenoids and pneumatic cylinders

Actions such as thread trimming, thread wiping, reverse stitching and presser-foot lift have strokes of only a few millimetres and only two states, “move” and “don’t move”, so a solenoid is the simplest solution. A solenoid is just a coil plus a movable iron core (the armature). When the coil is energised, the magnetic force pulls the armature in, which drives a linkage to perform the action; when power is removed, a return spring pushes it back.

How long does pull-in take?　The coil has a resistance R and an inductance L. When a voltage V is applied, the current rises with time constant τ = L/R and does not reach V/R immediately. The magnetic force is proportional to the square of the current, and only when the current has risen far enough for the magnetic force to exceed the spring force does the armature begin to move. The armature’s motion in turn changes the inductance and generates a back-EMF, so the current actually dips for a moment: this is the notch in the current curve of Fig. 13-6. Once the armature is fully pulled in, the air gap is smallest and the same current gives the greatest force, so a much smaller current suffices to hold it.

This chapter’s example takes a coil resistance of 8 Ω, an inductance changing from 20 mH with the armature open to 80 mH when pulled in, a stroke of 3 mm and a return-spring preload of 7 N (illustrative values). With a hold current of only 1 A, the magnetic force is insufficient and the armature will not pull in. Applying 3 A continuously will pull it in, but the coil then dissipates about 70 W and becomes too hot to touch within seconds. The practical solution is called boost (over-excitation): first apply the full voltage (24 V) so the current rises quickly and the armature is fully in after 6.9 ms; after 20 ms switch to chopping and hold the current at 1 A, dissipating only 8 W.

How long does release take?　When power is removed, the current in the coil does not vanish immediately; it must be given a freewheeling path, or the inductance will generate a very high voltage that breaks down the power transistor. The simplest method is a diode in parallel with the coil: the current decays slowly through the diode with a time constant still equal to L/R, and since the inductance is greatest with the armature pulled in, the decay is slowest. In the example, it takes 12.9 ms from power-off for the armature to return to its rest position. With Zener freewheeling instead, the coil voltage is clamped at several tens of volts at switch-off and the current quickly falls below the release current; with a 48 V Zener diode release takes only 5.1 ms, most of which is the mechanical time for the spring to push the armature through its 3 mm. This refers to the usual circuit: the power transistor on the low side of the coil, and the Zener diode (in series with a diode) across the coil. When the transistor switches off it must withstand “supply voltage + Zener voltage”, 24 + 48 = 72 V in the example, and a margin must be allowed when selecting it. On every release, the energy stored in the coil, ½Li² (about 40 mJ at 80 mH and 1 A), is dissipated in the Zener diode, so its power rating must be checked when the solenoid operates frequently. The 20 ms boost time in the example is also on the long side: the armature is fully in after 6.9 ms, so about 10 ms of boost is enough, which lowers the coil’s average heating somewhat further.

Converting to main-shaft angle　At a main-shaft speed of n (r/min), the shaft turns 6n/1000 degrees per millisecond. For trimming, the main shaft first slows to a few hundred r/min: at 400 r/min, 1 ms is 2.4°. In the example, the solenoid pull-in of 6.9 ms corresponds to 17° of main-shaft rotation; the release of 12.9 ms with diode freewheeling corresponds to 31°, and with Zener freewheeling to 12°. As Chapter 14 will explain, the trimming moving knife must act within a definite window of shaft angle, so the controller must issue the command early by this delay; and since the delay itself varies with voltage, temperature and spring wear, the main shaft is always slowed to a very low speed before trimming, so that the same number of milliseconds corresponds to fewer degrees. If neither pull-in nor release may exceed 20°, then with a 48 V Zener diode the longer pull-in time of 6.9 ms sets the limit, and the maximum trimming speed is about 20 ÷ (6 × 0.0069) ≈ 480 r/min. Pneumatic cylinders　The clamps, folding plates and stackers in automatic units (Chapter 12) mostly use pneumatic cylinders. Cylinders give large force and long stroke at low cost, but respond slowly: the solenoid valve takes a dozen or so milliseconds to switch, filling the cylinder with air and driving the piston through its stroke takes several tens of milliseconds more, and the speed varies with air pressure and load. Cylinders therefore suit “slow actions” performed once or twice per garment, not actions required at every stitch. Automatic presser-foot lift on lockstitch machines comes in both solenoid and pneumatic versions.

13.7 Power circuits

The power section of a servo drive is shown in Fig. 13-7. The 220 V AC supply is turned into DC by a rectifier bridge and smoothed by the DC-link capacitor; single-phase 220 V rectifies to about 310 V (this is the no-load peak value; it is slightly lower under load). The inverter bridge consists of six switching devices (IGBTs or MOSFETs) that switch at high frequency (several kHz up to 20 kHz) with duty cycles calculated by the vector control, delivering current to the motor’s three-phase winding.

Energy in an emergency stop　A main shaft turning at high speed stores kinetic energy ½Jω². In an emergency stop the servo motor becomes a generator and returns this kinetic energy to the DC link through the inverter bridge; but the rectifier bridge is one-way, so the energy cannot go back to the mains and can only charge the DC-link capacitor, raising its voltage. In the example, J = 6×10⁻⁴ kg·m² and the kinetic energy at 5000 r/min is about 82 J; assume 70% of it is returned to the DC link (the rest is dissipated in friction and the windings), about 58 J. If a 470 μF DC-link capacitor, initially at 310 V, absorbed all this energy, its voltage would rise to

V_2=\sqrt{V_0^2+\frac{2E}{C}}=\sqrt{310^2+\frac{2\times 58}{470\times10^{-6}}}\approx 584\ \text{V}

This far exceeds the voltage rating of the DC-link capacitor (typically 400–450 V) and is already close to the limit of 600 V switching devices, with no margin left (all values illustrative). The drive therefore connects a braking resistor in series with a switching transistor across the DC link: when the voltage exceeds a set value (for example 400 V), the transistor turns on and converts the surplus energy into heat in the braking resistor. With the example’s data, the braking resistor is required whenever the speed before the emergency stop exceeds about 2550 r/min. Another approach is to decelerate more slowly so that friction and winding losses absorb more of the energy, at the cost of a longer stopping time. Some drives also treat supply undervoltage, overvoltage, overcurrent and overtemperature as protection items, raising an alarm and stopping the machine when a fault occurs; these are the fault codes most often met in field service.

13.8 Controllers and communication

The main controller in the control box has to do several kinds of work at once: compute the current loop about every 0.1 ms, the velocity loop every 0.1–0.25 ms and the position loop somewhat more slowly (0.25–1 ms); issue feed, tension, trimming and other commands at every stitch according to the main-shaft angle; handle the pedal, control panel and various sensors; and communicate with other equipment. Depending on their real-time requirements, these tasks are often shared among different chips.

For the simplest electronically controlled lockstitch machine, one microcontroller (MCU) or digital signal processor (DSP) is enough: it has dedicated PWM outputs and an encoder interface; the current loop is computed in the PWM interrupt, actions such as trimming are triggered by an encoder interrupt when the main shaft reaches a specific angle, and the human–machine interface is handled in the remaining time. Programmable pattern sewers and embroidery machines have more axes and often use an ARM processor running a real-time operating system to handle pattern data, the interface and communication, with several servo drives and stepper drives each looking after one axis. The selectors of a computerised flat knitting machine must be fired needle by needle as the carriage moves at high speed, with timing accuracy at the microsecond level; such “many channels triggered simultaneously by position” tasks are given to a field-programmable gate array (FPGA), as Chapter 17 will describe.

The principle of division is this: the faster and more deterministic a task, the closer to the hardware it should be, in an interrupt or in the FPGA; the slower and more complex a task, the more it belongs in an ordinary task of the operating system. A common mistake is to handle an angle-triggered action such as trimming in an ordinary task: when the interface gets busy, the trim comes several degrees late.

Communication　Between the control box and the operator panel an RS-485 serial bus is commonly used: two wires with differential transmission, good noise immunity, and a modest but sufficient speed. Drives in multi-axis equipment communicate over the CAN bus, which has priority arbitration and error detection and good noise immunity, and is also used in cars. Where higher-performance multi-axis synchronisation is needed, for example in multi-head embroidery machines or high-speed template machines, the industrial Ethernet EtherCAT is used, with synchronisation errors between axes down to the microsecond level. Networking machines and sending output and downtime data to shop-floor systems uses another layer of protocols, covered in Chapter 26.

13.9 Design notes and limits of the model

This chapter can be summed up in a few points. For the main-shaft servo, tune the velocity loop first and then the position loop; retune when the inertia changes; the velocity loop needs integral action to eliminate the steady-state error caused by friction. Use the T method or M/T method for low-speed measurement. Select stepper motors with a margin on the torque–frequency characteristic, or they will lose steps. Drive solenoids with boost followed by chopped hold current, use Zener freewheeling for fast release, and convert the delays of all actuators into main-shaft angle at the speed at which they act. The energy of an emergency stop must have somewhere to go, so the DC link needs a braking resistor. Angle-triggered actions belong in interrupts.

All the models in this chapter are illustrative. The servo model is continuous-time, simplifies the current loop to a first-order lag, and does not account for quantisation and delay in speed measurement, PWM and sampling periods or encoder resolution, nor for the variation of main-shaft load with angle; inertia, friction and peak torque are illustrative values. The solenoid model represents the variation of inductance with air gap by a simple magnetic-circuit formula and ignores core saturation, eddy currents and the effect of temperature on resistance; the parameters of a real solenoid must be measured. The stepper motor’s torque–frequency curve shows only its shape. The braking example assumes that 70% of the kinetic energy returns to the DC link; this fraction depends on how fast the deceleration is.

Shop-floor notes

  • If after changing the handwheel, pulley or machine head the needle stops inaccurately or swings back and forth, first reset the load inertia in the servo parameters, then adjust the gains.
  • If the needle always stops slightly off, and in no consistent direction, the cause is most likely excessive friction or a disabled velocity-loop integral; first check that the main shaft turns freely.
  • If trimming works sometimes and fails at other times, measure the voltage and resistance of the trimming solenoid; low voltage or a hot coil slows the pull-in, and the trimming angle is delayed accordingly.
  • If an “overvoltage” fault appears on emergency stops, check the braking resistor and its switching transistor; the deceleration time can also be set longer.
  • If stepper-driven feed gives an erratic stitch length, first check for lost steps: load too heavy, voltage too low or acceleration too abrupt.
