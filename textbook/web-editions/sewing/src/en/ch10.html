The lockstitch machine forms its seam with a rotary hook and a bobbin; the overlock machine wraps the fabric edge with two loopers (Chapter 9). This chapter covers a third approach: a looper takes the place of the rotary hook, and the thread is fed straight from a large cone, with no bobbin at all. With a single needle the result is the 401 two-thread chainstitch; with several needles in a row sharing one looper it becomes 406 or 407; add a cover thread across the top of the fabric and you get 602 or 605. The latter are all sewn on coverstitch machines, and on the shop floor they are collectively called coverstitching. We first meet the chainstitch family, then follow how a 401 stitch forms, stitch by stitch. The key point is the flat ellipse the looper traces: on the forward stroke it catches the needle-thread loop behind the needle; on the return stroke it swings round in front of the needle so that the next needle penetration enters the thread triangle formed by the looper, the looper thread and the needle-thread loop. We then see how a coverstitch machine lays the cover thread with a spreader, and finish with thread consumption and the uses of these stitches in garments.

10.1 Opening: a T-shirt hem and the back rise of a pair of jeans

Turn a T-shirt over and look at the hem. On the face there are two parallel straight lines; on the back a single thread runs back and forth between them like a net, covering the raw edge of the folded hem. Now look at the back rise (seat seam) of a pair of jeans: there are two heavy rows of thread on both face and back, and pulling hard will not break them. Yet if you find the end of the seam, pinch the thread on the back and pull, the whole seam sometimes comes apart like a zip.

Both observations have to do with chainstitches. The T-shirt hem is a 406 coverstitch, whose net on the back lets the seam stretch together with the knit fabric. The back rise of the jeans is a two-needle 401 chainstitch: the loops interlock one inside the next, which makes the seam both elastic and strong — but precisely because the loops are only interlooped, the seam unravels if its end is not secured. To understand why, we must start from the structure of the stitch and how it is formed.

10.2 The chainstitch family

ISO 4915 divides stitches into six classes, as introduced in Chapter 1. This chapter covers three of them: class 100 single-thread chainstitches, class 400 multi-thread chainstitches, and class 600 covering chainstitches (coverstitches). Fig. 10-1 shows the face and back of several common stitches.

101 single-thread chainstitch uses only one thread. The needle carries the thread down to form a loop; the next penetration passes through that loop and forms a new one, each loop held by the next. It is the simplest stitch, but pulling the free end unravels the whole seam, so it is used only where the seam is meant to be removed or need not be secure, such as temporary basting or closing the mouths of sacks.

401 two-thread chainstitch adds a looper thread. The needle-thread loops no longer interloop with each other but with the looper thread. From the face, 401 is a single straight line, just like the 301 lockstitch; the back shows a row of chain links. It is much more secure than 101, more elastic than 301 and needs no bobbin, so it is used extensively on the long seams of jeans, work trousers and shirts. A multi-needle chainstitch machine (a waistband machine, for example) simply sews several rows of 401 side by side.

406 and 407 are “bottom covering” stitches: two or three needles share one looper. The face shows two or three straight lines; on the back the looper thread runs back and forth through the loops of each needle, forming a net-like cover. 602 and 605 add a cover thread to this: the spreader lays it back and forth across the top of the fabric, where it is tacked down by each needle thread, so the face is also covered by a net. Thus 602 is a two-needle, four-thread stitch (two needle threads, one looper thread and one cover thread) and 605 is a three-needle, five-thread stitch. Note the numbering: under ISO 4915, 406 and 407 belong to class 400 multi-thread chainstitches; they are merely sewn on coverstitch machines and called coverstitching on the shop floor. Class 600 refers specifically to stitches with a top cover thread, such as 602, 605 and 607.

Compared with 301, chainstitches have three advantages. The thread comes from large cones, so there are no bobbin changes and a long seam can be sewn in one run. The loops can be drawn out, so the seam stretches more than a lockstitch seam, which suits knit fabrics. And a coverstitch is covered on the back (602 and 605 on the face as well), so joining and edge-covering are done in a single operation. The costs are higher thread consumption (Section 10.6) and chains that can unravel from the end. A chainstitch cannot be locked by backtacking the way a lockstitch can; other means are needed, such as trapping the end in another seam.

10.3 Formation of the 401 stitch

As with the lockstitch, the chainstitch begins with the needle-thread loop. Recall from Chapter 2: after the needle reaches its lowest point and starts to rise, the needle thread on the side not protected by the long groove is gripped by the fabric and cannot follow the needle, so it bulges out as a loop on the scarf side. The lockstitch machine catches this loop with the hook point of the rotary hook; the chainstitch machine catches it with the point of the looper. The difference is that the rotary hook, having caught the loop, must carry it right round the bobbin case (and the bobbin inside it), whereas the looper simply moves to the left with the loop and then comes back, relying on its front-to-back offset relative to the needle. Fig. 10-2 divides one stitch into four instants.

First instant: loop catching (about 214° in this chapter’s worked example). The needle has risen about 3.4 mm from its lowest point and the loop has bulged out. The looper moves from right to left, and its point slips into the loop from behind the needle (the scarf side), close against the needle. The clearance between the looper point and the needle should be as small as possible (a manufacturer’s coverstitch manual gives 0–0.05 mm), and the point should be about 1.5 mm above the top of the needle eye (a manufacturer’s single-needle chainstitch machine instruction manual). Too low, and the point passes below the eye and misses the loop; too high, and it slides over the top of the loop.

Second instant: carrying the loop. The needle keeps rising and leaves the fabric, and the feed dog advances the fabric by one stitch length. The looper continues to the left until it reaches its leftmost point, and the needle-thread loop, hanging on the looper blade, is spread open. The looper thread comes out of an eye near the looper point and runs back to the previous stitch.

Third instant: entering the triangle (about 130°). The looper starts back from its leftmost position (about 356°). If it retraced its path it would collide with the next descending needle, so while moving back it also shifts slightly forward and passes in front of the needle. This is the needle-avoiding motion. By about 130° the looper blade is in front of the needle, and the looper thread runs diagonally from near the point back to the previous stitch; the blade, the looper thread and the loop just carried enclose a triangle. The next stitch penetrates exactly behind the blade and in front of the looper thread, entering this triangle.

Fourth instant: shedding and tightening the loop (about 176°). The looper retreats to its rightmost position, the needle-thread loop that was on the blade slips off the point, and the take-up mechanism draws it tight, so that it grips the looper thread — a length of looper thread that the new stitch’s needle thread has already passed through. The looper thread must also be paid out and taken up. As mentioned in Chapter 4, the looper thread is controlled by a separate looper-thread take-up cam or take-up plate; the manual specifies that when the needle descends until the point of the left needle is level with the underside of the looper, the looper thread should leave the high point of the take-up cam, slackening the looper thread so that the needle passes smoothly through the thread triangle. Meanwhile the needle carries the new needle thread to its lowest point, and as it rises again a new loop bulges out and the next cycle begins.

The 401 stitch therefore hinges on two moments. At loop catching, the looper point must be behind the needle and pass exactly into the loop. When the next needle descends to looper height, the point must already have swung round in front of the needle but not yet have retreated past it, so that the needle can enter the thread triangle. Both moments are determined by the looper motion.

10.4 Looper motion and timing

10.4.1 A flat ellipse

The looper motion is the sum of two components: a left–right swing, which decides when the looper reaches the needle, and a front–back needle-avoiding motion, which decides on which side of the needle it passes. The two are about 90° out of phase, so together the looper point traces a flat ellipse in the horizontal plane (Fig. 10-3, left).

This chapter uses an illustrative model to study the ellipse. The needle bar moves according to the slider-crank mechanism of Chapter 3, with a stroke of 31 mm (the same as the needle-bar stroke in a manufacturer’s coverstitch manual). Both the left–right and the front–back motions of the looper are approximated as simple harmonic: left–right stroke 30 mm, needle-avoiding amplitude ±1 mm, and the looper reaches its rightmost position at 176°, where the point is 3.2 mm to the right of the needle centre. These 3.2 mm are called the looper retraction (looper back-off), and are taken from the instruction manual cited above; the statement that “the looper is roughly at its rightmost when the needle is at its lowest point” is also from the manual, and the model places it 4° before bottom dead centre. The stroke and the needle-avoiding amplitude are not given in the manual and are illustrative values.

In this model the looper point passes the needle from behind at 214°, when the needle has risen 3.36 mm from its lowest point and the point is 1.5 mm above the top of the eye (this condition fixes the looper height). The point travels left to its leftmost position at 356° and then returns. The point of the next needle descends to looper height (about 6.1 mm below the throat plate) at 130°; at that moment the looper point is 1.4 mm to the left of the needle centre (about 0.95 mm from the needle surface) and 0.41 mm in front of the needle surface, and the needle enters the thread triangle. Only at 138° does the point return to the line on which the needle lies (Fig. 10-3, right).

10.4.2 Five criteria and the timing window

Writing the requirements above as criteria gives five in all:

First, at loop catching the needle rise must be within the range where the loop is large enough. Here we borrow the Chapter 5 model of loop size versus needle rise (largest at about 2.2 mm rise); this chapter takes a loop larger than 60% of the maximum as large enough (illustrative), corresponding to a needle rise of about 1.0–4.2 mm.

Second, at loop catching the looper point must be at least 0.2 mm above the top of the needle eye and below the top of the loop (the manual’s standard value is 1.5 mm).

Third, as the looper passes the needle on its forward stroke, the clearance between point and needle must be 0–0.1 mm (manual: 0–0.05 mm); a negative value means the looper strikes the needle.

Fourth, when the needle point descends to looper height, the looper point must still be at least 0.3 mm to the left of the needle and at least 0.2 mm in front of it; otherwise the needle cannot enter the thread triangle. Here, as in the third criterion, distances are measured from the needle surface.

Fifth, for the entire time the needle point is below looper height, the looper must not strike the needle.

Shifting only the looper phase, with everything else unchanged, the usable window is about −3.5° to +4° — less than 8°. The two limits have different causes. If the looper is advanced, the point comes back early and has already retreated past the needle before the needle reaches looper height; criterion four fails first, the looper thread is not passed through by the needle, and the chain falls apart. If the looper is retarded, the needle has risen too far at loop catching and the loop has shrunk; criterion one fails first, which is a skipped stitch. This window is comparable to, if slightly narrower than, the rotary-hook window of Chapter 5 (a little over eight degrees of main-shaft angle). The criteria differ but the reasoning is the same: the 401 looper uses a single motion to serve both loop catching and entering the triangle, so it is constrained at both ends.

The other quantities can be examined in the same way. Raising the looper (or lowering the needle bar) raises the point relative to the needle eye, and the loop is still caught up to about +1.7 mm; lowering the looper by more than about 0.8 mm makes the needle reach looper height later, and criterion four fails again. The looper retraction is usable between about 2.5 and 3.75 mm: too small and the point is too high at loop catching, too large and catching is too late. With all else at standard values, the needle-avoiding amplitude must be at least about 0.86 mm; any less, and the looper is still directly below the needle as the needle descends, so the needle cannot enter the triangle and may even strike the looper. All these figures depend on illustrative parameters; they only show how the quantities constrain one another and must not be taken as adjustment specifications.

The adjustment procedure in the manuals corresponds closely to these criteria. First, with the needle at its lowest point the looper should be roughly at its rightmost; set the looper retraction. Next, make the distance from the top of the needle eye to the looper point the same when the looper passes the needle on its forward and return strokes (called A = B in a manufacturer’s coverstitch engineer’s manual). Then align the marker line of the needle-avoiding mechanism with its standard position. Finally adjust the clearance between looper point and needle, and the needle guard. “A = B” means that the looper motion is symmetric about the needle bar’s bottom dead centre: on the forward stroke it passes the needle while the needle rises, on the return stroke while the needle descends, and the needle is at the same height on both passes. This chapter’s model does not fully achieve this. With the rightmost looper position at 176°, the needle has risen 3.4 mm at loop catching on the forward stroke, while on the return stroke the looper passes the needle when the needle is at the 5.0 mm-rise height, so A and B differ by about 1.6 mm. If, following A = B, the rightmost position is placed at 180°, the needle rise at loop catching becomes 4.15 mm, right at the upper edge of this chapter’s illustrative loop window. This shows that borrowing the rotary-hook loop model to judge the chainstitch is on the conservative side; the true loop-catching window of a chainstitch must be determined by measurement (Section 10.8).

10.4.3 Drive mechanism

The two components of the looper motion are usually produced by two separate mechanisms on the hook shaft (Fig. 10-4). One is an eccentric with a ball-jointed connecting rod: the eccentric drives the rod, which through a rocker arm oscillates the looper rock shaft; the looper is mounted on a looper holder on the rock shaft and swings left and right. Both ends of the rod have ball joints, because the rock shaft is not parallel to the hook shaft and the rod must move in space (the spatial four-bar linkage of Chapter 9). The other is a needle-avoiding eccentric, which through a forked lever pushes the rock shaft back and forth axially to produce the needle-avoiding motion. A manufacturer’s coverstitch engineer’s manual calls this arrangement a “ball-rod drive”: the combination of the rock-shaft oscillation and the needle-avoiding eccentric gives the looper its elliptical motion.

10.5 Coverstitch machines

The stitch-forming zone of a coverstitch machine contains three things: a row of needles, a looper and a spreader (Fig. 10-5).

The needles are held in a single needle clamp, in one row perpendicular to the feed direction. A manufacturer’s coverstitch machine offers needle gauges (the distance between the two outermost needles) of 3.2 mm and 4.0 mm for two needles and 4.8 mm, 5.6 mm and 6.4 mm for three needles, a maximum speed of 6500 sti/min and a needle-bar stroke of 31 mm, and can sew four stitch types: 406, 407, 602 and 605 (a manufacturer’s coverstitch machine instruction manual). Moving left, the looper passes the right, centre and left needles in turn and catches their loops one after another. Because each needle’s loop is caught at a different moment, the needles are set at slightly stepped heights so that the eye-to-point height is right for each one at its own catching moment. The manual specifies a clearance of 0–0.05 mm between the looper point and the centre needle, and gives the looper-to-right-needle distance for each needle gauge.

The spreader (also called the top cover-thread fork) swings left and right above the fabric. While the needles are up, it carries the cover thread to the left, pulling it across the paths of the descending needles; as the needles come down, each enters behind the cover thread, so that the cover thread is held down by the needle threads and tacked to the fabric surface. The engineer’s manual for another model from the same manufacturer specifies: spreader 7.8–8.2 mm above the throat plate, stroke 17–18 mm, and spreader at its leftmost when the needle bar has descended 1.1 mm from top dead centre. From the needle-bar motion of Chapter 3, a 1.1 mm descent corresponds to about 25°; the needle point reaches spreader height at about 77°, when the spreader is already moving back to the right and the cover thread is already laid in front of the needles.

Coverstitch machines are used mostly on knit fabrics, so they all have differential feed. On the machine above the differential ratio is 1 : 0.7 to 1 : 2 (for stitch lengths below 2.5 mm). Its purpose is the same as that of the differential feed on the overlock machine of Chapter 9: to keep knit fabric from being stretched into waves or from puckering. Hems and cuffs are sewn on a cylinder-bed head, whose lower part is a slim cylinder over which the tubular part of the garment is slipped; flat panels are sewn on a flatbed head.

10.6 Thread consumption

Chainstitches use far more thread than lockstitch, and in production this is a real cost. Fig. 10-6 gives the thread consumption ratios from a thread manufacturer’s consumption guide — the length of thread used per 1 cm of seam — at 7 stitches/cm, plus 10–15% wastage (a thread manufacturer’s data).

The consumption ratio is 2.5 for the 301 lockstitch, 5.5 for 401, 18 for 406, 25 for 602 and 28 for 605; for the overedge stitches it is 14 for 504 and 20 for 516. Coverstitches use 7 to 11 times as much thread as lockstitch, and most of it is in the looper thread and cover thread: the needle threads account for only 30% in 406 and only 20% in 602.

These figures can be understood with a simple geometric model. In each stitch, each needle thread travels one stitch length, passes through the fabric thickness twice (down and up) and leaves a loop below the fabric (about 1 mm per stitch). In each stitch, for each needle, the looper thread travels about two stitch lengths and wraps one loop, then travels one more stitch length, and also crosses the needle gauge once out and back. In each stitch the cover thread crosses the needle gauge once out and back, plus about 0.6 mm extra where each needle holds it down. Taking a fabric thickness of 0.5 mm, a loop depth of 0.5 mm and 7 stitches/cm, the model gives 6.1 for 401, 16.8 for 406, 23.2 for 602 and 31.0 for 605, within ±12% of the published data. The model also explains why the ratio changes with stitch density: in each stitch the segments “through the fabric thickness”, “loop” and “across the needle gauge” do not depend on stitch length, so the denser the stitching, the more times these fixed segments repeat per centimetre and the higher the ratio.

An example. A T-shirt has a hem of about 100 cm and two cuffs totalling about 72 cm, 172 cm in all, sewn with 406. At the published ratio of 18, each garment uses about 31 m of thread; for a batch of 1000 garments plus 12% wastage, that is about 35 km. Cones must be counted thread by thread: by the model, the two needle threads use about 4.6 km each and the looper thread about 23 km (about 32 km for the three together, some 8% less than from the published data). With 5000 m cones you need one cone for each needle thread and five for the looper thread, seven in all. Virtual lab 10-2 does this calculation for different stitches and parameters.

10.7 Uses in garments

Fig. 10-7 shows typical positions of chainstitch and coverstitch seams on a T-shirt and a pair of jeans.

T-shirts and knitwear: hems and cuffs are first folded and then sewn with 406 on a cylinder-bed coverstitch machine — two lines on the face hold the fold down and the looper thread on the back covers the raw edge, all in one operation. Necklines are sewn with 602 or 605 and a binder that wraps a binding strip round the neck edge. Joining seams in sportswear and underwear use 605 (the two plies slightly overlapped) or a 607 flatlock (flat-seam) machine (four needles, six threads; the two plies are butted edge to edge without overlap), giving a flat seam that does not chafe against the skin. All these places must stretch with the knit fabric; the loops of a chainstitch can be drawn out, so the seam does not easily snap.

Jeans: the back rise and the inseam are usually sewn as felled seams on a feed-off-the-arm chainstitch machine with two- or three-needle 401; the outseam is usually overlocked first and then topstitched. The two plies are folded into each other, the two rows of chainstitch pass through four layers, and all raw edges are enclosed. On a feed-off-the-arm machine the head is a long, slim cantilevered arm; the trouser leg is slipped over the arm, sewn, and comes off the end of the arm when finished. A manufacturer’s feed-off-the-arm double chainstitch machine comes in two-needle and three-needle versions: two-needle with a gauge of 3.2–6.4 mm and a maximum of 4000 sti/min; three-needle, for heavy fabric, with a gauge of 5.6–9.6 mm and a maximum of 3600 sti/min (a manufacturer’s product information). The waistband is attached in a single pass with a multi-needle chainstitch machine and a puller; belt loops are sewn continuously as a long strip with 406 and a folder, then cut to length.

The reasons for choosing chainstitch come down to three: long seams without stopping (no bobbin changes), elasticity, and appearance and function in one. The reasons for choosing lockstitch are just as clear: it saves thread, the end can be locked with a backtack, and the seam will not unravel from one end. So a single garment often combines several stitches, each chosen for its position.

10.8 Design notes and limits of the model

The analysis in this chapter yields several design notes for chainstitch machines. The looper motion must satisfy both moments — catching the loop and entering the thread triangle — at once, so its left–right stroke, retraction, needle-avoiding amplitude and phase must be designed together, not fixed one at a time with the rest adjusted to fit. The looper point must be able to slip into the loop close against the needle scarf. On a multi-needle machine the needle height steps must follow the order in which the loops are caught. The spreader phase must ensure that the cover thread is already laid in front of the needles as they descend. And the feed must be differential to suit knit fabrics.

The model in this chapter has clear limits. The looper motion is approximated as simple harmonic, whereas a real ball-rod mechanism has higher harmonics and the ellipse is not a true ellipse. The 30 mm stroke, the ±1 mm needle-avoiding amplitude and the allowable range of needle rise at loop catching are all illustrative values not given in the manuals. Loop size is borrowed from the rotary-hook model of Chapter 5, whereas the real loop depends on the type of thread, the needle scarf and the fabric; and the model’s standard setting does not satisfy the manual’s A = B (Section 10.4.2). The thread triangle is judged from the looper-point position alone, without considering looper-thread tension or its coordination with the looper-thread take-up cam. The thread consumption model is a geometric estimate that ignores thread stretch and tension. All of these must be corrected with measured data in cooperation with factories.

Shop-floor notes

  • If 401 skips stitches, check the looper timing and retraction first, then the clearance between looper point and needle and the needle height; after changing to a needle of a different size, readjust the clearance.
  • The end of a chainstitch seam must be trapped in another seam or secured with a knotting or backtack device; otherwise it will unravel from the end.
  • If a coverstitch machine makes knit fabric wavy, increase the differential ratio; if it puckers, decrease it (Chapter 9). If the cover thread does not lie flat, check the spreader height and timing first.
  • Coverstitching uses nearly ten times as much thread as lockstitch. Before placing an order, calculate the number of cones from the stitch type and stitch density, and stock several times more looper thread than needle thread.
