With the lockstitch machine behind us, we turn to the machine that, after the lockstitch machine, is the most numerous in a garment factory: the overlock machine. Three features make it unfamiliar to beginners: it has no hook or bobbin, its needle is slanted, and under the throat plate there are two oddly shaped loopers, one swinging sideways and one flipping up and down around the fabric edge. This chapter takes these mechanisms apart one by one on a “worked-example machine”. Its parameters are taken from a common high-speed three-thread overlock machine: needle bar inclined at 20°, needle-bar stroke 24.5 mm, maximum 7000 stitches/min, stitch length 0.6–3.8 mm, differential ratio 1:0.7 to 1:2. All the curves, instants and clearances in the text are computed by one and the same program, and 3D Model 9-1 is driven by the same data, so the figures, the text and the model agree with one another.

9.1 Opening: why isn’t a T-shirt side seam lockstitched?

Turn a knitted T-shirt inside out and look at the side seam: along the fabric edge you will see a dense row of thread loops wrapped around it. This is an overedge stitch. Why not sew two rows on a lockstitch machine, as for a shirt? There are three reasons, all to do with knitted fabric.

The first is elasticity. Knits such as single jersey can stretch by 30% or more, while a lockstitch hardly stretches at all; pull on the garment while putting it on and the thread breaks. An overedge stitch is made of loops interlooped with one another. Under tension the loops deform and thread slides from one place to another, so the whole stitch can stretch along with the fabric.

The second is edge finishing. The cut edge of a knit curls and unravels as soon as it is cut. While seaming, the overlock machine trims the edge straight with a knife and then wraps thread around the cut, doing three things — trimming, seaming and edge neatening — in one operation.

The third is speed and continuity. All the threads of an overlock machine are fed directly from large cones above the machine; there is no bobbin to change, so the machine can run for long periods without stopping. The main shaft can reach 7000 r/min, faster even than an industrial lockstitch machine.

These three points determine the whole structure of the overlock machine: to trim the edge, it has upper and lower knives; to wrap the edge, it has an upper looper that can travel around the edge; for elasticity, it forms loops with loopers instead of interlocking threads with a rotary hook; and because knits stretch easily, it has differential feed. Let us first look at the machine as a whole.

9.2 The machine as a whole

Fig. 9-1 shows the external appearance of the worked-example machine. The most obvious difference from a lockstitch machine is that there is no long arm: the head of an overlock machine is very short, the needle bar is hidden in the head at the upper left, and the needle that projects downward is not vertical but inclined 20° toward the operator. To the right of the needle is the knife, and the trimmed strip of fabric drops off on the right. On the right-hand side is the handwheel; turning it rotates the main shaft slowly, which is often done during adjustment. The space under the throat plate is closed off at the front by a looper cover, behind which are the loopers and feed dogs.

Making the housing transparent (Fig. 9-2) shows that all the mechanisms hang on one main shaft. The main shaft runs left to right through the base, with the handwheel at its right end. Several cranks (eccentrics) sit on the main shaft: one drives the rocking shaft of the lower looper through a ball-jointed link; another drives the rocker of the upper looper through a second ball-jointed link; the feed and the knife are also driven by eccentrics on the main shaft; and the needle bar is driven by a crank in the head. Unlike the lockstitch machine, the overlock machine has no division into “arm shaft, bed shaft and timing belt”: nearly every motion is taken from a single main shaft in the base, so the structure is compact and the speed can be very high.

The roles of the mechanisms can be remembered like this: the needle carries the needle thread through the fabric; the lower looper works below the fabric and catches the needle thread; the upper looper travels around the fabric edge and brings the lower-looper thread up onto the top of the fabric; the knife trims the fabric edge; and two feed dogs feed the fabric, one of which can feed more and the other less. We start from the stitch itself, because the stitch determines how these mechanisms must work together.

9.3 Class 500 stitches: 504, 514 and 516

Under the international standard ISO 4915, overedge stitches belong to Class 500, “overedge chainstitches”. Their characteristic is that at least one thread passes around the fabric edge. The three most used in garment factories are drawn in Fig. 9-3. So that the threads on both the top and the underside of the fabric are visible, the figure draws the edge “unfolded”: the upper half is the top of the fabric, the dark narrow strip in the middle is the edge, and the lower half is the underside, turned over.

504 is the three-thread overedge stitch, using one needle thread and two looper threads. Look first at the underside: at each stitch the needle thread passes through the fabric and leaves a small loop on the underside; the lower-looper thread (orange) passes through this loop and then runs diagonally to the edge. Now look at the edge: here the lower-looper thread and the upper-looper thread (green) interloop. Finally, the top: the upper-looper thread runs diagonally from the edge back to the needle position, where it is held down by the needle thread of the next stitch. So each of the three threads looks after one stretch: the needle thread “penetrates the fabric”, the lower-looper thread covers “underside to edge”, and the upper-looper thread covers “edge to top”. There is not a single tight knot in the whole stitch, and under tension the threads can slide at every interlooping point; this is why it is elastic.

514 is the four-thread overedge stitch. It has one more needle than 504, the two needles 2 mm apart. The second needle thread holds the looper threads down once more on the top of the fabric, making the stitch stronger and harder to pull open; it is commonly used for seaming knitwear. 516, called the five-thread safety stitch, is really two stitches sewn side by side: on the left a 401 two-thread chainstitch does the “seaming”, and on the right a 504 three-thread overedge stitch does the “edge neatening”. Chainstitch is strong, so 516 is often used to seam woven fabrics in jeans, jackets and the like.

This chapter follows the basic 504 stitch. 514 and 516 merely add a needle, or a set of chainstitch loopers, to 504; once 504 is understood, the others are not hard to follow.

9.4 Forming one stitch: three “loop entries”

A stitch on a lockstitch machine has only one critical hand-over: the hook point catches the needle-thread loop. A stitch on an overlock machine requires three hand-overs, each at a different place and a different instant. Understanding these three hand-overs is the key to the whole overlock machine. Fig. 9-4 draws four moments of one stitch from the calculation results of the worked-example machine, viewed from the operator’s side looking straight at the needle.

First hand-over: the lower looper enters the needle-thread loop (Fig. 9-4a). As on a lockstitch machine, the needle passes through the fabric, reaches its lowest point and starts to rise; because of friction between the needle thread and the fabric, the thread above the eye cannot follow the needle and bulges out into a loop behind the needle. At this moment the lower looper is swinging from right to left; its point passes close behind the needle and enters this loop. On the worked-example machine the lower-looper point reaches the back of the needle at a main-shaft angle of 213.6°, by which time the needle has risen 2.6 mm from its lowest point; the blade of the lower looper is only 0.05 mm from the needle. This clearance is too small to see with the naked eye, yet it must be held: too large, and the looper point may slip past outside the loop, causing a skipped stitch; too small, and the looper scrapes or even breaks the needle.

Second hand-over: the upper looper picks up the lower-looper thread (Fig. 9-4b). The lower looper carries the needle-thread loop on to the left; at its leftmost position its point is 3.8 mm past the needle. The lower looper is itself threaded: its thread comes out of the eye near the point and runs back to the right along the blade toward the cone, so a long, thin triangular gap opens between the blade and this thread. Just then the upper looper rises vertically from below, passes through this triangle and picks the lower-looper thread up onto itself. On the worked-example machine this happens at 270.8°; the upper looper passes in front of the lower-looper blade with a clearance of 0.10 mm.

Third hand-over: the needle enters the upper-looper thread triangle (Fig. 9-4c, d). The upper looper continues upward, goes over the fabric edge and then reaches almost horizontally to the left, to the upper left of the needle, carrying the lower-looper thread hanging on it up onto the top of the fabric. At its upper-left limit (22°) its point is 4.3 mm to the left of the needle and 5.3 mm above the throat plate; its own thread runs diagonally from its eye to the fabric edge, again forming a triangle with the blade. The needle, coming down from above, passes between the upper-looper blade and the upper-looper thread at around 57.5° (Fig. 9-4d), pinning the upper-looper thread at the position of the next stitch. Immediately afterwards the needle penetrates the fabric and the next stitch begins; in this way stitch follows stitch, end to end.

What the three hand-overs have in common is that in each a “point” enters the triangle that opens between another thread and the part carrying it. This makes two demands on the mechanisms. First, the timing must be right: the loop and the triangles stay open only very briefly — too early and they have not yet opened, too late and they have already closed. Second, the clearance must be small: the point must pass right against the carrier so that it does not miss on the outside of the thread. The slanted needle-bar mechanism, the lower-looper mechanism and the upper-looper mechanism described in the following sections are all, in the end, about meeting these two demands at the same time at high speed.

9.5 The slanted needle-bar mechanism

The needle-bar mechanism of the worked-example machine is, like that of the lockstitch machine in Chapter 3, an in-line slider-crank, except that the whole mechanism, together with the needle bar, is inclined by 20°. Take crank radius r = 12.25 mm and connecting-rod length l = 42 mm (λ = r/l = 0.292), giving a needle-bar stroke 2r = 24.5 mm. The needle point moves along the needle axis, and its displacement from the highest point is still given by the formula of Chapter 3:

\[s(\varphi) = r(1-\cos\varphi) - l\left(1-\sqrt{1-\lambda^{2}\sin^{2}\varphi}\right)\]

Because the needle is slanted, the vertical height of the needle point must be multiplied by cos 20°: at its highest the needle point is 9.5 mm above the throat plate, at its lowest 13.5 mm below it. With the fabric (two plies of single jersey, about 1.5 mm thick) lying on the throat plate, the needle point enters the fabric surface at 80.8° and leaves it at 279.2°; the needle stays in the fabric for 198° of shaft rotation, and the remaining 162° are used for feeding.

Why slant the needle? The most direct reason lies in the lower looper. The lower looper must pass close behind the needle and stay close to it throughout the short interval while the needle rises. If the lower looper swings about an axis parallel to the needle, its point travels on a circular arc in a plane perpendicular to the needle; wherever the needle happens to be in its rise, the distance from the looper point to the needle stays the same, so the 0.05 mm clearance is easy to maintain. When the needle is slanted, this rocking shaft is slanted with it, and the two stay parallel in space. Besides, the slanted needle frees the space directly beneath the head, so the operator can see the needle point and fabric edge directly from the front, which makes threading and alignment easier. The price is that the guidance and drive of the needle bar must be laid out along a slanted line, adding an angle to the machining datums of the head; this comes up again in Chapter 23 on machining the housing.

It should be noted that the formula above simplifies the needle-bar mechanism to “an in-line slider-crank with the crank above the needle bar”. In real high-speed overlock machines the needle bar is often not driven directly by a crank and connecting rod: an eccentric on the main shaft drives a needle-bar rocker back and forth through a link, and the other end of the rocker pulls the needle bar through a short link or slider; the needle bar slides up and down in two slanted bushings at the front of the head. This is done because the overlock head is narrow and the main shaft is far from the needle bar; a rocker can “turn the corner” and deliver the motion to the slanted needle bar, and the ratio of its two arms can be used to set the stroke. Such a mechanism is a planar six-bar linkage. Its needle-bar displacement curve is not exactly that of a slider-crank, but the stroke, the dead-centre positions and the instants of entering and leaving the fabric are very close, so the loop hand-over instants and clearances that concern this chapter are hardly affected; only when calculating inertia forces and balancing must the actual six-bar linkage be recomputed.

9.6 The lower-looper mechanism: a spatial four-bar linkage

9.6.1 Structure

The lower looper is a long, slender steel blade bent into an arc, with the point and the threading eye at the front and the rear clamped in the looper holder. The looper holder is mounted on the upper end of a rocking shaft parallel to the needle, so the looper point swings in a plane perpendicular to the needle. On the worked-example machine the distance from the looper point to the rocking shaft is R = 38 mm and the swing angle is 25.7°; the point swings from 13.0 mm to the right of the needle to 3.8 mm to its left, an arc length of about 17 mm. The blade is also made as an arc centred on the rocking shaft, so as it swings the entire blade slides along one and the same arc, never brushing the needle at one point and moving away from it at another.

The lower end of the rocking shaft carries a rocker arm of length c = 22 mm, with a ball at its end, connected through a ball-jointed link of length b = 22.48 mm to an eccentric on the main shaft (crank radius r = 4.36 mm). The key point here is that the main shaft runs left to right, while the rocking shaft is parallel to the needle, inclined 20° in the fore–aft vertical plane: the two shafts are neither parallel nor intersecting. A planar four-bar linkage requires all its revolute axes to be parallel, which is impossible here, so both ends of the link use ball joints, forming a “revolute – spherical – spherical – revolute” spatial four-bar linkage, RSSR for short. Fig. 9-5 is an oblique sketch of it; rotating the view in Model 9-1 shows it more clearly.

9.6.2 Calculating the swing angle

A spatial mechanism sounds complicated, but it is not hard to calculate; the key is to hold on to the single fact that “the link length is constant”. Let B be the ball centre at the end of the rocker and A the ball centre at the end of the crank. The position of A depends only on the main-shaft angle φ and is known; B rotates about the rocking shaft and can be written as

\[\mathbf{B}(\theta) = \mathbf{Q} + c\left(\cos\theta\,\mathbf{v}_0 + \sin\theta\,\mathbf{v}_1\right)\]

where Q is the centre of the rocker on the rocking shaft, v₀ and v₁ are two mutually perpendicular unit vectors perpendicular to the rocking shaft, and θ is the swing angle. The link length is b, that is |A − B| = b. Substituting the expression above and expanding, the squared terms cos²θ + sin²θ = 1 cancel, leaving a trigonometric equation in θ of first degree:

\[P\cos\theta + K\sin\theta = M,\qquad P = (\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{Q})\cdot\mathbf{v}_0,\quad K = (\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{Q})\cdot\mathbf{v}_1,\quad M = \frac{|\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{Q}|^{2}+c^{2}-b^{2}}{2c}\]

Its solution is

\[\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(K, P) \pm \arccos\frac{M}{\sqrt{P^{2}+K^{2}}}\]

The two signs correspond to the two assembly configurations of the mechanism; a real machine uses only one, so take whichever matches the installed position. For each increment of main-shaft angle, compute A once and obtain one θ; the position of the looper point is then obtained by rotating through θ about the rocking shaft. This is exactly what the book’s calculation program and Model 9-1 do.

For just a rough estimate of the swing angle, treat the link as pushing and pulling exactly along the direction of motion of B; the travel of B is then about the crank diameter 2r, so the swing angle Δθ ≈ 2r/c = 2 × 4.36 / 22 ≈ 0.40 rad, about 22.7°. The exact calculation gives 25.7°, 13% larger, because there is an angle between the link and the direction of motion of B: the ball-jointed link pushes obliquely in space. Fixing approximate dimensions with a rough estimate first and then correcting them with the exact formula is standard practice in mechanism design.

9.6.3 Worked example: motion of the lower looper

With the dimensions above, the lower-looper point reaches its rightmost position at 101.5° and its leftmost at 269.8°. The swing to the left takes 168.5° and the swing back to the right 191.5°: fast to the left, slow to the right. This asymmetry is no accident: the relative positions of the rocker, eccentric and link are deliberately offset so that the looper moves faster on the leftward stroke in which it catches the loop, freeing time for the upper looper that follows (Section 9.8 will show why this time is needed).

At 7000 stitches/min the angular velocity of the main shaft is ω = 733 rad/s, the maximum speed of the lower-looper point is about 6.9 m/s, and the maximum angular acceleration of the rocking shaft is about 1.4 × 10⁵ rad/s². The looper holder and looper together weigh only a few tens of grams, but they are far from the rocking shaft, so their moment of inertia cannot be neglected; they reverse twice in every stitch, and the ball joints and rocking-shaft bearings take heavy shocks. This is why the lower-looper holder of an overlock machine must be both light and stiff, and why the ball joints must use wear-resistant materials and be well lubricated.

9.6.4 Complete analysis of the spatial four-bar linkage

Section 9.6.2 computed only the swing angle. Whether a mechanism works, and works well, also requires answers to four questions: how many degrees of freedom does it really have; can the crank make full turns; how fast does the rocker turn and how large is its acceleration; and how “squarely” does the link push the rocker? These are explained in turn below, again using the lower-looper RSSR as the example; all the formulas apply equally to the RSSR of the upper looper.

Degrees of freedom　A free body in space has 6 degrees of freedom (3 translations, 3 rotations). A revolute joint leaves only 1 rotation, removing 5; a spherical joint leaves 3 rotations, removing 3. The RSSR has 4 links — frame, crank, coupler and rocker — and the mobility formula for spatial mechanisms gives:

\[F = 6(n-1) - \sum_i (6 - f_i) = 6\times 3 - (5 + 3 + 3 + 5) = 2\]

The result is 2, yet the machine has only one input, the main shaft. Where is the extra one? In the link itself: with ball joints at both ends, the link can spin freely about the line joining the two ball centres, and this spin does not affect the position of any other link. This is called a passive (idle) degree of freedom; removing it, the actual mobility of the mechanism is 1 — once the main-shaft angle is set, the position of the looper is determined. The passive degree of freedom does no harm to the motion, but at high speed it lets the link spin slightly and wear the ball sockets, so some designs replace one end with a cross-type universal joint to eliminate it.

Can it be assembled, and can the crank make full turns?　Return to the equation of Section 9.6.2, P cosθ + K sinθ = M. The left side can be at most √(P²+K²), so the equation has a solution only when

\[|M| \le \sqrt{P^{2}+K^{2}}\]

Its geometric meaning is intuitive: the ball-jointed link has a fixed length, and if the crank ball centre moves too far away or too close, the link cannot reach the circle described by the rocker ball; the mechanism cannot be assembled at that crank angle, and the crank cannot turn past it. For a planar four-bar linkage the Grashof condition tells at a glance whether the crank can turn fully; a spatial mechanism has no such simple formula, and the most reliable method is to step φ through a full revolution and check the inequality at every angle. On the worked-example machine, the minimum of √(P²+K²) − |M| over one revolution is still 5.2 mm, an ample margin; if the crank radius were increased from 4.36 mm to more than about 10.7 mm, there would be a range of angles with no solution, and the mechanism would jam.

Angular velocity　Velocity analysis again starts from “the link length is constant”. Let d = A − B be the link vector, r = B − Q the rocker vector, and e the unit vector along the rocking shaft. Differentiating d·d = b² with respect to time gives d·(Ȧ − Ḃ) = 0. B rotates about the rocking shaft, so its velocity is Ḃ = θ̇ (e × r); the crank rotates uniformly at angular velocity ω about the X axis, so Ȧ = ω (i × (A − C)), where C is the crank centre. Substituting gives the angular velocity of the rocker:

\[\dot\theta = \frac{\mathbf{d}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{A}}}{\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{t}},\qquad \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{e}\times\mathbf{r}\]

The numerator is the component, along the link, of the velocity of the crank ball centre; the denominator is the component, along the link, of t, the velocity of the rocker ball centre per unit angular velocity. The formula says something very plain: the velocities of the two ends of a rigid link must have equal components along the link, or the link would be stretched or compressed.

Angular acceleration　Differentiate d·(Ȧ − Ḃ) = 0 once more. The acceleration of the rocker ball centre has tangential and centripetal parts, B̈ = θ̈ t − θ̇² r; with the main shaft at constant speed, the crank ball centre has only centripetal acceleration, Ä = −ω² (A − C). Rearranging gives

\[\ddot\theta = \frac{|\dot{\mathbf{d}}|^{2} + \mathbf{d}\cdot\ddot{\mathbf{A}} + \dot\theta^{2}\,\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{r}}{\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{t}},\qquad \dot{\mathbf{d}} = \dot{\mathbf{A}} - \dot\theta\,\mathbf{t}\]

Both formulas have the denominator d·t. When it approaches zero, the link is almost perpendicular to the direction of motion of the rocker ball centre and cannot drive the rocker; the angular velocity and angular acceleration become very large. This is a dead point, and it leads to the fourth question.

Transmission angle　The link can transmit force only along its own direction d, while the rocker ball centre can move only along t. The angle between them is the pressure angle, and its complement is the transmission angle γ:

\[\sin\gamma = \frac{|\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{t}|}{|\mathbf{d}|\,|\mathbf{t}|}\]

At γ = 90° the link pushes along the rocker’s direction of motion and all of the force is used; the smaller γ is, the more “obliquely” it pushes, the larger the link force needed for the same output torque, and the greater the loads and wear on the ball joints and bearings. A common rule of thumb for planar mechanisms is a minimum transmission angle of no less than 40°, preferably more for high-speed mechanisms; the same rule applies to spatial mechanisms.

Most noteworthy is the transmission angle. The transmission angle of the lower looper lies between 49.5° and 75°, with its minimum near 223°, just after the looper catches the loop. If the rocker is moved 5 mm up the rocking shaft and the crank radius is readjusted to keep the swing angle at 25.7°, the leftward stroke becomes 174° and the minimum transmission angle rises to 64°; moved 10 mm, the strokes become fully symmetric (180°) and the transmission angle rises to 74°. In other words, the benefit of making the lower looper “fast to the left, slow to the right”, and so separating it in time from the upper looper, is bought at the cost of part of the transmission angle. Design consists of trade-offs between such conflicting demands.

The worked-example machine nevertheless keeps these upper-looper dimensions, for two reasons. First, the shortfall is very small. To drive the same resisting torque, the force in the link is proportional to 1/sin γ: at γ = 40° this factor is 1.56, at γ = 39° it is 1.59, only 2% more; even compared with the lower looper’s 49.5° (factor 1.32), the link force is only about 20% greater, which the ball joints and bearings can easily bear. Second, changing it is costly. Once the rocker shaft of the upper looper is moved, the instant at which it reaches its upper-left limit and the three clearances with the lower looper and the needle must all be rechecked, and the timing diagram of Section 9.8 and Model 9-1 must be recomputed as well. In engineering practice, a figure that “misses by only a little” like this is usually left as it is until the prototype has been run in; whether to change it is then decided from the temperature rise and wear of the ball joints. Relation to planar mechanisms　If the rocking shaft is turned parallel to the main shaft, the two planes of rotation become parallel and the RSSR reduces to the planar crank-rocker mechanism familiar from Theory of Machines and Mechanisms; the position, velocity and acceleration formulas above likewise reduce to the results of the planar vector-loop equation. So there is no need to regard spatial mechanisms as something entirely new: every step follows from the single constraint “the link length is constant”, only with vectors in three dimensions instead of two. Virtual lab 9-2 puts the three cases side by side: you can switch between the lower-looper, upper-looper and planar crank-rocker presets, adjust each dimension and the angle between the two shafts, and see the four curves and the step-by-step calculation at any crank angle in real time.

9.7 The upper-looper mechanism: the hook that goes around the edge

9.7.1 The path the upper looper must follow

The upper looper has the hardest job. It must pick up the lower-looper thread beneath the throat plate and then carry it onto the top of the fabric, to the left of the needle. The problem is that the fabric lies on the throat plate, from the needle all the way to the fabric edge 4 mm to the right. The upper looper cannot pass through the fabric; it must first travel vertically upward to the right of the edge, and only after rising above the fabric surface turn and reach horizontally to the left. Such a path looks like an upside-down “hook”: the lower part almost vertical, the upper part almost horizontal, with a sharp bend in between.

A simple rocker cannot do this: a point on a rocker can only travel on a circular arc, which either does not bend sharply enough and passes through the fabric, or has such a large radius that it carries the looper far off to the right. Common overlock machines use an “oscillating guide-bar” mechanism: the upper looper is mounted on the upper end of a guide bar, the guide bar passes through a guide bushing that can swing, and the lower end of the guide bar is pushed along by a rocker. When the rocker pushes, the guide bar both slides up and down in the bushing and swings about it; the two motions combine to trace out that hook.

9.7.2 Structure

The left side of Fig. 9-6 shows the upper-looper mechanism of the worked-example machine. The whole mechanism lies in a plane tilted back by about 14°, and the figure is drawn looking perpendicular to this plane. The rocker swings about shaft O; it is 30 mm long and swings through 50.5°. Its other, driving arm is 20 mm long and is connected through a ball-jointed link (29.47 mm long) to an eccentric of radius 7.38 mm on the main shaft. Here too the main shaft and the rocker shaft are not parallel, so this stage is again an RSSR spatial four-bar linkage, calculated exactly as in Section 9.6.2. The other end of the rocker, Q, is hinged to the guide bar; the guide bar passes upward through the oscillating guide bushing G, and the upper looper is clamped at its top. The bushing can only swing, not translate; the guide bar slides in it over a range of 20.7–44.1 mm, and swings through about ±10°.

9.7.3 Deriving the looper-point position

First think the problem through: once the position of hinge Q is known, the direction of the guide bar is fixed, because the guide bar must pass through the fixed bushing centre G. Let the unit vector along the guide bar be

\[\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{Q}}{|\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{Q}|}\]

The upper looper is curved, so its point does not lie on the axis of the guide bar; it is at a distance L from Q along the guide bar and offset sideways by h. With n denoting the unit vector in the plane of the mechanism perpendicular to u, the position of the looper point is

\[\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{Q} + L\,\mathbf{u} + h\,\mathbf{n}\]

On the worked-example machine L = 60.06 mm and h = 9.96 mm. Main-shaft angle φ → rocker angle ψ from the RSSR → Q → u and n → T: every step is elementary geometry.

This formula explains where the hook-shaped path comes from. For each small movement of Q, the angle through which the guide bar turns equals the movement of Q perpendicular to the guide bar divided by |QG|. When the upper looper is low, |QG| is as long as 44 mm and the guide bar hardly turns; the looper point mainly follows the sliding of the guide bar, so it travels vertically. When it rises high, |QG| shortens to 21 mm, the same push turns the guide bar more than twice as fast, and since the looper point is far from the bushing, it is flung almost horizontally to the left. This property of the oscillating guide bar is exactly what the “first vertical, then horizontal” path requires.

The right side of Fig. 9-6 shows the computed path of the looper point. The rising part is almost vertical, 5.4–7.2 mm to the right of the needle, just outside the fabric edge; as it passes the edge the looper point is already 3.5 mm above the throat plate, 2 mm above the fabric surface; it then reaches horizontally to the left and arrives at its upper-left limit at 22°, 4.3 mm from the needle, which falls right inside the factory setting standard of “4–4.5 mm”. On the way down it retreats along almost the same path and reaches its lowest point at 219°. The rise takes 163° and the descent 197°.

9.7.4 Why the looper has the shape it has

Looking after the point alone is not enough: once the point has gone over the fabric edge, the body of the looper following behind must go over too, again without touching the fabric or the throat plate. The solution is an interesting one: the looper’s outline can be made to follow the path its own point has travelled. Transform the positions of the point at successive instants into the looper’s own coordinate frame at its highest position and join them up — that is the shape of the looper. Then, as the looper moves, its body runs roughly within the “channel” cleared by its point, like railway carriages following the locomotive along the track. Having generated the looper outline this way, the worked-example machine was checked degree by degree over a full revolution: at no instant does the looper body enter the space occupied by the throat plate or the fabric. This is the origin of the familiar shape of real upper loopers — flat at the front, sharply bent in the middle, vertical at the rear.

At 7000 stitches/min the maximum speed of the upper-looper point is about 10 m/s and its maximum acceleration about 1260 times the acceleration due to gravity, even more violent than for the lower looper. So the upper looper and guide bar must be as light as possible, and the guide bushing must have a small clearance and resist wear. This bushing is one of the usual places where high-speed overlock machines develop noise and wear.

9.8 Coordination and clearances of the three

Plotting the motions of the needle, lower looper, upper looper, feed dog and upper knife on one time axis gives the timing diagram of the overlock machine (Fig. 9-7). When reading it, keep your eye on the six instants marked by vertical dashed lines: they are the three hand-overs described in Section 9.4 and the instants at which the needle enters and leaves the fabric.

Three pairs of “must not collide” relations can be read from the diagram, and they determine the phases of the mechanisms.

First, the needle and the lower looper. The needle passes downward through the plane of motion of the lower looper at 125°, when the lower-looper point is 12.2 mm to the right of the needle and the whole blade is further right still, so the needle’s way is clear. By the time the lower-looper point reaches the back of the needle at 213.6°, the needle has already begun to rise, and the two pass each other with a clearance of only 0.05 mm.

Next, the upper looper and the lower looper: the tightest pair in the whole machine. In one cycle the upper looper crosses the plane of motion of the lower looper twice: on the way up at 270.8°, deliberately passing through the lower-looper thread triangle, with a clearance of 0.10 mm; and on the way down at 162.5°, when the lower looper is coming across from right to left, its point at 7.9 mm while the upper looper is at 6.5 mm — only 1.4 mm apart. This is why Section 9.6.3 makes the lower looper “fast to the left, slow to the right”: the instant of catching the loop is fixed by the needle (213.6°), so a fast leftward swing means it stays longer on the right and sets off later. Recompute the worked-example machine with the lower looper swinging symmetrically (180° each way), and it sets off from its rightmost position about 5° earlier; at 162.5° its point is already at 7.2 mm, only 0.7 mm from the upper looper. In other words, the asymmetric design doubles the safety margin at this point. At high speed, clearances in the parts and elastic deformation eat up part of the margin as well, so 0.7 mm is already very tight.

The third pair is the needle and the upper looper. When the upper looper reaches its upper-left limit at 22°, the needle point is still 3.7 mm above it; then the upper looper begins to withdraw, and the descending needle passes between its blade and its thread at 57.5°, with a clearance of 0.10 mm. The upper-looper triangle must be open before the needle arrives, and must not close until the needle has passed through.

The requirements for feed and knife are much simpler. The feed dogs rise above the throat plate and feed between 292° and 68°, while the needle leaves the fabric at 279.2° and enters it at 80.8°, leaving a margin of about 13° at each end, so the fabric moves only when the needle is not in it. The upper knife reaches its lowest point at 180°, when the needle is in the fabric and the fabric is stationary, so the cut is clean.

In the factory, these clearances and instants are what “setting the loopers” is about. The mechanic adjusts the distance from the looper points to the needle, the height of the looper points and the crossing clearance between the loopers; get them wrong and the result is skipped stitches, broken needles, or even the two loopers colliding. From the design point of view, these clearances are of the order of a tenth of a millimetre while the looper points move at about ten metres per second; manufacturing errors in the parts, the clearances in the ball joints and bushing, and elastic deformation at high speed all eat into this small margin. This is the fundamental reason why the overlock machine demands such high machining accuracy.

9.9 The trimming mechanism

The trimming mechanism works like a pair of scissors. The lower knife is fixed to the right of the throat plate, with its edge facing up and flush with the throat-plate surface; its inner edge is the trimming line, 4 mm from the needle, which is also the stitch width. The upper knife is mounted on a knife holder driven by an eccentric on the main shaft and moves up and down close against the outside of the lower knife. On the worked-example machine the upper-knife stroke is 6 mm; at its highest the edges are 5.2 mm apart and the fabric is fed in between the two knives; at its lowest the upper-knife edge passes 0.8 mm below the lower-knife edge, and the fabric edge is cut off (Fig. 9-8).

Trimming quality depends on three things. First, the fabric must be stationary during the cut, so the upper knife’s lowest point is placed at 180°, while the needle is in the fabric. Second, there must be no gap between the two knives: the upper knife is pressed lightly against the lower knife by a spring, otherwise thin material is drawn into the gap and frays. Third, the overlap must be right: too small and the knife does not cut through, too large and the edges wear quickly; it is usually set at 0.5–1 mm. The lower knife is generally made of cemented carbide or high-speed steel and the upper knife of tool steel; when the edges become dull, the upper knife is normally replaced first.

9.10 Differential feed

9.10.1 Why two feed dogs?

A lockstitch machine has only one feed dog, but an overlock machine has one in front of the needle and one behind it (Fig. 9-9). The one behind the needle is the main feed dog, which moves the fabric on by one stitch length s per stitch; the one in front of the needle is the differential feed dog, whose stroke s_d may differ from that of the main feed dog. The ratio of the two is the differential ratio:

\[D = \frac{s_d}{s}\]

Why do this? Go back to the T-shirt of Section 9.1. While a knit is being sewn, the presser foot holds back the upper ply while the main feed dog pulls forward from below, so the loops are easily stretched. The stitch “sets” this stretched edge; when the seam is released after sewing, the fabric tries to shrink back, but the thread does not, so the excess length can only bulge into waves — the so-called “lettuce” or wavy edge. If the differential dog in front of the needle feeds a little more fabric per stitch, the fabric between the two dogs is pushed together slightly, exactly cancelling the stretch. Conversely, thin, slippery woven fabrics (such as chiffon) are easily pushed together and puckered under the presser foot, so the differential dog should feed a little less and gently stretch the fabric.

9.10.2 How the differential ratio is adjusted

The horizontal motion of both feed dogs is driven by the same feed rocking shaft, which swings through the same angle α each stitch. The main feed dog is connected to an arm of fixed length l_m; the differential dog is connected to a slotted rocker with a movable slide block in the slot, whose distance l_d from the rocking shaft can be adjusted. For the same swing angle, the distance moved by the end of an arm is proportional to the arm length, so

\[D = \frac{s_d}{s} \approx \frac{l_d}{l_m}\]

The differential adjusting lever on the outside of the machine moves this slide block: moving the block outward lengthens l_d and increases D, pushing the fabric together; moving it inward decreases D, stretching the fabric. The adjustment range of common high-speed overlock machines is 1:0.7 to 1:2, i.e. D from 0.7 to 2. The relation is written as “approximately equal” because the angles of the slide block and link change slightly during the swing; exact values must be calculated from the actual linkage.

9.10.3 A simplified model and worked example

In the factory the right differential ratio is found by test sewing, but a simple model makes the principle clear. Suppose that at a differential ratio of 1 the edge of a given fabric is stretched by a fraction ε₀ during sewing, and that the extra travel of the feed dog is not all transmitted to the fabric: call the transmitted fraction η (the gripping efficiency of the teeth). The differential ratio actually acting on the fabric and the final strain of the fabric edge are then

\[D_e = 1 + \eta\,(D-1),\qquad \varepsilon = \frac{1+\varepsilon_0}{D_e} - 1\]

ε greater than 0 means the seam thread is longer than the fabric and the edge waves; less than 0 means the fabric is longer than the thread and is gathered into fine puckers. Setting ε = 0 gives the differential ratio that keeps the edge flat:

\[D^{*} = 1 + \frac{\varepsilon_0}{\eta}\]

Take single jersey as an example, with ε₀ = 18% and η = 0.85 (illustrative values chosen by this book to explain the point; real values must be measured by test sewing). Then D* = 1 + 0.18/0.85 = 1.21. That is, with the differential ratio set to about 1.2, the side seam of a jersey T-shirt will not wave. If D = 1 is used instead, the edge gains 18% extra length. How high will the waves be? Treat the waves as a sine curve of wavelength λ and height A; its arc length exceeds the straight length by a fraction of about (πA/λ)². Setting this equal to ε gives

\[A \approx \frac{\lambda\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{\pi}\]

For a jersey edge the wavelength is about 30 mm; with ε = 0.18, A ≈ 30 × 0.42 / 3.14 ≈ 4 mm, already a defect visible at a glance.

Different fabrics have different ε₀ and η, and hence different flat-edge differential ratios (Fig. 9-10). Rib knit stretches more easily than jersey and needs about 1.4; less elastic sweatshirt fleece needs about 1.1; medium-weight twill hardly deforms and uses 1.0; chiffon needs less than 1, about 0.94. Where the curves flatten out at either end, the pushing or stretching has exceeded what the fabric can take, the feed dogs begin to slip under the fabric, and changing D any further has no effect.

The differential can also be deliberately “overdone”. Sewing jersey with a small differential ratio of about 0.8 stretches the edge by more than 40%, producing a decorative ruffled edge after sewing, common in women’s and children’s wear and known as a “lettuce edge”; sewing chiffon with a large differential ratio above 1.5 gathers the fabric into fine puckers in a single pass. The same mechanism, set at different positions, can either remove a defect or produce a deliberate effect. Virtual lab 9-1 lets you try this yourself: choose a fabric, drag the differential ratio, and watch the edge go from wavy to flat to finely gathered.

9.11 Design notes

Having read this chapter, we can string together the line of thought behind overlock machine design. Everything starts from the stitch: the 504 stitch requires three “loop entries”, each of which must happen at the right instant with a clearance of a tenth of a millimetre. To make the lower looper always pass the needle with the same clearance, its rocking shaft is made parallel to the needle, so the needle and rocking shaft are slanted together; since the main shaft and rocking shaft are not parallel, a ball-jointed link forming a spatial four-bar linkage is the only option. To make the upper looper go around the fabric edge without touching the fabric, an oscillating guide bar traces the “first vertical, then horizontal” hook-shaped path, and the looper body is bent to follow that path. To keep the two loopers out of each other’s way, the asymmetry of the mechanism is used to arrange the order in which they advance and retreat. The knife and the feed must either avoid the period when the needle is in the fabric or, conversely, make use of it. Finally, because knits stretch easily, a differential feed dog with an adjustable stroke is required.

From the manufacturing point of view, the difficulties of the overlock machine are concentrated in three places. The first is the positional accuracy of the whole stitch-forming zone: the mounting bores for the needle bar, the two rocking shafts and the bushing in the housing do not lie in the same direction, yet their relative positions must be accurate to a few hundredths of a millimetre. The second is the ball joints and bushing at high speed: they must have small clearances, resist wear and be well lubricated, and modern machines mostly use fully enclosed automatic lubrication. The third is the looper itself: its shape is determined directly by its motion path, and the geometry of its point and eye affects the reliability of loop formation, making it the most “particular” part of the overlock machine. These topics are developed further in Chapter 23 on manufacturing processes.
