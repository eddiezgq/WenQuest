Chapters 3 to 8 worked out the mechanisms of a lockstitch machine one by one: the inertia forces of the needle bar, the thread supply of the take-up lever, the loop-catching window of the rotary hook, the path of the feed dog, and the timing and balancing of the whole machine. But a design that is correct on paper does not guarantee that the machine as built will work. The parts have to be made from suitable materials by suitable processes, the dimensional errors of dozens of parts accumulate layer upon layer, they have to be adjusted into place during assembly, and the finished machine must still pass vibration, noise and life tests. Following the path “from drawing to volume production”, this chapter explains how the key parts are made, how errors are controlled and how machines are inspected, and finally how standards and patents constrain design.

23.1 Opening: a lockstitch machine from drawing to volume production

A new model goes through roughly the following steps from project approval to volume production (Fig. 23-1). First come the design inputs: what kind of machine the target market wants — maximum speed, stitch-length range, suitable fabrics, noise, price — and the standards it must meet. Next comes concept and detailed design: mechanism calculations, 3D modelling, part drawings and tolerances. Design reviews bring in people from manufacturing engineering, quality and after-sales service to look at the drawings and ask three questions: can this part be made economically (manufacturability), can it be fitted easily and accurately (assemblability), and can it be repaired when it fails? Then prototypes are built and put through performance, vibration, noise and life tests according to a test plan; problems found lead to drawing changes, often over several rounds. Once the prototype design is frozen, a small pilot batch is produced first to validate the tooling, processes and inspection methods and to collect yield statistics, and only then does the model enter volume production.

The most common lesson along this path is that the later a problem is found, the more it costs to fix. Changing a dimension on a drawing costs almost nothing; changing it after the moulds have been made means remaking the moulds; changing it after the machines have been sold means recalls and field visits. Calculation and review in the design phase and testing in the prototype phase are all ways of finding problems early at lower cost.

23.2 Materials and processes for the key parts

By working conditions, the parts of a lockstitch machine fall into several groups: parts in high-speed sliding contact that must resist wear (rotary hook, bobbin-case holder, needle bar and needle-bar bushing, feed dog); parts under alternating loads that need strength and stiffness (main shaft, hook shaft, cranks, connecting rods); parts that support and locate everything else (housing, bed); and transmission parts (gears, timing belt). Their materials and processes differ (Fig. 23-2; the materials and hardnesses are illustrative of common practice — a manufacturer’s drawings govern in each case).

Rotary hook　The rotary hook is the most precise part in a lockstitch machine. The hook point sweeps past less than 0.1 mm from the needle at a speed of ten-odd metres per second (Chapter 5), and the bobbin-case holder slides at high speed in the race, so the hook point and the race must be very hard and wear-resistant while also having an accurate shape and very low surface roughness. Common practice is to machine the hook from alloy steel and then carburise and quench it, or through-harden it, to reach a very high surface hardness, followed by finish-grinding of the race and lapping of the hook point by hand or machine. Lubricating the hook has always been difficult: too much oil soils the fabric, too little causes heat and wear. Rotary hooks with a diamond-like carbon (DLC) coating have appeared in recent years: a very hard carbon film with a very low friction coefficient is deposited on the surfaces of the hook body and the bobbin-case holder. One manufacturer’s product literature claims a surface hardness 86% higher than an ordinary hook and 33% less heat generation, with only very little oil needed (a manufacturer’s product literature (in Chinese)). This is the low-oil hook mentioned in Chapters 5 and 8.

Needle bar and needle-bar bushing　The needle bar reciprocates eighty or ninety times a second, with inertia forces of several hundred newtons at bottom dead centre (Chapter 3); it slides in the needle-bar bushing and must be both wear-resistant and straight. Bearing steel or alloy steel is commonly used, quenched and finish-ground, and the surface may be hard-chrome plated; some models use a hollow needle bar to reduce the reciprocating mass (Chapter 8). The clearance between the needle bar and its bushing must be very small: if it is large, the needle bar wobbles during loop catching, which directly affects the hook-point clearance (Section 23.3).

Shafts, cranks and connecting rods　The main shaft and hook shaft carry alternating bending and torsional loads. They are commonly made of medium-carbon or alloy steel, quenched and tempered, with the journals hardened and then ground to ensure accurate fits with the bearings and bushings. The counterweight on a crank (Chapters 3 and 8) must be dynamically balanced together with the crank.

Housing and bed　The housing is the “skeleton” of the whole machine: it determines the relative positions of all shaft bores and guideways, and its stiffness in turn determines the vibration behaviour (Section 23.6). Housings of industrial lockstitch machines have traditionally been cast in grey cast iron, which damps vibration well, is stiff and is easy to machine; some are die-cast in aluminium alloy to save weight. After casting, the blank must be aged to relieve internal stresses, or it will slowly distort after machining; the critical shaft bores must be bored on a machining centre in a single setup to guarantee their parallelism and centre distances.

Feed dog, throat plate and transmission parts　The teeth of the feed dog must be hardened; the tooth form and tooth height determine the feeding ability and the damage done to the fabric. The edge of the needle hole in the throat plate must be smooth so that it does not scrape the thread. The timing belt or gears between the main shaft and the hook shaft must guarantee an exact ratio (a full rotary hook turns at twice the speed of the main shaft); timing belts run quietly without lubrication and are the mainstream choice in modern lockstitch machines.

The complete process routings, key operations and inspection methods for four kinds of parts — needles, rotary hooks, housings and cams — are given in Chapters 24 and 25; how these routings are brought into the digital factory and linked to quality data is covered in Chapter 27.

23.3 The accuracy chain: where the hook-point clearance comes from

Dimensional chain　Chapter 5 stated that the clearance between the hook point and the needle scarf must be kept at 0.04–0.1 mm (the requirement in a manufacturer’s operating manual). This clearance of only a few tens of micrometres is determined jointly by the dimensions and positions of a long series of parts (Fig. 23-3): the position of the needle-bar bushing bore in the housing, the play between the needle bar and its bushing, the offset of the needle-clamp hole from the needle-bar axis, the distance from the needle’s scarf face to the needle-bar axis and the twist of the needle as fitted; and, on the other side, the position of the hook-shaft bore, the distance from the hook point to the hook’s mounting datum, and the radial play of the hook shaft. This series of dimensions links end to end and finally closes on the “clearance” link; it is called a dimensional chain (tolerance stack). The error in every link is passed on to the clearance.

Worst-case and statistical methods　The worked example in this chapter assigns a tolerance to each of the eight links (illustrative values, e.g. bore position ±0.03 mm, needle ±0.02 mm, hook-shaft radial play ±0.01 mm) and takes a nominal clearance of 0.07 mm. The most conservative calculation is the worst-case method: assume that every link deviates to its least favourable limit, so that the range of the clearance is the sum of all the link tolerances — ±0.153 mm in the example, far beyond the permitted ±0.03 mm. In reality, however, the chance of all links reaching their limits at once is negligible. The statistical method treats the error of each link as a random variable, normally distributed with its tolerance equal to ±3σ; the standard deviation of the clearance is then the root sum of squares of the link standard deviations:

\sigma_g=\sqrt{\sum_i\sigma_i^{2}},\qquad \sigma_i=\frac{T_i}{3}

In the worked example σ_g is about 0.019 mm. A Monte Carlo simulation of 4000 machines puts about 87% of the clearances within 0.04–0.10 mm (Fig. 23-4, left). In other words, if the parts are fully interchangeable and simply fitted and used, about 130 machines in every 1000 have an out-of-tolerance clearance: either the hook strikes the needle or it is prone to skipped stitches.

To raise the yield to 99% through part accuracy alone, tightening all links uniformly would mean tightening each to about 60% of its original tolerance. Higher accuracy costs more to machine: with the rough rule that halving a tolerance doubles its cost, the machining cost in the example rises by about two-thirds. Allocating tolerances according to how hard each link is to machine (a little more for difficult links, a little less for easy ones) is considerably cheaper, but still more expensive than the original, and every link still has to be made very accurately.

Adjustment during assembly　What industrial lockstitch machines actually do is adjust during assembly. The rotary hook can be moved axially; the assembler sets the clearance to 0.07 mm against the needle actually fitted, using a feeler gauge or measuring tool, and then tightens the screws (as Chapter 5 put it, “the clearance is adjusted by moving the hook along its axis”). This one adjustment absorbs the errors of the housing bore position, the needle-clamp hole, the hook-point dimension, the needle and so on; what remains is only the error of the adjustment itself (±0.01 mm in the example) and the needle-bar play and hook-shaft radial play, which adjustment cannot remove (a fixed runout of the hook shaft per revolution always has the same value at the moment of loop catching and is absorbed by the adjustment; what cannot be absorbed is the wobble caused by bearing play). The standard deviation of the clearance falls to about 0.005 mm and practically every machine passes, at the cost of only a little extra adjustment time per machine (Fig. 23-4, centre). More importantly, the links that are absorbed can be relaxed: in the example, relaxing all four absorbable links to ±0.05 mm still gives a yield close to 100%, and the total cost of machining plus adjustment actually ends up lower than before. Even if the machining error of some link is not normally distributed, or a batch is shifted, the adjustment absorbs it just the same. Absorbing the error of the whole dimensional chain with one adjustable link is called the adjustment method in mechanical design; it allows most parts to be machined to economical accuracy.

After a needle change　The adjustment is valid only for the needle that was fitted at the time. On the shop floor needles are consumables, replaced when they break or become blunt; a new needle’s scarf-face dimension and fitting twist differ from those of the old one, so the errors of these two links come back. In the worked example, the yield after a needle change drops from nearly 100% to about 98% (Fig. 23-4, right). To keep the clearance within tolerance after needle changes, one can either demand tighter accuracy of the needles themselves (the needle manufacturer’s tolerances), or, as Chapter 5 says, readjust the clearance after changing needle size. Dimensional-chain analysis tells us which links can be dealt with by adjustment and which will recur again and again in service; the latter are where accuracy really has to be controlled.

23.4 Assembly and adjustment

Assembly sequence　A lockstitch machine is assembled roughly from the inside out and from the bottom up: first the main and hook shafts with their bearings and the timing belt are fitted in the housing and bed, then the rotary hook and feed mechanism, then the needle bar, take-up mechanism and presser foot, and finally the face plate, controls and accessories. Every step has specified tightening torques and inspection items. Many relative positions between parts are secured by adjustment during assembly, so the assembly process documents spell out the order of the adjustments and their standard values.

Adjustment　An assembled machine must be adjusted item by item, mainly for the timings and clearances covered in Chapters 5, 6 and 8: needle-bar height, hook timing (the moment of loop catching), hook-point clearance, feed-dog height and feed timing, and the settings of the take-up and tension assembly. As Chapter 5 explained, the loop-catching moment, needle-bar height and clearance must be adjusted together in sequence; change one and another often has to follow. Electronically controlled models also need parameters such as the needle-up and needle-down stop positions and the trimming sequence to be set (Chapter 14) and written into the controller.

Final inspection and running-in　Before leaving the factory every machine is run without fabric and test-sewn: it runs at the specified speed for a period while temperature rise, abnormal noise and oil leaks are checked; then it sews the specified fabric and thread while stitches, stitch length, skipped stitches and thread breaks are checked. Inspection rules for sewing machines are set by dedicated industry standards — for example QB/T 4293-2012 (Sewing machines — Rules for product inspection) (National Digital Library of Standards (in Chinese)), which specifies the items and sampling methods for routine (factory) inspection and type testing. The surfaces of a new machine’s kinematic pairs still carry tiny asperities left by machining and wear relatively fast at first, so some models specify reduced speed for an initial period to allow running-in.

23.5 Vibration, noise and reliability tests

Vibration testing　Vibration is measured according to QB/T 1178-2006 (Industrial sewing machines — Test method for vibration) (standard information (in Chinese)): accelerometers are mounted at specified positions on the machine head and vibration is measured at specified speeds. Beyond the overall vibration level, the spectrum is more useful: breaking the vibration signal down by frequency shows how large the components at the main-shaft rotation frequency (first order), at twice that frequency (second order) and at higher orders are, which reveals where the vibration comes from (as Chapters 3 and 8 explained, the inertia force of the needle bar has first- and second-order parts, and the counterweight can balance only part of the first order).

Noise measurement　The manual quoted in Chapter 8 measures noise according to ISO 10821 and ISO 11204: a manufacturer’s standard lockstitch machine at 5000 sti/min has an A-weighted emission sound pressure level of 83.5 dB at the workstation and a sound power level of 88.0 dB. The sound pressure level is measured at the operator’s ear position and reflects what the worker actually hears; the sound power level is the acoustic energy radiated by the machine itself, independent of the measuring position and the room, and is used to compare different machines.

Modal testing and finite elements　Structures such as the housing, the arm and the table top each have their own natural frequencies and mode shapes. Striking the arm with an impact hammer and measuring the response, or calculating with finite-element software, gives their first few natural frequencies (Fig. 23-5). The design must keep the main excitation frequencies (first and second order) over the working speed range away from these natural frequencies; otherwise resonance occurs and vibration and noise are amplified many times over. The case study in the next section is exactly such a problem.

Reliability and life tests　The life of mechanical parts is limited mainly by wear and fatigue. Industry standards specify various performance test methods — for example QB/T 2628-2004 (Industrial sewing machines — Test method for multi-ply sewing) checks a machine’s ability to sew several layers of fabric. For life, test methods such as continuous sewing are used: prototypes run for long periods under specified conditions while part wear and failures are checked. Life data are commonly analysed with the Weibull distribution (Chapter 18 used it to describe early failures of the control box): for wear-type failures the shape parameter is greater than 1 and the failure rate rises with time. From the failure times of a batch of prototypes one can estimate the B10 life, the running time at which 10% of the machines have failed. The estimate needs enough failure data, and prototypes that have not yet failed must be treated as censored data; with few prototypes, the confidence interval of the estimate will be very wide. To shorten test time, accelerated tests can be run at higher speed and heavier load, but it must be confirmed that the failure modes under acceleration are the same as in normal use.

23.6 Case study: vibration and noise exceed limits after a speed increase to 5500 r/min

The technical problem　This is a generic case constructed from first principles; all figures are illustrative values. A lockstitch model originally had a maximum speed of 5000 r/min, and the market wanted it raised to 5500 r/min. The speed limit in the control was changed, but prototype tests revealed that at 5500 r/min the vibration of the machine head was more than three times that at 5000 r/min, the noise rose from 83.5 dB(A) to about 94 dB(A), and operators complained that the machine “screamed”. Yet by the method of Chapter 8, a 10% increase in speed raises the inertia forces by only about 21% (at a balance ratio of 0.45 the maximum resultant force rises from about 368 N to about 445 N), so the vibration should not have increased nearly so much.

Analysis　A speed sweep is carried out: the speed is raised slowly from 2000 r/min to 6000 r/min and the vibration is recorded at each speed (Fig. 23-6). Vibration rises sharply above 5000 r/min and peaks at about 5700 r/min. The spectrum shows that the growth is mainly in the second-order component: at 5500 r/min the second-order frequency is 183 Hz. A modal test on the arm then finds its first bending natural frequency near 190 Hz. The second-order excitation frequency is close to the natural frequency of the arm: resonance has occurred.

Treating the arm as a single-degree-of-freedom spring–mass–damper system, its response to an exciting force is the static deflection multiplied by an amplification factor:

X=\frac{F}{k}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-r^{2})^{2}+(2\zeta r)^{2}}},\qquad r=\frac{f}{f_n}

where r is the ratio of excitation frequency to natural frequency and ζ is the damping ratio. The worked example takes a modal stiffness of 1×10⁸ N/m and a damping ratio of 0.03. The exciting forces are taken as the components along the arm’s bending direction: at 5000 r/min, 120 N second order and 300 N first order (the residual after balancing), both proportional to the square of the speed (illustrative values, not calculated directly from the resultant forces of Chapter 8). At 5000 r/min the second-order frequency is 167 Hz, r = 0.88, the amplification is about 4.2 and the RMS vibration velocity of the machine head is about 4.0 mm/s; at 5500 r/min, r = 0.96, the amplification is about 11 and the vibration is about 13.3 mm/s. Estimating the noise roughly as rising 6 dB for every doubling of vibration velocity takes it from 83.5 dB(A) to about 94 dB(A). This estimate assumes that the noise comes mainly from vibration of the arm; in reality noise from the motor, the hook and the take-up also grows with speed, so only the trend is meaningful. The speed rose by just 10%, yet the amplification factor rose by one and a half times — that is what happens near resonance.

Improvements　There are three kinds of remedy, with very different effects (Fig. 23-7). The first is to change the structure and stiffen the arm: adding ribs inside the arm and thickening its walls raises the modal stiffness by 30% and the natural frequency to about 217 Hz (ribs also add mass, so this is an upper bound). At 5500 r/min r falls to 0.85, the vibration to about 3.4 mm/s and the estimated noise to about 82 dB(A), comparable to the original machine at 5000 r/min. The price is a modified casting pattern and slightly more weight, and the resonance speed moves to about 6500 r/min, where any future speed increase will run into it again. The second is to add damping, for example damping material inside the arm cavity: raising the damping ratio from 0.03 to 0.06 gives a vibration of about 9.0 mm/s, and only at 0.10 does it fall to about 6.1 mm/s; damping is effective only near resonance and has to be very large to be enough. The third is to reduce the exciting force at its source: a hollow needle bar and a lightweight take-up lever reduce the reciprocating mass; a 20% reduction gives a vibration of about 10.6 mm/s, and only a 40% reduction brings it down to about 8.0 mm/s, so weight reduction alone can hardly do the job. For this problem the most effective remedy is therefore to change the structure and move the natural frequency out of the working speed range; weight reduction and added damping can serve as supporting measures.

After the changes, the speed sweep, noise measurement and life tests must be repeated on new prototypes to confirm that the problem is solved and no new problems have been introduced; only then are the changes written into the drawings and process documents. Finding the cause from measured data, changing the design and verifying again is the basic cycle of product improvement.

23.7 Standards and intellectual property

Standards　The standards a sewing machine must meet fall roughly into several groups. Safety standards: ISO 10821 (safety requirements for industrial sewing machines) and IEC 60204-31 / GB/T 5226.31 (electrical safety and EMC, Chapter 18). Product standards: the industry standards for each type of sewing machine specify performance parameters and technical requirements. Test-method standards: how vibration, noise, continuous sewing, multi-ply sewing and so on are measured and tested. Inspection rules: which items routine (factory) inspection and type testing cover and how samples are taken. At the start of design, the standards required by the target market should be listed and turned into design inputs, rather than “sending the machine for testing” only after it is finished.

Intellectual property　As Chapter 1 explained, the history of sewing-machine invention is also a history of patents. Designing a new machine today likewise involves three tasks. The first is searching: before the design concept is fixed, search the relevant patents to confirm that your own concept does not fall within the scope of protection of someone else’s patent in force. Patents are territorial, so the search must cover the countries and regions where the product will be sold, and must also include applications that have been published but not yet granted. The second is design-around: if a structure is protected by someone else’s patent, you can achieve the same function with a different principle or structure, wait for the patent to expire, or negotiate a licence. The third is protecting yourself: file patents promptly for innovative structures and methods. In China, structural improvements to sewing machines are often protected by utility-model patents, and appearance can be protected by design patents. The thread-trimming devices and the thread-break re-embroidery method cited in Chapters 14 and 16 of this book are technical principles read from the patent literature; because the patent literature discloses technical solutions, it is itself good study material for engineers.

23.8 Limits of the model

The dimensional-chain model in this chapter treats the errors of the eight links as mutually independent and normally distributed. Real machining errors are often not normal (for example, the drift caused by tool wear), and the links may also be correlated; the clearance also varies with temperature, wear and the deflection of the needle in the fabric. The cost model “halving the tolerance doubles the cost” is only a rough empirical relation. The vibration model treats the arm as a single-degree-of-freedom system and considers only one natural frequency and two exciting forces, first and second order; a real machine has several modes and several sources of excitation (hook, take-up lever, feed, timing belt), as well as the influence of the table top and stand. The relation between noise and vibration velocity is likewise only an estimate. The part materials and hardnesses are illustrative of common practice. All of these need to be calibrated and enriched with measured industry data.

Shop-floor notes

  • Check the hook-point clearance after changing the needle or the needle size; the adjustment is valid only for the needle fitted at the time.
  • Run a new machine in at reduced speed as the manual specifies; after the running-in period, check all clearances and timings again.
  • If the machine suddenly “screams” at a certain speed and is fine again above it, it is most likely resonance; note that speed, and either avoid it or solve it structurally.
  • When replacing precision parts such as rotary hooks and needle bars during repair, use original-specification parts; spare parts of insufficient accuracy can throw the whole dimensional chain out of control.
  • List the target market’s standards and the relevant patents when a new product project starts; do not wait until type testing or market launch to discover problems.
