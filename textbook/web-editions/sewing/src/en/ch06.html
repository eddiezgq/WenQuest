The needle penetration, thread take-up and loop catching of the previous three chapters all serve one purpose: to “tie a knot” in the fabric. Once a knot is tied, the fabric has to move forward a little before the next one can be made. That is the job of the feed mechanism: the toothed feed dog under the throat plate rises through the slots of the plate on every stitch, grips the fabric and pushes it forward, then drops and returns to its starting point. The motion looks simple, but the requirements are strict: the fabric may move only while the needle is out of it, and must stop as soon as the needle penetrates; every stitch must advance the same distance; and the upper and lower plies must advance by the same amount. This chapter first sets out the tasks and structure of the feed mechanism, then uses a worked-example machine to calculate the feed-dog trajectory and the feed timing. It goes on to stitch-length adjustment, feed-dog height and the presser foot, and ends with the inherent weakness of drop feed — the upper and lower plies do not travel equally — and how needle feed, compound feed and other feed systems overcome it.

6.1 Opening: three faults that come from the feed

There are three faults for which a mechanic on the shop floor looks at the feed first. The first is uneven stitch length: at the same dial setting some stitches come out long and some short, especially where thick and thin sections alternate. The second is the upper ply growing longer when two plies are sewn together: two cut pieces of equal length are sewn, and at the end the upper one sticks out and has to be trimmed or eased in as a pleat; this is most obvious on linings, silk and knits. The third is needle breakage on heavy material: sewing the waistband or belt loops of jeans, the needle is dragged sideways by the fabric, strikes the rotary hook and snaps.

The three faults have different causes, but they all come down to two questions: when does the fabric move, and by how much; and do the upper and lower plies move together? Let us start with what the feed mechanism has to accomplish.

6.2 The tasks of the feed mechanism

As Chapter 1 explained, Wilson patented the four-motion feed in 1854: the feed dog first rises to grip the fabric, then advances carrying the fabric forward, then drops below the throat plate, and finally returns to its starting point. Lockstitch machines today still use these four motions. Of the four, “rise” and “drop” decide when the fabric is gripped and when it is released; “advance” and “return” decide how far it is pushed. Only the part of the advance that happens while the dog is above the throat plate actually moves the fabric; the return takes place below the plate and does not touch the fabric, so the fabric is not carried back.

The fabric is held between three things: above it the presser foot, pressing down under spring force; below it the throat plate, which supports it; and the feed dog, whose teeth rise through the slots in the plate and dig into the fabric. As the dog advances, friction between the teeth and the fabric carries the fabric along, while the presser foot and throat plate keep the fabric from wandering. How well the fabric feeds therefore depends not only on how the feed dog moves, but also on feed-dog height, presser-foot pressure and the fabric itself.

In time, the feed is constrained by needle penetration. In the worked example of Chapter 3, with a fabric thickness of 1.5 mm the needle point enters the fabric at 101.7° and leaves it at 258.3°, so the needle is in the fabric for 156.6° of main-shaft rotation. The remaining two hundred degrees or so is all the time available for feeding. If the fabric is pushed while the needle is still in it, the needle is bent: at best the seam runs crooked and the stitch length is uneven; at worst the needle hits the hook point and breaks. This is the first design constraint on the feed mechanism.

6.3 Structure: two drive trains combined into one motion

The feed dog has to move both horizontally and vertically at the same time. In the worked-example machine these two motions come from two separate drive trains (Fig. 6-1), consistent with Section 2.1 of Chapter 2.

The horizontal motion comes from the main shaft. Mounted on the main shaft is the feed eccentric, which drives the feed connecting rod up and down; the lower end of the rod is attached to the stitch regulator. The stitch regulator is a guide link that can be turned, and the lower end of the connecting rod can only slide along it. The tilt of the guide is set by the stitch-length dial, and the reverse lever can push it over to the other side. The motion of the rod’s lower end is passed through a drag link to the feed rock shaft, which rocks back and forth and moves the feed bar forward and backward.

The vertical motion comes from the hook shaft. The lift eccentric on the hook shaft rocks the lift rock shaft, whose fork arm carries one end of the feed bar up and down. The feed dog is screwed to the feed bar, so the forward-and-back motion and the up-and-down motion of the feed bar add together to give the motion of the feed dog.

This division of labour has an advantage: the stitch length changes only the amplitude of the horizontal motion and does not affect when the dog rises or drops; the feed-dog height changes only the position of the vertical motion and does not affect the stitch length. Below we write the two motions separately and then combine them.

6.4 The feed-dog trajectory: two harmonic motions combined

A linkage driven by an eccentric produces an output motion very close to simple harmonic motion. Taking the main-shaft angle φ as the independent variable, let X be the horizontal position of the feed-dog tooth tip (positive in the feed direction) and Y its vertical position (zero at the throat-plate surface, positive upward). Approximately,

\[X(\varphi) = \frac{S}{2}\cos(\varphi-\varphi_F),\qquad Y(\varphi) = (h-A) + A\cos(\varphi-\varphi_L)\]

where S is the horizontal stroke of the dog and φ_F the angle at which the dog reaches its foremost position; A is the lift amplitude (total rise and fall 2A), h is the height of the tooth tips above the throat plate at the top of the stroke, called the feed-dog height for short, and φ_L is the angle at which the dog reaches its highest point.

The dog can grip the fabric only when Y > 0, i.e. when the tooth tips stand above the throat plate. Setting Y = 0 gives the half-width of the angular interval during which the dog is above the plate:

\[\alpha = \arccos\frac{A-h}{A}\]

The dog emerges above the throat plate at φ_L − α and drops below it at φ_L + α, so the gripping interval is 2α in total.

Worked example Take a feed-dog height h = 0.8 mm (a manufacturer’s instruction manual gives 0.75–0.85 mm for the standard model; a manufacturer’s instruction manual) and a lift amplitude A = 1.1 mm (total rise and fall 2.2 mm, a worked-example value). Then α = arccos(0.3/1.1) = 74.2° and the gripping interval is 148.3°. Placing the highest point at φ_L = 40.0°, the dog emerges above the plate at 325.8° and drops at 114.2° (how these two angles are chosen is explained in the next section). The foremost point of the horizontal motion is placed at the same instant as the drop: φ_F = 114.2°.

Fig. 6-2 plots the two components and the trajectory they combine into.

There are three things to take from Fig. 6-2.

First, the combined trajectory is a tilted ellipse. If the highest point fell exactly at the middle of the horizontal stroke (the two components 90° apart), the ellipse would be upright; in the worked example the highest point comes 74° before the foremost point, so the ellipse tilts up to the right. The tooth tip travels round the ellipse: above the throat plate it moves from left to right, pushing the fabric forward; below the plate it returns from right to left.

Second, the distance the fabric travels is not equal to the horizontal stroke of the dog. The fabric follows the dog only while the dog is above the throat plate, so the feed per stitch is the difference between the dog’s horizontal positions at the instants it emerges and drops:

\[s = X(\varphi_L+\alpha) - X(\varphi_L-\alpha)\]

In the worked example the foremost point coincides with the drop, and substituting gives s = (S/2)[1 − cos 2α] = 0.926 S. In other words, to sew a 3 mm stitch the dog needs a horizontal stroke of 3.24 mm. The dog has already moved forward a little before it emerges; that part happens below the throat plate and is “wasted”. The stitch-length scale indicates the distance the fabric actually travels, not the stroke of the dog.

Third, if the phase difference is wrong, the trajectory is distorted. The two dashed curves in the figure show the horizontal motion alone advanced or retarded by 30°. When it is advanced (red), the dog reaches its foremost point at 84° but does not drop until 114°; during those thirty degrees the dog is already moving back and carries the fabric back by 0.22 mm, so the actual stitch length is only 2.17 mm. This is called back-drag, and moreover this backward motion happens just around the moment the needle penetrates the fabric. When it is retarded (purple), the dog is still moving forward when it reaches the plate surface, so the fabric movement extends past the moment the needle enters the fabric. The first case shortens the stitch and makes it uneven; the second pushes the fabric while the needle is in it. Both are avoided by setting the mounting angle of the eccentrics correctly.

6.5 Feed timing: when does the fabric move?

Plotting the feed-dog motion and the needle motion of Chapter 3 on the same angle axis gives the feed timing diagram (Fig. 6-3). The upper half shows heights: the black line is the needle point, the dashed blue line the top of the needle eye (2 mm above the point in the worked example, as in Chapter 4), and the amber line the feed-dog tooth tip; the lower half shows the horizontal position of the dog and the position of the fabric.

First look at how the two intervals relate. The grey interval is the needle in the fabric (101.7°–258.3°); the green interval is the dog gripping the fabric (325.8°–114.2°, crossing 0°). They overlap briefly between 101.7° and 114.2°: the needle point has already entered the fabric but the dog has not yet fully dropped. By then, however, the horizontal motion of the dog is close to its foremost point and is hardly advancing any more. In the lower half of the figure, the fabric position curve has already covered 99% of its travel at 101.7° and moves only about 0.04 mm more after that. So with this timing the fabric essentially completes its movement after the needle leaves and before it re-enters.

This timing is not arbitrary. A manufacturer’s instruction manual specifies the standard feed timing as follows: when the feed dog descends below the throat plate, the top surface of the dog and the top of the needle eye should be flush with the top surface of the throat plate at the same moment (a manufacturer’s instruction manual). In the worked example the top of the eye reaches the plate surface at 114.2° and the dog drops at 114.2° — exactly this rule. The rule is practical because no angle needs to be measured: just turn the handwheel by hand and check that the dog face and the needle eye sink below the plate together.

Now look at a few instants. When the dog emerges at 325.8°, the needle left the fabric long before, at 258.3°; there is a margin of more than sixty degrees, so the rising side is not critical — the dropping side is. The take-up lever pulls the thread tightest at 55° (Chapter 4), when the fabric has travelled only about three-quarters of its distance; in other words, the stitch is tightened while the fabric is still moving. Loop catching occurs at 206°, when the dog is below the plate, the fabric is at rest and the needle is in the fabric. The feed interval (290°–60°) in Fig. 2-9 of Chapter 2 is a schematic; the interval calculated here from the manual’s standard timing is 325.8°–114.2°: feeding begins well after the needle leaves the fabric and continues until just after the needle has entered it.

Advancing and retarding the timing The manual says: to prevent uneven feeding, the feed timing may be advanced; to tighten the stitches, the feed timing may be retarded. The manual’s procedure is to loosen the screws of a feed eccentric and turn it through some angle on the shaft; which eccentric is turned, and which component it drives, depends on the model. As a simplification, this chapter shifts the horizontal and lift motions together and calls this the “feed timing”; shifting only the horizontal motion is the phase difference of the previous section. Fig. 6-3 makes the first statement clear: advancing the feed timing by 10° brings the drop forward to 104.2° and the fabric movement while the needle is in it falls almost to zero; retarding by 10° delays the drop to 124.2° and the fabric is pushed 0.12 mm while the needle is in it; retarding by 20° gives 0.25 mm. Fabric being pushed while the needle is in it is the source of “uneven feeding” and of needle breakage. The second statement concerns how stitch tightening and feeding fit together: with the feed retarded, the fabric has not moved very far when the take-up pulls in the thread, so more of the feed happens after the stitch has been tightened. This is only one possible explanation; how this coordination affects stitch tightness is not included in this chapter’s model and has to be judged by test sewing.

Heavy material The thicker the fabric, the earlier the needle point meets its surface. In the worked example, four plies of denim are about 4 mm thick and the needle point enters the fabric surface at 92.7°, 9° earlier than for thin fabric. With standard timing, the fabric is pushed about 0.11 mm while the needle is in it. Advancing the feed timing (horizontal and lift together) by about 8° brings this below 0.05 mm; if only the horizontal motion is advanced by the same 8°, about 0.06 mm remains and a little back-drag appears. This is why machines for heavy material are often set with slightly earlier feed timing.

6.6 Stitch-length adjustment and reverse stitching

The stitch regulator has two jobs: to vary the stitch length continuously without stopping the machine, and to make the fabric travel backward, i.e. reverse stitching (backtack). Fig. 6-4 shows the principle.

The lower end C of the feed connecting rod is moved up and down by the feed eccentric, with a stroke of about 2e (e being the eccentricity). C is also constrained to a guide link and can only slide along it. When the guide is vertical (θ = 0), C moves only up and down, with no horizontal component; the motion passed to the feed rock shaft is zero and so is the stitch length. When the guide is tilted by θ, each up-and-down stroke of C also moves it sideways by 2e·tanθ; this passes through the feed rock shaft (ratio i) to the feed bar, and the horizontal stroke of the dog is

\[S = 2e\,i\tan\theta,\qquad s = 0.926\,S\]

When the guide tips to the other side (θ < 0), the horizontal motion of C reverses: the dog moves backward while it is above the throat plate, and the fabric is fed backward. The reverse lever is the part that pushes the guide across to the other side of the vertical; when the lever is released, a spring pulls the guide back to the position set by the dial. In this principle model the forward and reverse directions are symmetric and the reverse stitch length equals the forward one; the linkage geometry of a real machine is not perfectly symmetric, so forward and reverse stitch lengths differ slightly, which is why some models provide a fine adjustment for the reverse stitch length.

In the worked example, e = 3 mm and i = 1, so s = 5.55 tanθ. A 3 mm stitch needs θ = 28.4° and a 5 mm stitch θ = 42.0°. Because tanθ grows ever steeper, each additional 1 mm of stitch length needs successively 10.2°, 9.6°, 8.6°, 7.4° and 6.2° of guide rotation, so the stitch-length scale becomes more crowded towards the long-stitch end. On real machines a cam or lever between the dial and the guide can correct the scale to make it more uniform.

In the same instruction manual, the maximum stitch length is 5 mm for the standard and medium-heavy types, 4 mm for the light-material type and 7 mm for the heavy-material type. The longer the stitch, the larger the horizontal stroke of the dog and the longer the slots in the throat plate must be.

Feed-dog speed At a stitch length of 3 mm and a main-shaft speed of 5000 r/min, the horizontal stroke of the dog is 3.24 mm, the maximum horizontal velocity is (S/2)ω = 1.62 mm × 523.6 rad/s ≈ 0.85 m/s, and the maximum horizontal acceleration is (S/2)ω² ≈ 444 m/s². That is more than forty times the acceleration of gravity — no small number; fortunately the small patch of fabric under the presser foot is very light, and the friction force needed is not large. The real limit is a slippery fabric combined with insufficient presser-foot pressure: the fabric cannot keep up with the dog. This is the slipping discussed in the next section.

6.7 Feed-dog height, feed-dog tilt and presser-foot pressure

Feed-dog height The feed-dog height determines how deep the teeth bite into the fabric. The manual gives feed-dog heights by model: standard type 0.75–0.85 mm, light-material type 0.7–0.8 mm, medium-heavy type 0.95–1.05 mm, heavy-material type 1.0–1.1 mm. Light materials use a low dog so that the teeth do not leave marks on the fabric; heavy materials use a high dog so that it can grip.

Changing the feed-dog height has another consequence that is easily overlooked: the gripping interval changes with it. From α = arccos((A − h)/A), raising the dog from 0.8 mm to 1.0 mm changes the gripping interval from 148.3° to 169.6°; the dog emerges 10.6° earlier and drops 10.6° later. Because the drop is later, after reaching its foremost point at 114.2° the dog stays above the plate another 10.6°, during which it has already started to move back: the fabric is pushed more while the needle is in it, and there is a little back-drag (about 0.03 mm). Because it emerges earlier, the dog covers more of its forward stroke above the plate, and the actual stitch length goes from 3.00 mm to 3.11 mm. So after adjusting the feed-dog height, recheck the feed timing and the stitch length; checked against the manual’s standard timing, the dog face and the top of the needle eye are no longer flush at the same moment.

Feed-dog tilt The manual takes a level dog face as standard and gives two tilts: raising the front of the dog prevents puckering; lowering the front of the dog prevents uneven feeding. Tilting changes which part of the dog face meets the fabric first and which part leaves it last — in effect redistributing the gripping force between the front and rear of the dog. This chapter’s model treats the dog face as level and does not include the effect of tilt.

Presser-foot pressure Presser-foot pressure is the other half of the condition for the dog to grip the fabric. Too little pressure, and the friction between the teeth and the fabric is not enough to drag the fabric: the fabric lags the dog and the stitches become short and irregular — this is called slipping. Too much pressure leaves marks on the fabric, and, as the next section shows, the higher the pressure, the more the presser foot holds back the upper ply. The presser-foot lift is also specified: in the same manual, the standard lift with the knee lifter is 10 mm, adjustable up to 13 mm (9 mm for the light-material type), which determines how thick a fabric can be put under the foot.

6.8 Ply shift and the various feed systems

Why the upper ply grows longer A drop-feed mechanism has only one dog, and it is below the fabric. The dog pushes the lower ply directly; the upper ply must be carried along by friction between the two plies, while its top surface is pressed and dragged by the presser foot. The upper ply therefore experiences a backward friction force under the foot, is compressed and sheared, and with every stitch travels slightly less than the lower ply. If it travels 1% less per stitch, then after 40 cm of seam the difference is 4 mm — this is why “the upper ply grows longer”. The effect is more pronounced with slippery fabrics, two different fabrics, and stretch fabrics.

This chapter describes it with an illustrative model. Let μ_f be the coefficient of friction between the presser foot and the upper ply, P the presser-foot pressure (relative value, standard pressure = 1), and K the fabric’s resistance to this deformation. The fraction by which the upper ply falls short per stitch is then

\[\rho = \frac{\mu_f P}{K}\]

At the same time, for the dog to drag the fabric, the gripping force of the teeth must exceed the sum of the presser-foot friction and the fabric’s own resistance. Expressing the fabric’s own resistance as a relative value F₀ on the same scale, the pressure must be no less than

\[P_{\min} = \frac{F_0}{\mu_d-\mu_f}\]

where μ_d is the equivalent coefficient of friction between the teeth and the fabric, which decreases when the feed-dog height is insufficient. These two expressions capture the dilemma of drop feed: too little pressure and it slips, too much and the plies shift. All parameters here are illustrative values, meant for understanding trends, not for predicting specific figures for a particular fabric.

Fig. 6-5 shows the case of two plies of slippery lining. The minimum pressure at which drop feed does not slip is 0.8, and at that pressure the upper ply already gains 4.8 mm over 40 cm, exceeding the 3 mm allowance; higher pressures make the shift larger still, and above 1.4 pressure marks appear as well. Here drop feed has no usable range. The situation for cotton is much better: about 2 mm of shift at standard pressure.

The various feed systems There are two kinds of remedy for ply shift: “pin” the two plies together so that they travel as one, or give the upper ply an active feed of its own. Fig. 6-6 brings the common feed systems together.

Differential feed has one dog in front of the needle and one behind; the two dogs can have different strokes, to push the fabric together or stretch it apart. It is discussed in detail in Section 9.10 of Chapter 9. It deals with a single ply being stretched or puckered, and does little for shift between plies.

Needle feed makes the needle take part in feeding as well: the needle bar is linked to the feed rock shaft, and once the needle has penetrated the fabric it swings forward with the dog. The needle passes through both plies and “pins” them together, so the upper ply cannot lag far behind. Note that its timing is exactly the opposite of drop feed: drop feed requires the fabric to move only after the needle has left it, whereas needle feed feeds only after the needle has penetrated, with the highest point of the dog placed near the lowest point of the needle. In the worked example, placing the dog’s highest point at 180° gives a gripping interval of 105.8°–254.2°, which falls just inside the interval during which the needle is in the fabric (101.7°–258.3° for a 1.5 mm fabric; the thinner the fabric, the later the needle enters and the earlier it leaves, and the smaller the margin). Note also that at 206°, when the loop is caught, the needle is swinging with the dog, so the relative position of hook and needle changes; the hook mechanism of a needle-feed machine has to be designed specially for this. According to this chapter’s illustrative model, needle feed reduces ply shift to about 30% of that with drop feed.

Compound feed (also called unison feed) generally combines dog feed, needle feed and a pair of alternately lifting presser feet. In the common arrangement an inner foot surrounding the needle moves forward on top of the fabric in step with the dog and needle, while an outer foot holds the fabric down as the inner foot lifts and returns. In this way the upper ply is carried from above, the lower ply from below and both plies from the middle (by the needle), all at once. Ply shift falls to about 10% of that with drop feed, and because the foot moves with the fabric instead of holding back the upper ply, slipping is also reduced. One manufacturer’s compound-feed heavy-duty machine has an alternating lift adjustable between 2.0 and 6.0 mm, a maximum stitch length of 9 mm, and a separately adjustable top-feed amount (a manufacturer’s compound-feed machine instruction manual). Thick, stiff materials such as leather, bags and luggage, car seats and canvas are mostly sewn on machines of this kind.

Another class adds a separate active feed above the fabric — a dog, roller or feed belt — so that the top and bottom feed amounts can be set independently. The sleeve-setting machine described in Chapter 29 works this way: two feed mechanisms, top and bottom, each push one ply and, following a program, ease the extra length of the sleeve cap evenly into the armhole. Finally, electronic feed drives the horizontal motion of the dog directly with a stepper or servo motor, so that stitch length, reverse stitching and stitch-length changes in mid-seam are all set by the program; this is covered in Chapter 14.

6.9 Limits of the model

This chapter approximates the feed-dog motion as harmonic; the real eccentric–connecting-rod–rock-shaft mechanism introduces some higher harmonics, and the trajectory is not a strict ellipse. The tanθ relation of the stitch regulator is likewise only the principle; the real linkage geometry makes forward and reverse stitch lengths differ slightly. The fabric is assumed to follow the dog perfectly, with no account taken of the teeth biting into the fabric, or of the fabric’s elastic stretch and inertia. The models of ply shift and slipping are even more illustrative: the friction coefficients and fabric parameters were chosen to make the dilemma clear, they vary widely between fabrics, and in practice can only be determined by test sewing. Mechanism dimensions (lift amplitude, eccentricity, etc.) are worked-example values; the standard timing, feed-dog heights, maximum stitch lengths and so on come from manufacturers’ instruction manuals.

Shop-floor notes

  • To check the feed timing, turn the handwheel by hand and see whether, as the feed dog drops below the throat plate, the dog face and the top of the needle eye become flush with the plate at the same moment.
  • When changing to heavy material, consider three things together: feed-dog height, presser-foot pressure and feed timing. After raising the dog, recheck both timing and stitch length.
  • If the upper ply grows longer, first try reducing the presser-foot pressure; if that does not work, consider needle feed, compound feed or top feed — do not rely on the operator pulling the fabric by hand to compensate.
  • If light or slippery fabrics show tooth marks or pressure marks, first lower the feed dog and reduce the pressure, or change to a fine-toothed feed dog and a smooth presser foot.
