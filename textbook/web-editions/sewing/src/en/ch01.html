This chapter is the way into the book. We first go back two hundred years to see what problems people ran into when they tried to teach a machine to make “one stitch”, and which few key ideas solved them. We then follow the timeline forward to see how the industrial sewing machine became step by step faster, electronically controlled, automatic and intelligent. Finally we look at where sewing equipment sits in the apparel industry as a whole, and how this book intends to explain it. There are no complicated derivations in this chapter, but the theme of every later part can be traced back to something here.

1.1 Opening: why a machine cannot copy a hand stitch

Sewing two pieces of cloth together with a hand needle is a simple action: the point goes into the top of the fabric, the needle is pulled out underneath, then pushed back up from below, and so on. But think about it and there is something a machine finds very hard to imitate. The eye of a hand needle is at its tail, and the thread runs through the tail, so with every stitch the whole needle, together with all the thread trailing behind it, has to pass completely from one side of the fabric to the other. Two human hands can take turns catching the needle on either side of the cloth; a machine has no way of “catching” a needle under the fabric and then pulling it through along with a long length of thread.

Inventors two hundred years ago tried many ways around this, and every route that succeeded avoided that motion altogether: the needle no longer passes right through the fabric; it goes in only part way and comes back out. The eye moved accordingly from the tail to near the point. As the needle goes down it carries the thread to the underside of the fabric and leaves a loop there, and some means is then found under the fabric to “lock” that loop. How it is locked determines the type of stitch: pulling one loop through the next with a hook gives a chainstitch; passing a second thread through the loop gives a lockstitch. Animation 1-1 shows hand sewing, single-thread chainstitch and two-thread lockstitch side by side; you can step through it, and you can pull the thread end to see which one unravels.

1.2 The age of invention: five key ideas

Between 1830 and the 1850s the basic form of the sewing machine took shape in about thirty years. Looked at as lines of technical thinking, the period can be summed up in five key ideas, each of which solved a specific problem of the previous generation of machines (upper half of Fig. 1-1; Fig. 1-2).

Idea 1: a hooked needle making a chainstitch　The French tailor Barthélemy Thimonnier obtained a French patent for a sewing machine in 1830. It drove a hooked needle through the fabric, hooked the thread up from underneath, and passed each loop through the next to form a single-thread chainstitch (Encyclopaedia Britannica, Musée des Arts et Métiers, Paris). According to The Invention of the Sewing Machine, published by the Smithsonian Institution, he later improved the machine to 200 stitches per minute and used several dozen of them in a Paris workshop to sew army uniforms, but the workshop was wrecked by tailors who feared for their jobs (Cooper, The Invention of the Sewing Machine; sources disagree on whether this happened in 1831 or 1841). The weakness of the chainstitch shows up in Animation 1-1 with a single pull: it has only one thread, the last loop is not secured, and if you pull the thread end the whole seam unravels loop after loop.

Idea 2: an eye at the point, plus a second thread　Around 1833–1834 the American inventor Walter Hunt built a sewing machine with an eye-pointed needle and a shuttle, producing for the first time a lockstitch in which two threads interlock; but he did not apply for a patent (Smithsonian National Museum of American History, Lemelson Center, MIT). On 10 September 1846 Elias Howe was granted US Patent No. 4,750: a curved, grooved needle with its eye near the point, mounted on a swinging arm, pierced the fabric horizontally, and the needle-thread loop was locked by a second thread carried by a shuttle (Library of Congress, Smithsonian collections). From then on, “eye-pointed needle + second thread” became the basic form of the great majority of sewing machines; this is the origin of the 301 lockstitch covered in Chapter 2.

Howe’s machine had an obvious shortcoming: the fabric did not lie flat but hung vertically on a metal plate fitted with pins, and the plate was moved intermittently by a gear. When the plate reached the end of its travel the machine had to stop so the fabric could be taken off and rehung, so it could only sew straight, short seams (same Smithsonian collection note as above).

Idea 3: a straight needle moving up and down, with the fabric lying flat and fed continuously　On 12 August 1851 Isaac Singer was granted US Patent No. 8,294 (Smithsonian collections). His machine replaced the horizontal curved needle with a straight needle moving up and down; the needle bar was carried in an overhanging arm reaching out above the table. The fabric lay flat on the table, held down by a spring-loaded presser, and a wheel with a friction surface underneath fed it forward continuously (original patent). The fabric was no longer limited by the length of a plate: long seams were possible, and curves could be sewn by turning the work while sewing. The machine was driven by a treadle, leaving both of the operator’s hands free to guide the fabric (Encyclopaedia Britannica). Today’s industrial lockstitch machine still has this “overhanging arm + straight needle + presser foot + table” layout (Fig. 2-1 in Chapter 2).

Idea 4: a continuously rotating hook, and a feed dog with four motions　Allen B. Wilson’s patents of 1851 and 1852 used a continuously rotating hook instead of a reciprocating shuttle to catch the loop; in the 1852 patent the bobbin holding the under thread is already held axially stationary, and only the hook rotates around it (US 8,296, US 9,041). A reciprocating shuttle has to accelerate, decelerate and reverse on every stitch, so at higher speed it causes impact and heat; a continuously rotating hook has no reversal shock, which is exactly the principle of the later high-speed rotary hook (Chapter 5). On 19 December 1854 Wilson was also granted Patent No. 12,116 for four-motion feed: the feed dog first rises to press against the fabric, then moves forward carrying the fabric with it, then drops below the throat plate, and finally returns to its starting position (original patent). The Invention of the Sewing Machine remarks that this feed is still used, with only small changes, on almost every sewing machine today. The feed mechanisms of Chapter 6 are modern versions of these four motions.

Idea 5: a single-thread chainstitch can be made reliable too　In 1857 James Gibbs was granted US Patent No. 17,427 for a single-thread chainstitch machine using a rotating hook, simple in construction and capable of high speed (original patent). Although a chainstitch unravels easily, it is elastic, needs no bobbin thread and can sew continuously for very long lengths; it later developed into the multi-thread chainstitches of chainstitch, overlock and coverstitch machines (Chapters 9 and 10).

These inventions came from different people and their patents overlapped, so anyone who built a machine risked infringing someone else’s patent, and lawsuits went on endlessly — the episode is known as the “Sewing Machine War”. In 1856 Howe, Singer and two manufacturers combined nine patents into a “patent pool” licensed jointly, which greatly reduced the licence fee per machine. It is regarded as the first patent pool in American history and operated until the last of its patents expired in 1877 (Smithsonian Magazine, Mossoff, Arizona Law Review). Once the patent situation was sorted out, sewing machines went into mass production and into homes and factories. This history means something to engineers too: a machine is usually not one person’s invention but a combination of several key mechanisms, and Chapter 23 returns to intellectual property when it discusses design.

1.3 Five stages of the industrial sewing machine

After its mechanisms settled, the industrial sewing machine went on developing for more than a hundred years. By the main problem each period set out to solve, this can be divided into five stages: mechanisation, high speed, electronic control, automation and intelligence (lower half of Fig. 1-1). The five stages did not replace one another in turn but were added layer upon layer: inside a smart lockstitch machine today are still the needle-bar, take-up, loop-catching and feed mechanisms that took shape in the 19th century.

Mechanisation: getting the mechanisms right　From the second half of the 19th century to the first half of the 20th, the main work was making the mechanisms reliable, durable and suitable for volume production: cast-iron housings, hardened needle bars and bobbin-case holders, interchangeable parts. Power changed from the treadle to the electric motor; a practical sewing-machine motor already existed by 1884 (ISMACS sewing-machine history). The mechanism principles established in this stage are the content of Parts I and II of this book (Chapters 2–8).

High speed: making the mechanisms fast　Factories wanted output, so speeds had to rise. But as speed increases, the inertia forces of the reciprocating parts grow with the square of the speed, and vibration, noise, wear and heat all rise with them. The main work of the high-speed stage was balancing inertia forces (Chapter 3), replacing reciprocating motion with rotary motion (rotary hooks, rotary take-ups), and enclosed automatic lubrication (Chapter 8). The maximum speed of a modern high-speed lockstitch machine is generally around 5000 stitches per minute.

Worked example: how much does the needle bar’s inertia force grow when speed rises?　Using the worked-example data of Chapter 3: reciprocating mass m = 120 g, crank radius r = 15.5 mm, connecting-rod length l = 55 mm. At 5000 r/min the acceleration at bottom dead centre is about 5.45 km/s² and the inertia force about 0.12 × 5450 ≈ 654 N. Acceleration is proportional to the square of speed, so at the 200 stitches per minute of Thimonnier’s machine the acceleration is

\[a_{200} = 5450 \times \left(\frac{200}{5000}\right)^2 \approx 8.7\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\]

The inertia force is only about 1 N — a factor of 625 smaller. In the days of hand cranks and treadles it was enough for a mechanism simply to move; at several thousand stitches per minute the same needle bar takes more than eighty impacts per second, each of several hundred newtons, and balance, materials and lubrication become the core of the design. This is why Chapter 3 spends a whole section on inertia forces and balancing.

Electronic control: letting electronics take over timing and actions　In the past, thread trimming, reverse stitching and presser-foot lift were done by hand or with pedal-operated linkages; with electronic control they are performed automatically by servo motors, solenoids and pneumatic cylinders when the main shaft reaches a certain angle. Taking one sewing-machine maker’s published product chronology as an example, it introduced a lockstitch machine with an automatic thread trimmer in 1969, and in 1999 a direct-drive lockstitch machine with automatic thread trimming, with the motor mounted directly on the main shaft (company technology chronology; this is the company’s record of its own products and does not imply an industry first). The core idea of electronic control is to turn the mechanical timing that Chapter 2 secures with cams and links into electronic timing: “an encoder reads the main-shaft angle and software issues commands according to angle”. This is the main thread of Part V (Chapters 13–19).

Automation: letting the machine follow patterns and load and unload by itself　With electronic timing, the fabric can be carried by an X–Y table along a programmed path. This produced programmable pattern sewers, template machines, and special machines that work to fixed patterns such as buttonholers, button sewers and bartackers (Chapters 11 and 15). One step further, combining loading, positioning, sewing and stacking gives automatic sewing units such as automatic pocket-welting and pocket-setting machines, and a hanger system then links the individual work centres into a single-piece-flow line (Chapter 12; the made-to-measure suit factory of Chapter 29 is an example).

Intelligence: letting machines connect, see and learn　In the last decade or so, sewing machines have been networked: data on output, downtime, thread breaks and so on are uploaded in real time, and shop-floor management systems use them for scheduling and maintenance (Chapter 26). Vision systems recognise fabric edges and seams, and artificial intelligence adjusts stitch length, tension and presser-foot pressure to suit the fabric (Chapters 14 and 31). Sewing robots are also trying to solve the old problem that “soft fabric is very hard for a machine to grasp” (Chapter 30).

1.4 Classifying sewing equipment

There are many kinds of sewing equipment, and the most useful way to classify them is first by stitch, then by machine. The stitch determines what stitch-forming mechanism the machine needs, and machines for the same class of stitch are broadly similar in construction. The international standard ISO 4915 divides stitches into six classes numbered in hundreds (ISO 4915:1991; class names per the ScienceDirect entry):

Class Name How the stitch is formed Typical machines Typical uses Chapters
100 Single-thread chainstitch One thread loops through itself Single-thread chainstitch machines, some buttonholers Temporary seams, closing sacks 1, 10
200 Hand stitch Hand sewn or imitation hand stitch Pick-stitch machine Decorative edge stitching on suit fronts 22
300 Lockstitch Two threads interlace in the middle of the fabric plies Lockstitch machines, pattern sewers, bartackers, button sewers Most seams in shirts, suits and denim 2–8, 11, 15
400 Multi-thread chainstitch Needle-thread loops interloop with looper-thread loops under the fabric Twin-needle chainstitch machine Jeans back rise, waistband 10
500 Overedge stitch The stitch wraps around the fabric edge Overlock machine Seaming knits, edge finishing 9
600 Covering stitch Cover threads on both face and back Coverstitch machine T-shirt hems, cuffs, necklines 10

By machine, the sewing equipment in a garment factory falls broadly into these groups: lockstitch machines (class 300, used in the largest numbers); overlock machines (class 500, the most numerous in knitwear factories); coverstitch and chainstitch machines (classes 600 and 400); special machines, including buttonholers, button sewers, bartackers, blindstitch and pick-stitch machines, each doing one specific operation; programmable pattern sewers and template machines, which use an X–Y table to sew any contour under program control; automatic sewing units and hanger systems, which automate one or several operations together with loading and unloading; and computerised embroidery machines, with many heads embroidering at once. Strictly speaking, the computerised flat knitting machine belongs to knitting machinery, but in a sweater factory it is the main equipment ahead of “sewing”, so Chapter 17 of this book covers it as well.

1.5 The apparel supply chain and the sewing-equipment industry

A garment passes through a long chain of stages on its way from fibre to consumer: spinning, weaving or knitting, dyeing and finishing, design and pattern making, cutting, sewing, pressing and packing, logistics and retail (Fig. 1-3). Each stage has an industry that supplies its equipment. Sewing equipment serves the “cutting” and “sewing” stages, where labour is most concentrated in garment production: spinning and weaving have long been highly automated, with one operator tending dozens of looms, but sewing is still one person at one sewing machine, aligning pieces of soft fabric by hand and feeding them under the needle.

A few figures show the scale of this industry. The International Labour Organization estimates that about 91 million people worked in the global textile and clothing industry in 2019, with women making up about 80% of those in garment manufacturing (ILO, 2022); global exports of clothing and footwear in 2020 were about US$583.5 billion, of which China accounted for 30.8% (ibid.). An analysis based on World Trade Organization data puts China’s share of global clothing exports in 2024 at about 29.6% (Sheng Lu, University of Delaware). For sewing equipment, according to data from the China Sewing Machinery Association, China produced about 6.85 million industrial sewing machines in 2024, up about 22% on the previous year (as cited by Zhiyan Consulting (in Chinese)); in the same year it exported 4.69 million industrial sewing machines worth US$1.522 billion (as cited by SewWorld from China Sewing Machinery Association and customs data (in Chinese)). Industry analysts estimate that China produces about 70–80% of the world’s sewing machinery (estimates range from 70% to 80%).

Behind these figures is a fact that matters a great deal to engineers: the users of sewing equipment are a labour-intensive, thin-margin, highly price-sensitive industry. Whether a machine sells depends not only on its speed and precision but even more on whether it lets an ordinary operator sew a few more good garments a day, with fewer thread breaks and fewer repairs. That is why, after the mechanisms and controls, this book devotes a whole part to the shop floor and the factory (Part VIII): the value of equipment is ultimately shown in a factory’s output, quality and cost.

1.6 A roadmap of the book

The eight parts of this book follow the sequence “one stitch → one machine → a class of machines → how machines are controlled → how the control is built into a prototype and tested → how machines are manufactured → a factory → the future” (Fig. 1-4). Part I starts from the stitch and breaks one stitch down into four motions that must be precisely coordinated; Part II covers, one by one, the lockstitch-machine mechanisms that produce those four motions; Parts III and IV apply the same method to overlock machines, coverstitch machines, special machines and automatic units; Part V covers electrical control, showing how mechanical timing becomes electronic timing; Part VI is hands-on: following the principles of the earlier parts, it assembles control prototypes for a lockstitch machine, an overlock machine, a pattern sewer and a flat knitting machine from modules in the parts library, and iterates them round after round with automated testing until they reach industrial grade; Part VII covers how key parts such as needles, rotary hooks, housings and cams are manufactured, how the complete machine is tested, and how a digital factory links design, manufacturing, quality and service into one whole; Part VIII walks into the garment factory and then looks to the future.

Every chapter is organised the same way: it opens with a real problem from the shop floor, then looks at structure, modelling, derivation and worked examples, then verifies things hands-on with animations or virtual labs, and ends with design notes, shop-floor notes and exercises. Any value in the book marked “worked-example value” is set for teaching; values marked “to be verified” need checking against a specific machine model. All animations, virtual labs and 3D models run directly in the web edition; we suggest trying each one as soon as you reach it.

Shop-floor notes

  • To tell what class a machine is, turn the fabric over and look at the stitch: a straight line on both sides with the interlacing hidden inside the fabric is a lockstitch (class 300); a row of loops on the underside is a chainstitch (class 100 or 400); a stitch wrapping the fabric edge is an overedge stitch (class 500); two or three parallel lines on the face with a net of thread on the back is a covering stitch (class 600).
  • A chainstitch must always be knotted or backstitched at the end of the seam, otherwise it unravels with one pull.
  • In a workshop that mixes old and new machines, whenever an operation moves to a different machine, run a test seam again: with different speed, feed type and presser-foot pressure, the same fabric can give very different results.
