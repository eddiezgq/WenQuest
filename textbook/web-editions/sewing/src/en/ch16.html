This is an optional chapter (★). It assumes the embroidery-head mechanism from Chapter 11 and the X–Y motion planning from Chapter 15.

Chapter 11 described the mechanism of an embroidery head: a case of needle bars, each threaded with a different colour, which shifts sideways as a whole to change colour; on a multi-head embroidery machine all the heads share one main shaft and one hook shaft. Chapter 15 showed that the X–Y table of a programmable pattern sewer must complete each stitch's move within the crank-angle window in which the needle is out of the fabric. An embroidery machine combines these two things and scales them up: a multi-head machine has anywhere from a few to more than twenty heads, a single embroidery frame spans all of them, and the frame holds several or even dozens of pieces of fabric, all being embroidered with the same design at the same time. This chapter covers only three issues specific to embroidery machines: the frame is large and heavy, so how does the speed fall with stitch length and number of heads; how are colour changes, trimming and jump stitches sequenced; and when one head breaks its thread, how does the whole machine stop, back up and mend (Case T8).

16.1 Opening: an eight-head embroidery machine

One manufacturer’s series of multi-head embroidery machines has 2–8 heads, each with 9, 12 or 15 needles, a top speed of 1100 r/min, and a head pitch of 360 mm or 500 mm; the presser foot is motor-controlled and can adjust its pressure to the fabric thickness within 0.05 s (manufacturer’s product information). Chapter 11 cited a single-head machine from the same manufacturer with a top speed of 1200 r/min.

When this machine embroiders a design for the back of a jacket, the design may have twenty or thirty thousand stitches and about ten colours. The operator hoops eight pieces of fabric, presses start, and the eight heads begin embroidering at once. Throughout the run the operator mainly does three things: glances at the machine during colour changes, rethreads a head when its thread breaks, and swaps in the next batch of fabric when the run is finished. The machine’s output depends on the speed, the number of colour changes and trims, how often and for how long it stops for thread breaks, and the time spent loading and unloading the frame. The first three are covered in Sections 16.3, 16.4 and 16.6 respectively.

16.2 Structure and control system of a multi-head machine

Fig. 16-1 shows the structure of a multi-head embroidery machine. A row of heads is mounted on a beam; a single main shaft runs through all the heads, driven by the main (servo) motor, and drives each head’s needle bar and take-up lever; a hook shaft under the table drives each head’s rotary hook. The needle-bar case of each head holds 9–15 needle bars, but the main shaft drives only the one aligned with the needle-bar driver (Chapter 11). The embroidery frame is a frame spanning all the heads, with one piece of fabric hooped under each head; servo motors in X and Y move it through the same design.

The control system is layered much like that of a programmable pattern sewer (Chapter 15): the main controller reads the design file and plans the speed profile and colour-change sequence; motion control interpolates X and Y within the window according to the main-shaft angle; in addition, each head has a head board that handles that head’s own functions: the needle-bar jump solenoid (which disengages that head’s needle bar from the driver and holds it up, stationary), trimming, thread-break detection and the indicator lamp. This is what is special about a multi-head machine: the shafts are shared, yet each head must be able to “not sew” on its own; otherwise, when one head has a problem, all the others have to keep it company.

16.3 A high-inertia frame: speed falls with stitch length and number of heads

How the frame moves within one stitch　As on a programmable pattern sewer, the frame movement for each stitch on an embroidery machine must be completed within the range of crank angle in which the needle is out of the fabric (Fig. 16-2). This chapter borrows the window from Chapter 15, about 204°. At 800 r/min one revolution takes 75 ms, so the window is only 42.5 ms. If the frame covers one stitch distance s with a cycloidal motion law, the peak acceleration is

a=\frac{2\pi s}{T^{2}},\qquad T=\frac{204^\circ}{6n}

Acceleration is proportional to stitch length and to the square of speed. At 800 r/min a 3 mm stitch requires 10.4 m/s², and a 7 mm stitch 24.4 m/s².

The heavier the frame, the lower the acceleration　The acceleration the frame can achieve equals the drive force minus friction, divided by the mass of the moving parts. On a multi-head machine the frame spans all the heads, so the more heads, the longer and heavier it is. The worked example in this chapter takes the equivalent moving mass of the Y axis (the heavier axis) as 10 + 4 × number of heads kg, a drive force of 800 N and friction of 50 N (all illustrative values): an 8-head machine is about 42 kg, with an available acceleration of about 17.9 m/s²; a 15-head machine is about 70 kg, with only about 10.7 m/s².

Speed limit　Setting the required acceleration equal to the available acceleration gives the speed limit for each stitch length:

n_{max}=\frac{204}{6}\sqrt{\frac{a_{max}}{2\pi s}}

The speed limit is inversely proportional to the square root of stitch length (Fig. 16-3). With few heads and short stitches this limit does not bind, and the speed is set by the head itself (1100 r/min in the worked example); with longer stitches and more heads the machine must slow down, and an 8-head machine is already slightly limited on 3–4 mm fill stitches. For an 8-head machine the limits at stitch lengths of 3 mm, 5 mm and 7 mm are about 1050, 810 and 690 r/min; for 7 mm satin stitches, a 4-head machine reaches about 870 r/min, a 15-head machine about 530 r/min, and a 20-head machine only about 470 r/min.

So, like a programmable pattern sewer, an embroidery-machine controller must compute the speed in advance from the length of each stitch (look-ahead speed control, Chapter 15), slowing down automatically on long satin sections and speeding up on short-stitch sections. Chapter 11 noted that an embroidery machine runs at only about a quarter of the speed of a lockstitch machine; the intermittent motion of the frame is one of the reasons, especially for large multi-head frames: in every stitch, a frame of several tens of kilograms must be pushed a few millimetres within a few tens of milliseconds and then brought to rest. A single-head machine has a light frame and, in this chapter’s worked example, is not limited up to stitch lengths of about 6 mm; its speed limit is also set by other factors such as the dynamics of the needle bar and take-up mechanism and the quality of the embroidery. To run 7 mm satin stitches on an 8-head machine at 850 r/min, the drive force must be raised to about 1200 N (worked example), and the motors, reduction drives and frame structure must all be strengthened accordingly.

16.4 Colour changes, trimming and jump stitches

Colour change　When one colour of a design is finished, the machine must change to the next colour. All heads change to the same colour simultaneously, in the sequence of Fig. 16-4: the main shaft decelerates, the thread is trimmed and wiped at the last stitch, and the main shaft stops at the specified needle-stop angle; the needle-bar case shifts sideways to align the needle bar of the next colour with the needle-bar driver, which then drives the new needle bar; the machine starts slowly and then accelerates. The frame does not move during the colour change. From deceleration to regaining speed, one colour change takes roughly four or five to six seconds (illustrative value; the worked example in Section 16.6 uses 6 s), and a ten-colour design needs nine changes.

Jump stitches　If two areas of the same colour are far apart, the frame must travel a long distance without the needle penetrating; this is called a jump stitch (also called a jump; note that it has nothing to do with the “skipped stitch” fault discussed in earlier chapters). During a jump the needle-bar jump solenoid disengages the needle bar from the driver and holds it up while the frame moves to the start of the next area. Because the needle does not penetrate during a jump, the move is not constrained by the needle-out-of-fabric window, but the distance of each step is still limited by the file format (Section 16.5), and the frame’s acceleration is also limited, so long jumps on a large frame must also be slowed down. For long jumps the thread must also be trimmed first so that a long thread is not dragged across the work; trimming, stopping and restarting all take time.

When digitising, arranging the colour sequence well, stitching all areas of the same colour in one run, and minimising colour changes, trims and jumps is an important way to improve embroidery productivity.

16.5 Embroidery files and digitising

Three basic stitch types　The left side of Fig. 16-5 shows the three basic embroidery stitch types. Running stitches, two or three millimetres per stitch, are used for outlines and fine lines; satin stitches go back and forth across a “column”, so the stitch length equals the column width, from about 1 mm to seven or eight millimetres, and give a glossy surface; fill stitches (often called tatami) cover large areas with rows of parallel short stitches in a staggered arrangement. If a satin column is too wide, the threads snag easily, so wider parts are changed to fill or split with additional stitches in the middle. A layer of underlay stitches is often sewn beneath satin to stabilise the fabric and lift the satin; because stitches pull the fabric in across the stitch direction and push it out along the stitch direction, shapes must be drawn slightly wider than the finished size; this is called pull compensation. All of these are set in the digitising software, and the quality of the digitising directly affects the quality of the embroidery and the stitching time.

Embroidery files　What the digitising software finally outputs to the machine is a sequence of stitch commands. One format widely used in the industry is DST: after a 512-byte header, each record is 3 bytes and stores the displacement Δx, Δy of a stitch relative to the previous one, in units of 0.1 mm, with a maximum of ±121 in each coordinate, i.e. 12.1 mm (one diagonal record can travel at most about 17 mm); flag bits distinguish normal stitches, jumps, colour changes and so on, and there is an end record (description of the DST format). The file contains no colour values, only “colour change” commands; which needle bar is used for each colour is set on the machine. Nor is there a separate trim command; a common practice is for the machine to trim automatically when it meets several consecutive jump records, with the number of consecutive jumps set on the machine. Any move longer than 12.1 mm in either direction must be split into several records. The controller reads the records one by one, sets the speed according to each stitch’s length (Section 16.3), and executes the corresponding action sequence when it meets a colour-change or jump command (Section 16.4).

16.6 Case study: what to do when one head breaks its thread

Technical problem　The dozen or so heads of a multi-head machine share one main shaft and one frame. When one head breaks its needle thread, the other heads are still embroidering normally. If nothing is done, that head keeps stitching without thread and a piece of the design is missing; if the whole machine stops and waits for rethreading, all the other heads stop with it. Worse, a thread break cannot be detected the instant it happens; by the time the machine notices, several stitches have usually already been missed.

Principle and method　According to the descriptions in two patents on multi-head embroidery machines (thread-break handling patent, mending method patent), the procedure is roughly as shown in Fig. 16-6.

Step 1 is detection: each head has a thread-break detector, for example one in which the needle thread turns a small code wheel whose pulses are read by a photoelectric switch; if no pulses arrive for several consecutive stitches, a thread break is declared. It sees only the needle thread; a broken or exhausted bobbin thread must be detected separately. Step 2: the whole machine stops, and the lamp on the broken head lights. Step 3: if the backup distance is long, the heads without a break first trim their threads, so that long threads are not pulled out of their needle eyes during the backup. Step 4: the frame runs backwards along the stitch data already sewn, past the missed stitches. Step 5: the operator rethreads the broken head (the order of rethreading and backing up differs between models). Step 6: only the broken head penetrates and mends; the needle bars of all other heads are held up by their jump solenoids and stay still, and the mending continues up to the position where the machine stopped. Step 7: back at the break point, the whole machine resumes embroidering together.

Detection delay and backup stitches　The lower part of Fig. 16-6 traces this process along the stitch path. From the actual break to the stop, d stitches have already been missed (the detection delay), consisting of the stitches needed to confirm the break and the stitches turned while stopping; the example in the first patent totals about 10 stitches. The number of backup stitches b must be at least d, preferably two or three more, so that the mending overlaps the original stitches slightly; if b is less than d, the mending does not join up and a gap of missed stitches remains, making a reject; if b is too large, time is wasted and the overlap becomes thick. The faster the detection, the smaller d and the fewer stitches need to be backed up.

Number of heads and efficiency　A thread break on any head stops the whole machine. If each head breaks its thread on average λ times per ten thousand stitches, a machine with H heads stops for thread breaks H times as often as a single-head machine. Fig. 16-7 is a worked example: a 20000-stitch design at 800 r/min, 25 min of pure sewing; each thread break takes 40 s to handle (walking over, rethreading, confirming), plus about 1.6 s for backup and mending; 6 colour changes of 6 s each (illustrative values); frame loading and unloading are not included. At a break rate of 0.5 per head per ten thousand stitches, overall machine efficiency is about 88% for a 4-head machine, about 80% for 8 heads, about 69% for 15 heads and about 63% for 20 heads. Going from 4 to 20 heads, a fivefold increase, raises the pieces produced per hour only from about 8.5 to about 30, about 3.6 times. For a 20-head machine to reach 80% efficiency, the break rate must fall to about 0.2 per head per ten thousand stitches, or the handling time per break must be cut to about 15 s.

Discussion　This case shows that the “multi” in multi-head is a double-edged sword: one drive system serves many heads, saving equipment cost and floor space, but a problem at any head stops the whole machine. Machines with many heads therefore depend especially on a few things: sensitive thread-break detection (to reduce d), automatic backup and mending (to reduce manual work), independent needle-bar jump and trimming on each head (so the other heads are not affected), and good thread, needles and tension adjustment (to lower the break rate). How many machines one operator can tend at once also depends on these.

16.7 Other devices and the limits of the model

Embroidery machines have many other special devices: a sequin device feeds a sequin beside the needle, and the needle passes through the hole in the sequin’s centre to attach it to the fabric; chenille (towel) embroidery uses a hooked needle to make a chainstitch and form loops; there are also cording, bead embroidery and others. The models in the product information cited earlier can be fitted with sequin, bead, cording and similar devices. Each of them adds to a head a mechanism driven by a solenoid or stepper motor and synchronised with the main-shaft angle; the control principles are the same as in the earlier chapters of this book and are not developed further here.

The models in this chapter are illustrative. The needle-out-of-fabric window borrows the 204° of Chapter 15, whereas on a real embroidery machine the window depends on the needle-bar stroke and the fabric thickness; the frame mass, drive force and friction are values chosen to show the trends, without considering belt elasticity, synchronisation of drives at both ends, or frame vibration; and only the heavier of the X and Y axes is estimated. The thread-break efficiency model assumes that breaks on different heads are independent and that handling time is fixed; it does not include frame loading and unloading, trimming or jump time, nor the waiting that occurs when one operator tends several machines.

Shop-floor notes

  • If stitches are distorted or needles break on long satin sections, first check whether the machine slows down automatically on those sections; if necessary, narrow the satin columns or change them to fill when digitising.
  • When the frame is replaced with a larger size or attachments are added, its mass increases and the speed limit must be lowered accordingly.
  • If one head breaks thread frequently, first check that head’s threading path, tension and needle, then the thread-break detector; if the detection delay has grown, the number of backup stitches must be increased too.
  • A gap in the design after mending means the backup was shorter than the detection delay; a raised overlap means it backed up too far.
  • When digitising, stitching same-colour areas in one run to reduce colour changes and jumps shortens embroidery time more than raising the speed does.
