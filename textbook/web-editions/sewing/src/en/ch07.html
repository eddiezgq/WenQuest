Chapter 7Thread Tension and Stitch Quality

The previous four chapters explained how a stitch is “formed”: the needle carries the needle thread down, the rotary hook catches the loop, the take-up lever pulls the thread in, and the feed dog moves the fabric on. But being formed is not the same as being formed well. On the same machine the stitches are sometimes flat and firm; sometimes little dots of bobbin thread appear on the top side; sometimes loops of loose thread trail on the underside; sometimes the sewn seam puckers into a ridge; and sometimes the thread simply breaks partway through. The common root of these faults is thread tension. This chapter first explains where the tension in the needle and bobbin threads comes from and how it varies within one stitch, then uses an illustrative model to show how tension determines the position of the interlacing point and how tight the stitch is. It then covers stitch defects such as seam pucker and thread breakage, and closes with a brief introduction to electronically controlled tension.

7.1 Opening: four visible faults

When a mechanic looks at a length of seam, he checks four things first. First, is the bobbin-thread colour showing on the top? If a small dot of bobbin thread appears at every needle hole on the top side, the bobbin thread is said to be “pulled up”. Second, is there slack needle thread on the underside? Loops of needle thread trailing on the back, in bad cases in whole patches, are known as “looping” or a “hairy underside”. Third, is the sewn area flat? If, after two pieces are joined, fine ridges rise along the seam line, that is seam pucker. Fourth, does the thread break? The needle thread suddenly snaps in mid-seam, or the thread frays and turns whitish.

The first two faults come from a wrong ratio between the tensions of the two threads; the third from both tensions being too high (it may also have other causes, see Section 7.6); the fourth from the peak tension exceeding what the thread can withstand. This chapter therefore answers three questions: how tension is produced; how the ratio of the two thread tensions determines where the stitch sits; and how the magnitude of the tension affects puckering and thread breakage.

7.2 Where tension comes from

Fig. 7-1 brings together the parts along the needle-thread and bobbin-thread paths that produce tension.

Tension assembly The main source of needle-thread tension is the tension assembly. It consists of a fixed tension stud, two tension discs fitted on the stud, a compression spring and a tension nut. The needle thread passes between the two discs; the spring presses the discs together, and when the thread is drawn through, friction acts on both of its sides. If the spring force is N and the coefficient of friction between thread and discs is μ, the tension of the thread increases by 2μN on passing through the assembly:

\[T_{\text{out}} = T_{\text{in}} + 2\mu N\]

Tightening the tension nut compresses the spring further, N increases, and the needle-thread tension rises with it; this is exactly the direction of adjustment specified in the instruction manual (a manufacturer’s instruction manual). When the presser foot is lifted, a release pin pushes the two discs apart so that the thread can be drawn out with no resistance, making it easy to remove the work. The service manual requires the discs to open by at least 0.5 mm at that point, and possibly more than 1.0 mm for thick thread (a manufacturer’s service manual).

Thread guides and capstan friction Between the tension assembly and the needle eye, the needle thread also passes round several thread guides, the take-up lever eye and the needle-bar thread guide. Each time the thread wraps round a cylindrical part it is subject to friction. When thread being pulled in a given direction wraps round a cylinder through a wrap angle θ, its tension increases from T₁ to

\[T_2 = T_1\,e^{\mu\theta}\]

This is the capstan equation (Euler’s formula). It shows that the tension increase due to friction is not a fixed amount but a multiplier, and that it grows exponentially with the wrap angle (Fig. 7-2). With μ = 0.2, a guide wrapped through 1 rad (about 57°) raises the tension by a factor of 1.22; with μ = 0.35, a 180° wrap raises it by a factor of 3.

Reading the formula the other way round is just as important: the thread is always pulled from its low-tension end towards its high-tension end, and friction makes the “upstream” tension lower than the “downstream” tension. When a stitch is being tightened, the thread slides from the interlacing point up through the needle eye towards the take-up lever, so the interlacing-point end is upstream and has the lower tension; the needle thread turns through about 180° at the needle eye, so the tension at the take-up lever is cut down a great deal by the time it reaches the interlacing point. This is why the tension set on the tension assembly has to be several times the bobbin tension for the stitch to balance (Section 7.4).

Bobbin-case spring The bobbin-thread tension comes from a leaf spring on the bobbin case. After leaving the bobbin, the bobbin thread passes under the leaf spring, which presses it against the bobbin case; an adjusting screw changes the spring pressure and so the bobbin-thread tension (same instruction manual as above). The bobbin thread is under tension only while it is being drawn out, and that happens mainly while the take-up lever is tightening the stitch.

Check spring As explained in Chapter 4, the check spring takes up the surplus needle thread while the take-up lever is paying thread out, keeping a small tension on the needle thread. Its stroke and pressure can also be adjusted (same instruction manual); the service manual requires its stroke to be completed when the needle point reaches the surface of a medium-thickness fabric, with a stroke of about 7 mm (same service manual).

7.3 Needle-thread tension within one stitch

The needle-thread tension is not constant within a stitch. Measuring it with a tension sensor downstream of the tension assembly gives a curve of needle-thread tension against main-shaft angle over one stitch. A study of single-needle lockstitch machines found several regularities: the highest peak occurs at the moment the stitch is tightened, and it is directly controlled by the pre-tension of the tension assembly; thick threads have lower tightening tension, which may affect stitch balance; fabric thickness has almost no effect on needle-thread tension; near the tension assembly, speed has very little effect; and the bobbin-thread pre-tension affects the needle-thread tension only during the phase in which the take-up lever pulls up and the loop is cast off the rotary hook (a study of dynamic needle-thread tension on a single-needle lockstitch machine).

From these regularities, combined with the timing of the worked examples in Chapters 4 and 5, one can draw an illustrative curve of needle-thread tension over one stitch (Fig. 7-3).

Start at 0°. After cast-off, the take-up lever rises rapidly, pulling the loop back in and drawing the interlacing point into the fabric; the tension rises quickly, forming the tightening peak. After tightening, the take-up lever continues to rise, and up to its tightest point (55°, Chapter 4) it draws out of the tension assembly the thread needed for the next stitch; the thread is then sliding between the tension discs, and the tension equals that produced by the assembly plus the friction along the way. After 55° the take-up lever begins to pay out thread, the needle thread goes slack and the check spring keeps a small tension on it. After loop catching (206°), the loop is enlarged by the rotary hook and drawn round the bobbin case; friction between thread and hook raises the tension again, forming a second, smaller peak. When the take-up lever pulls up and the loop is cast off the hook (around 341°), the bobbin-thread pre-tension also comes into play; after cast-off the next tightening begins.

The curve makes a practical point: the setting of the tension assembly mainly affects the tightening peak. How tightly the stitch is set and where the interlacing point ends up are decided at the moment of tightening; whether the thread breaks is also mostly decided by this peak.

7.4 Stitch balance

The interlacing point is pulled in opposite directions by the two threads At the moment the stitch is tightened, the take-up lever pulls the needle thread upward and the interlacing point moves up with it; the bobbin thread is drawn out of the bobbin case, and its tension pulls the interlacing point downward. When the two pulls are unequal, the interlacing point moves towards the side with the higher tension. If the needle-thread side is much greater, the interlacing point is pulled out through the top of the fabric and the bobbin thread can be seen on the top side; if the bobbin-thread side is much greater, the interlacing point is pulled out through the bottom, and the needle thread forms small loops on the underside. Only when the two are roughly equal does the interlacing point stay in the middle of the fabric thickness, with each side showing only its own thread. This is called stitch balance.

Why there is a tolerance range Without friction, the interlacing point would stay put only if the two tensions were exactly equal — clearly impossible to set. In reality, the threads have to turn at the upper and lower edges of the needle hole in the fabric, and the friction there acts like a brake: the interlacing point still stays put while the two tensions differ within a certain range. By the capstan equation, this range is a factor, which this book calls the balance band B: while the tension ratio r (needle thread ÷ bobbin thread) at the interlacing point lies between 1/B and B, the interlacing point stays within the fabric thickness; outside this range it is pulled to the top or bottom surface.

This chapter expresses this with an illustrative model. Strictly speaking, the friction “brake” only says that any position within the balance band can hold; it does not say where the interlacing point will settle. The expression below uses a linear relation to represent the trend that “the further the tension ratio departs from 1, the closer the interlacing point lies to a surface”; it is a teaching simplification. Let the position z of the interlacing point run from 0 at the bottom of the fabric to 1 at the top:

\[z = 0.5 + 0.5\cdot\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\ln r}{\ln B},\,-1,\,1\right)\]

z between 0.4 and 0.6 counts as balanced; this book calls it the balance zone. The size of B depends on the fabric: on a thick, soft fabric with a rough surface the friction at the hole edges is high, B is large and balance is easy to set; on a thin, slippery fabric B is small, and a slight deviation in the tension ratio sends the interlacing point to the surface. The worked example takes 1.8 for lining, 2.2 for cotton and 3.0 for denim (illustrative values). Fig. 7-4 shows cross-sections on cotton at five tension ratios, and the z–r relation for the three fabrics.

Why the tension assembly must be several times tighter than the bobbin The model uses the needle-thread tension at the interlacing point. Between the tension assembly and the interlacing point, the needle thread is first amplified by the thread guides (worked example: wrap angle 1 rad, μ = 0.2, a factor of 1.22), then reduced as it passes through the needle eye (wrap angle π, μ = 0.35, leaving 1/3); taken together, the needle-thread tension at the interlacing point is about 0.41 times the tension-assembly setting. So with a bobbin tension of 40 cN, to obtain r = 1 the tension assembly must be set to about 98 cN. These two factors are worked-example values and vary widely between machines and threads, but “the tension assembly must be several times tighter than the bobbin” is generally true.

7.5 The order of tension adjustment

There is an established order for setting tension: set the bobbin thread first, then set the needle thread to suit it. There are two reasons. First, the bobbin tension is determined by the bobbin-case spring and stays essentially constant within a stitch, so it is a stable reference; the needle-thread tension is influenced by many things — take-up, thread guides and speed — and is better suited to being the one that is “adjusted to match”. Second, adjusting the bobbin case means taking it out, whereas adjusting the tension assembly only means turning a nut; for routine changes of material it is most convenient to touch only the tension assembly.

Following this order also gives direction to fault diagnosis. Bobbin thread showing on the top means r is too large: either the needle thread is too tight or the bobbin thread too loose. If the tension assembly is at its usual setting and the machine suddenly starts doing this, the bobbin side is the more likely culprit — the bobbin-case spring has loosened, the bobbin has been wound too loosely, or it has been inserted the wrong way round. Loops of needle thread on the underside mean r is too small: the needle thread is too loose or the bobbin thread too tight, or the needle thread has not seated properly between the tension discs (if the machine is threaded with the presser foot down, the discs are closed and the thread may not slip between them; threading should be done with the presser foot raised and the discs open). So when you find an unbalanced stitch, do not rush to turn the tension nut; first work out which side has changed.

Stitch balance only says that the ratio of the two tensions is right, not that their magnitude is right. If both threads are too loose, the interlacing point can still be in the middle, but the stitches are slack and pull out when snagged; if both are too tight, the interlacing point is likewise in the middle, but the seam puckers and the thread breaks. The next two sections deal with these in turn.

7.6 Seam pucker

Material from a thread manufacturer groups the causes of seam pucker into four types (a thread manufacturer’s technical material); Fig. 7-5 illustrates them.

Tension pucker The thread is stretched as the stitch is tightened; once the seam is sewn the tension disappears and the thread tries to spring back to its original length, shortening the strip of fabric along the seam, which can only buckle. The remedy is to reduce the tension to the minimum consistent with stitch balance, not to wind the bobbin too tightly, and to choose a thread with well-controlled elongation.

Structural pucker In a very tightly woven fabric there is no space between the yarns to accommodate needle and thread; the needle pushes the yarns apart as it penetrates, the fabric is stretched along the seam, and the material on both sides of the seam bulges. The remedy is to use a finer needle and thread, reduce the number of stitches per centimetre and, where possible, sew on the bias.

Dimensional instability After washing and pressing, thread and fabric shrink by different amounts. Whether the fabric shrinks and the thread does not, or the thread shrinks and the fabric does not, the seam puckers. The remedy is to choose a low-shrinkage thread and match the shrinkage behaviour of shell fabric, interlining and thread.

Feed pucker The two plies are fed by different amounts and the ply with the excess puckers. This is exactly the ply shift discussed in Chapter 6; the remedy is to adjust presser-foot pressure and feed-dog height, or change the feed system.

Of the four, tension pucker relates directly to this chapter, and an illustrative model can estimate it: the elongation of the thread during tightening is about ε = T/EA (T the tension, EA the tensile stiffness of the thread, approximately its strength divided by its elongation at break); the thread consumption per centimetre of seam is about (s + t)/s (s the stitch length, t the fabric thickness); the fractional shortening of the seam caused by the recovery of the two threads is then roughly the product of the two. The fabric itself can absorb a small part of the shortening (about 0.3% for lining and 0.6% for cotton, illustrative values); the rest shows up as pucker.

Worked example Two plies of cotton (fabric thickness 1.0 mm), stitch length 3 mm, Tex 24 thread (strength about 1000 cN, elongation at break about 15%, EA ≈ 6700 cN, illustrative values). With the tension assembly at 100 cN and the bobbin at 40 cN, the mean tension of the two threads at the interlacing point is about 40 cN; the seam shortens by about 0.81%, of which about 0.21% exceeds what the fabric can absorb — about 0.8 mm over 40 cm, with no visible pucker. If both threads are tightened to about 2.2 times their original values (tension assembly 220 cN, bobbin 90 cN), the stitch is still balanced, but the shortening rises to about 1.8% and the pucker to about 1.2% — nearly 5 mm short over 40 cm, which is a clearly visible pucker.

Fig. 7-6 also shows the role of thread size: at the same tension, a thicker thread has a larger EA, stretches less and puckers less. But a thick thread needs a thick needle, which on a tightly woven light fabric causes structural pucker instead. So on light fabrics, the fundamental remedy for tension pucker is still to lower the tension of both threads together to “just not loose”.

7.7 Thread breakage and other defects

Thread breakage When the needle thread breaks, it usually breaks at the tightening peak. The height of the tightening peak is set by the pre-tension of the tension assembly (Section 7.3), and the tension the thread can withstand is not the strength measured on a tensile tester: at high speed the thread repeatedly passes through the needle eye and rubs against the hook, and the needle heats up by friction in the fabric (synthetic threads soften when hot), so the thread’s real capacity is substantially reduced. This chapter’s model uses two illustrative conventions: the tightening peak at the take-up lever is about 1.22 T_d (1 + 0.15 (n/5000)²), where T_d is the tension-assembly setting, 1.22 is the amplification by the thread guides, and the last term assumes that the peak at the take-up lever increases by about 15% as the speed rises from low speed to 5000 r/min (this does not contradict “speed has very little effect near the tension assembly”, since the measuring points differ); and a tightening peak not exceeding one third of the nominal strength counts as safe.

Worked example Four plies of denim: to set the stitches into fabric this thick, the tension of both threads at the interlacing point must reach about 100 cN (illustrative value), so the tension assembly has to be set to about 246 cN; at 5000 r/min the tightening peak at the take-up lever is about 346 cN. That is 35% of the nominal strength of Tex 24 thread (about 1000 cN), more than one third; for Tex 40 thread (about 1700 cN) it is 20%. Heavy material therefore calls for thick thread, consistent with the heavy-material machines in the instruction manual using thick needles (DB×1 #20–#23) (same instruction manual as above).

7.8 Electronic tension

The tension of a mechanical tension assembly is set by the position of a nut: each change of fabric or operation requires manual readjustment, and it cannot change during sewing. A typical electronic tension design replaces the compression spring with tension discs pushed by a solenoid; the needle-thread tension is then determined by a current set by the controller, so it can be set and stored on the operation panel and switched automatically from operation to operation, or even between different sections of one seam. The fully digital model of one manufacturer’s high-end lockstitch machine puts the needle-thread tension under digital control (a manufacturer’s product information). Control methods for electronic tension are covered in Chapter 14.

7.9 Limits of the model

The tension-balance and pucker models of this chapter are illustrative. The ratio of needle-thread tension at the interlacing point to the tension-assembly setting (0.41) depends on the guide wrap angles, the shape of the needle eye, and the surface and lubrication of the thread, and varies widely between machines; the balance band B merely sums up the friction at the edges of the needle hole in a single number; the pucker model considers only the elastic recovery of the thread and counts the recovery of the thread segment passing through the fabric thickness entirely as seam shortening, which overestimates it; nor does it account for thread creep, the bending stiffness of the fabric, or the interaction of the fabric on the two sides of the seam; and the breakage criterion is cruder still. Real tension is dynamic (Fig. 7-3), and the tension at the instant of tightening also depends on take-up lever speed, the check spring and the elasticity of the thread. The value of the model is to make two relations clear — “the ratio sets the position; the magnitude sets tightness and pucker”; the actual tension values still have to be set by test sewing on the shop floor.

Shop-floor notes

  • Set the bobbin thread first, then set the needle thread to suit it; for routine material changes, touch only the tension assembly.
  • When the stitch is unbalanced, first work out which side has changed: if a machine that normally sews well suddenly shows bobbin thread on top, check the bobbin case and bobbin first.
  • For loops on the underside, first check that the needle thread was threaded with the presser foot raised and is seated between the two tension discs.
  • For pucker, loosen both threads together while keeping the stitch balanced; do not use overly thick needles and threads on light fabrics.
  • For thread breakage, check the needle eye, thread guides and hook point for burrs, then look at the tension and the needle–thread combination.

  1. The needle thread enters the tension assembly at a tension of 10 cN; the spring force N is 2 N (200 cN), and the coefficient of friction between thread and discs is μ = 0.2. What is the tension on leaving the tension assembly? And after passing round a thread guide with a wrap angle of 1 rad and μ = 0.2?
    Answer90 cN; about 110 cN.
  2. The needle thread passes in turn round three thread guides, each with a wrap angle of 90°, μ = 0.25. What is the total tension amplification factor? If the wrap angle of one guide is reduced to 45°, what does the factor become?
    AnswerAbout 3.25; about 2.67.
  3. Two plies of cotton (balance band B = 2.2), bobbin tension 40 cN, needle-thread tension at the interlacing point 0.41 times the tension-assembly setting. For the interlacing-point position to lie between 0.4 and 0.6, what is the adjustment range of the tension assembly? Verify in Virtual lab 7-1.
    AnswerAbout 84–115 cN; if the tension at the interlacing point must also be at least 35 cN, the lower limit rises to about 86 cN.

Find the minimum tension-assembly setting, and use the convention of Section 7.7 to find the tightening peak at 5000 r/min.
