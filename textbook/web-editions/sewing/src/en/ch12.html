The first eleven chapters were all about a single sewing machine: how the needle pierces the fabric, how the thread is caught, how the fabric is fed away. But a garment passes through anywhere from a dozen to several hundred operations, and a sewing room holds dozens or hundreds of machines and as many people. How the machines are laid out, how the work is divided and how cut parts flow between people and machines determine how many garments the room turns out per hour, and how much work in progress is sitting on the line. This chapter covers three forms of organisation. In an automatic sewing unit, one machine locates, sews, cuts and stacks by itself, and the operator only loads the parts. In a hanger system, the parts of each garment hang on a carrier and travel one garment at a time, with a computer deciding which work centre each one goes to. In a flexible cell, a few people and about ten machines are arranged in a U; each person tends several machines and the operators hand work on to one another.

Chapter 29 compares progressive bundle lines, hanger systems and U-shaped cells from the viewpoint of the whole factory (Virtual lab 29-1), while the mechanics and control of hanger systems are treated in detail here. This chapter therefore does not compare whole lines; it deals with three questions only: how many automatic units one operator should tend; the mechanical structure and dispatching rules of a hanger system; and why a bucket-brigade cell balances its own work. All three are worked out with simple models and simulations, and readers can adjust the parameters and check the results themselves in two virtual labs.

12.1 Opening: three sewing rooms

Start with three scenes. The first is the pocket-welting section of a shirt and trouser factory. To make a double-welt pocket with a flap on a lockstitch machine, a skilled operator must mark the lines, fold the edges, sew two rows of stitching, slit the opening and then cut the triangular notches at both ends; one pocket takes several minutes, and the two sides do not always come out symmetrical. Now the section has a few automatic pocket-welting machines. One operator walks from machine to machine, placing the trouser panel, welt strip and flap, pressing the pedal and moving on to the next machine; each machine sews, cuts and flips the panel onto the stacker by itself. The question here is: how many machines should one person tend?

The second is a hanger-system sewing room. Overhead runs a loop of rail, and the cut parts hang on carriers that circulate along it. When a carrier passes a work centre, the switch opens and the carrier slides into the work centre’s spur rail; when the operator finishes the operation, she presses a button and the carrier is sent back to the main rail and on to the next operation. There are no bundles of cut parts piled on the floor; only two or three carriers hang beside each work centre. The questions here are: once a carrier has finished an operation, which operator should it be sent to? And how many carriers in total should be on the line?

The third is a seven-person U-shaped cell: a dozen or so machines arranged in a U with the entrance and exit on the same side. Operators are not tied to any one machine. Each carries one garment forward, working on it; when the last operator finishes a garment, she walks back and takes over the garment in the hands of the person before her. Nobody calculates an assignment of operations for them, yet after a while each person’s share of the work matches her own speed. The question here is: why does the work balance without anyone scheduling it?

12.2 Common structure of automatic sewing units

There are many kinds of automatic sewing unit: automatic pocket welters, pocket setters, sleeve setters, placket units, hemming units, and the template machines of Chapter 15. They do different jobs but are built in much the same way, and all of them can be broken down into five steps (Fig. 12-1).

Step one is picking up or loading the parts. In most units a person still places the parts on the loading table, because picking exactly one ply off a stack of limp fabric remains the hardest step to automate; some units pick plies with needle grippers or vacuum suction cups, which suits stiffer fabrics. Step two is locating and folding: locating pins, stops and photoelectric sensors find the fabric edge, and folding plates driven by pneumatic cylinders fold the edge and hold it down. This step decides whether the seam lands in the right place, and most of a unit’s “know-how” is hidden in its folding plates and locating fixtures. Step three is sewing: the sewing head itself is no different from a lockstitch or chainstitch machine, but the fabric is carried along a programmed path by a clamp or feeding mechanism, the X–Y table of Chapter 15 being one example. Step four is cutting, which may be a knife, corner knives, or just thread trimming. Step five is stacking: a stacker bar or rollers flip the finished part onto a stacker and lay it neatly ready for the next operation.

The sequence of these five steps is orchestrated by the controller: each time an action is completed, the corresponding sensor gives a signal, and only then does the controller issue the command for the next step; if any sensor along the way fails to report, the machine stops and raises an alarm. Sequential control of this kind is usually written as a state machine. The thread trimming, thread wiping and clamp actions of the programmable pattern sewer in Chapter 15 already used this method, and Chapter 18 treats it systematically.

The lower part of Fig. 12-1 is the time budget of one unit. A cycle splits into two parts: manual loading a, and automatic locating, sewing, cutting and stacking m. During m the operator is free. That is why one person can often tend several units, and it is the problem worked out in Section 12.3.

Example: the automatic pocket welter　The welt pocket (in the trade, the “jetted” pocket) is the most labour-intensive component of trousers and suit jackets, and one of the first to be turned into an automatic unit. Fig. 12-2 shows the sewing sequence of a double-welt pocket with a flap. The operator places the body panel on the table, puts the flap and the welt strip in their marked positions and presses the pedal; the clamp comes down and holds them. Sensors read the position and angle of the two ends of the flap, and from these the program calculates the start and end points of the two seams and the positions of the corner knives at both ends. The folding plates fold the welt strip into two welts; a twin-needle lockstitch head sews the two parallel rows at once while the centre knife slits the opening between them. After sewing, the corner knives rise from below and cut a Y-shaped notch at each end; the clamp opens and the stacker flips the panel onto the stacker. Turn the welts through the opening and the welt pocket is done.

One manufacturer’s automatic pocket welter reaches up to 3000 sti/min, with pocket lengths of 18–220 mm (up to 300 mm on the long version), two needles and a needle gauge of 8–20 mm (22–32 mm on the long version). The feed is driven by a stepper motor, the centre knife by a separate stepper motor with adjustable speed, and on the slanted-pocket model the lateral position of the corner knives can be set electronically in steps of 0.1 mm; the machine needs compressed air at 0.5 MPa (a manufacturer’s product information). The manufacturer’s catalogue gives the output as 1900–2300 pieces per 8 h without flap and 1600–1900 pieces per 8 h with flap (a manufacturer’s catalogue). That works out to about 12.5–18 s per piece, including the manual loading time.

These figures show where the value of an automatic unit lies. It is not sewing speed (3000 sti/min is no faster than a lockstitch machine, in fact somewhat slower); it is that the skilled operator’s experience has been turned into fixtures and a program. Pocket welting used to need an operator with years of experience; now a newcomer who can place the parts correctly can do it, and the two pockets come out identical.

12.3 Multi-machine tending: how many units pay off best

The cycle of one automatic unit is a + m: the operator loads for a, the machine runs automatically for m. If one person tends only one unit, she is idle for m in every cycle. If she tends N units, she walks round them in turn; at each one she takes off the finished part, loads new parts and walks on to the next. Let the walk between two adjacent units take w; the question is which N is best. We assume the machines are arranged in a ring or a U, so that walking back from the last unit to the first also takes only w; if they stand in a straight row the return walk is longer and must be accounted for separately.

First take the case with fixed times and no variation. In one round the operator walks one leg and does one loading for each machine, so her round time is N(a + w). Each machine’s cycle is at least a + m, because it can only be loaded again once it has finished sewing. Whichever is larger sets the cycle time:

T_c=\max\big(a+m,\;N(a+w)\big),\qquad \text{output}=\frac{3600N}{T_c}\ \ \text{(pieces/h)}

The number of machines at which the two are equal is called the balance point:

N^*=\frac{a+m}{a+w}

When N is below N*, the machines never wait for the operator and the operator has idle time; when N is above N*, the operator never stops, the machines finish sewing and have to wait to be loaded, and the extra machines simply stand waiting too.

Worked example　Take a = 5 s, m = 12 s, w = 2 s, a cycle of 17 s, of the same order as the automatic pocket welter. N* = 17/7 ≈ 2.43 (Fig. 12-3). With 1 machine, output is about 212 pieces per hour. With 2 machines the cycle is set by the machines (17 s is longer than 14 s), output is about 424 pieces per hour, and the operator is idle 3 s per round, giving a busy rate of about 82% (about 59% loading and about 23% walking). With 3 machines the cycle is set by the operator (21 s), output is about 514 pieces per hour, and each machine waits 4 s per round for the operator. With 4 or more machines output does not rise any further and stays at 514, because the operator is already fully loaded.

When N* is not a whole number, whether to choose 2 or 3 machines depends on whether the operator or the machine is more expensive. In output per hour, 3 machines give 21% more; in output per machine, 2 machines are better: 212 pieces per machine per hour, against only 171 with 3. If machines are expensive and labour is cheap, tend fewer machines; in the opposite case, tend more.

When times vary　In practice loading is sometimes quick and sometimes slow, and sewing time changes with pocket length and fabric. The dashed line in Fig. 12-3 is an 8-hour simulation with 30% variation (coefficient of variation 0.3) in the loading, sewing and walking times. With 2 machines output is about 388 pieces per hour, about 8% less than with no variation; with 3 machines it is about 494, about 4% less; with 4 machines it is hardly affected. The loss is largest when the number of machines is close to N*, because there both the operator and the machines have little slack: if one machine sews a little slowly, the operator stands and waits; if the operator loads a little slowly, the next machine has finished and waits for her. The time a machine stands waiting because the operator is busy at other machines is called machine interference. With no variation it appears only when N is above N* (for example, 4 s per machine per round with 3 machines); with variation, near the balance point both operator and machines wait at the same time, and both interference and operator idle time increase. Far from N*, one side always has slack that absorbs the variation. So to work close to the balance point you must first reduce the variation in the times, for example by fixing the position of the loading table and preparing the flaps in advance.

Which of two improvements pays off　Starting from 2 machines, there are two ways to raise output: give the operator one more machine, or improve the loading fixture to cut a from 5 s to 3.5 s. By the formula above, adding a machine raises output from 424 to 514 pieces. Shortening loading alone moves the balance point to 15.5/5.5 ≈ 2.8 and raises output with 2 machines to about 465 pieces. Doing both—3 machines with a = 3.5 s—gives about 655 pieces, and the gain of 231 pieces is more than the sum of the two separate gains (90 + 41 pieces). The reason is that shorter loading reduces both the machine cycle and the operator’s round, pushing the balance point close to 3 machines, so that the third machine hardly waits for the operator any more (only 1 s per round). 12.4 Mechanical structure of a hanger system

A progressive bundle line ties ten or twenty sets of cut parts into a bundle; each operation processes the whole bundle and then pushes the whole bundle on to the next. Several bundles are always piled beside each work centre, and a garment spends several days in the sewing room between cutting and completion, most of it waiting in a bundle. A hanger system (unit production system, UPS) changes the unit from “one bundle” to “one garment”: the parts of each garment hang on one carrier, the rail delivers it to a work centre, and as soon as one operation is done it goes on to the next.

Fig. 12-4 shows the structure of a hanger system. Above the sewing room runs a closed main rail; a motor-driven chain inside it pushes the carriers round the loop. Beside each work centre is a spur rail, and an entry switch is fitted where the spur joins the main rail. Before reaching the switch, a carrier passes a reader that reads the carrier’s number (barcode or RFID tag); the control system looks up the garment’s next operation and which work centre it should go to, and decides whether to open that work centre’s switch. Once in the spur, the carrier queues up to the operator’s hand by gravity or a small chain; this stretch is the work centre’s buffer. When the operator finishes, she presses a button or swipes a card, a lift returns the carrier to the main rail, and the system records who completed the operation and when. At the loading station the cut parts are hung on carriers; at the unloading station the finished garments are taken off or sent on to inspection and pressing.

The conveying capacity of the main rail equals the chain speed divided by the carrier spacing. For example, at a chain speed of 12 m/min and a carrier spacing of 0.3 m, 40 carriers can pass per minute, 2400 per hour (illustrative values). A T-shirt has 6 operations; with loading and unloading, each garment travels 7 legs on the main rail, so at 120 garments per hour the main rail must handle about 840 movements per hour—well within its capacity. What really needs calculating is the length of the work-centre spurs (how many carriers they can hold) and the total number of carriers, and these two numbers are determined precisely by the dispatching rule and the WIP limit, the subject of the next section.

What if a work centre’s spur is full? The carrier does not enter and keeps circulating on the main rail, to be reassigned when it comes round again. In this way the main rail itself becomes a shared buffer; but carriers circling idle on the main rail not only travel for nothing, they also take up conveying capacity, so this has to be kept under control with a WIP limit.

Compared with the bundle system, single-piece flow has three benefits: less work in progress and shorter throughput time; real-time records of every completed operation, which give a basis for piece-rate pay and quality traceability; and parts that never touch the floor, so less handling and soiling. The price is the equipment investment and higher demands on planning and maintenance. The made-to-measure suit factory in Section 29.1 of Chapter 29 relies on a hanger system to send each garment to the right work centres according to its order.

12.5 Dispatching

The brain of a hanger system is its dispatching rule: when a carrier has finished one operation and several people can do the next one, whom should it go to?

A worked example　Let a T-shirt have 6 operations: join shoulders 30 s, set collar 45 s, set sleeves 60 s, close side seams 50 s, hem sleeves 35 s, hem bottom 40 s, a total standard time of 260 s. The sewing room has 10 operators; each can do 1–2 operations, with efficiencies between 0.8 and 1.1 (1 means working to standard time). For example, operator 4 has efficiency 0.8 on setting the collar and 1.0 on setting sleeves; operator 7 has 1.0 on closing side seams and 0.9 on hemming sleeves. The loading station launches one carrier every 5 s and stops when the number of carriers on the line reaches the WIP limit (for example 40). A carrier takes 15 s to travel from the main rail to a work centre; each work centre can queue at most 3 carriers, and when all suitable work centres are full the carrier goes round the main rail once more (60 s) and is reassigned. The actual work time of each job is the standard time divided by efficiency, with 15% random variation. Work times, skills and efficiencies are illustrative values.

Three dispatching rules are compared:

  • Fixed work centre: each operation goes only to the operators whose main job it is, in turn when there are several;
  • Shortest queue: send to the operator who can do this operation and has the fewest carriers queued;
  • Earliest finish: using each operator’s efficiency, estimate when she would finish the work in hand and in her queue plus this job, and send it to the one who would finish earliest.

The left of Fig. 12-5 shows the results of a 4-hour simulation averaged over 5 runs. The fixed-work-centre rule gives about 111 pieces per hour, shortest queue about 126 and earliest finish about 124: dynamic dispatching produces 12%–13% more than fixed work centres. The simulation is random; with another set of random numbers these figures will shift by a piece or two, and the numbers readers see in Virtual lab 12-2 will differ slightly from the figure.

Why does the fixed-work-centre rule produce less? Look at the capacity of each operation. When work is assigned by main job, only operator 8 (efficiency 1.1) hems sleeves, and that operator can do at most 3600 ÷ (35 ÷ 1.1) ≈ 113 pieces per hour. This is the bottleneck of the whole line, and the 111 pieces of the fixed rule sit right against this limit. With dynamic dispatching, operator 7 is sent to hem sleeves whenever closing side seams is not busy, and the bottleneck is shared. If the 10 operators’ time were allocated optimally according to their skills, linear programming gives a theoretical limit for this line of about 130 pieces per hour; the 124–126 pieces of dynamic dispatching are already close to that limit, and the remaining gap comes from queuing and work-time variation.

The difference is even larger when someone leaves the line. Let operator 5 (efficiency 1.1 on setting sleeves) be away during the second hour. The 4-hour average output with fixed work centres falls to about 100 pieces/h, because in that hour only operator 4 is left setting sleeves, and only about 60 pieces can be made in that hour. With dynamic dispatching the average falls only to about 120 pieces/h, because operators 6 and 10 can also set sleeves and the system passes the sleeve work to them. This is what hanger-system suppliers call “dynamic balancing”: no supervisor has to reschedule; the system adjusts automatically according to each person’s current queue. In this example the two dynamic rules differ little. Earliest finish takes efficiency into account while shortest queue only counts carriers; for products with large differences in efficiency or work time, the gap between them widens.

WIP limit　How many carriers should be on the line? The right of Fig. 12-5 shows how output and average throughput time vary with the WIP limit under the earliest-finish rule. With a very small limit, operators often have nothing to do and output is low; once the limit reaches about 20–25 carriers, output stops rising; beyond that, the extra carriers only queue on the spurs and the main rail, output stays the same, and the average throughput time keeps growing.

This is exactly Little’s law: in steady state,

\text{WIP} = \text{throughput} \times \text{average throughput time}

At constant output, WIP and throughput time are proportional. Check this with the simulation figures: with a limit of 40, output is about 124 pieces/h and the average throughput time about 1100 s, i.e. about 0.31 h; their product is about 38 pieces, in good agreement with the 40 carriers on the line. With the limit lowered to 20, output hardly changes and throughput time is halved. So the WIP limit should be set where the output curve has just levelled off, which corresponds to a little over two carriers per work centre on average; anything more just keeps garments on the line longer. The first page of Virtual lab 12-2 lets you find this point.

Results in practice　A comparative study at a factory in Bangladesh compared single-piece flow (41 operators) with the bundle system (42 operators) on the same men’s hooded sweatshirt: output per operator was 16.87 pieces per shift with single-piece flow against 15.79 with bundles, about 7% higher; WIP ratio was 21.72% against 32.40%; defects per hundred pieces were 6.91 against 8.46 (IEOM Bangladesh conference paper, 2023). These are data from one factory and one style and cannot simply be generalised, but they follow the same logic as the WIP-limit analysis in this section: when WIP comes down, throughput time becomes short, while the gain in output is comparatively modest.

12.6 Flexible cells and bucket-brigade production

The U-shaped cell　A flexible cell places the machines needed for a dozen or so operations close together in a U (Fig. 12-6), with the entrance and exit on the same side. Parts are handed directly from one machine to the next, and the WIP is only the few garments in operators’ hands and on the machines. Each operator can do several operations—a multi-skilled operator—and whoever is free helps with the next operation. With entrance and exit side by side, one person can handle both the first and the last operation with the shortest walk. A style change only requires rearranging these few machines, so the cell suits small orders of a few hundred pieces; the batch size that separates it from a long line is discussed in Section 29.5 of Chapter 29.

How should work be divided among the multi-skilled operators of a U-shaped cell? The most direct way is for an engineer to split the operations into segments by standard time, one segment per person. But people work at different speeds, and the same person differs between morning and afternoon, so segments divided by standard time always leave someone busy and someone waiting in practice. The following method needs no calculation and yet balances itself.

The bucket-brigade rule　The rule of bucket-brigade production is very simple. Each operator carries one garment forward, working on it. When the last operator finishes a garment, she walks back and takes over the garment from the person before her and carries on with it; that person in turn walks back and takes over the garment from the person before them; the first operator returns to the entrance and starts on a new set of cut parts. If a downstream operator catches up with the one in front, she can only follow and wait; she may not overtake.

Lay out the total work content of one garment as a line from 0 to 1, and let the speed of operator i be vᵢ (work completed per unit time). Bartholdi and Eisenstein proved that as long as the operators are ordered from slowest to fastest—slow upstream, fast downstream—the hand-off positions for each garment converge to a fixed set of values, and the work assigned to operator i is exactly proportional to her speed:

x_i=\frac{v_1+v_2+\cdots+v_i}{v_1+v_2+\cdots+v_n},\qquad \text{throughput}=v_1+v_2+\cdots+v_n

Here xᵢ is the position at which operator i hands the garment to operator i+1 (Bartholdi and Eisenstein, Operations Research, 1996). The meaning of the result is intuitive: after convergence, each person works for the same time T on every garment; in that time operator i completes vᵢT of work, and the segments add up to exactly one garment, so T = 1/(v₁ + … + vₙ) and the throughput is the sum of everyone’s speeds—nobody waits and nobody is idle.

Worked example　Three operators with speeds 0.5, 1.0 and 1.5. Ordered from slowest to fastest, the hand-off positions converge to 0.5/3 = 1/6 and 1.5/3 = 1/2 (Fig. 12-7, left); the three operators do 1/6, 1/3 and 1/2 of the total work respectively, and the throughput is 3.00 garments per unit time, equal to the sum of the three speeds. The figure shows that the hand-off positions still swing for the first few garments and settle by the fifth or sixth: even if the initial division is very uneven, the rule itself pulls it back to the balance point.

Reverse the order, from fastest to slowest, and the operator with speed 1.5 is furthest upstream; she soon catches up with the speed-1.0 operator in front and can only follow and wait, and the latter in turn catches up with the slowest operator. Throughput drops to about 1.5–1.6 garments, close to 3 × the slowest operator’s speed and about the same as fixed segments (in the model, several operators end up bunched behind the slowest one and hand off almost simultaneously; this idealised result comes from neglecting the walk-back time). Slowest-to-fastest is a sufficient condition for convergence; other orders do not necessarily converge, the hand-off positions may jump back and forth, and the throughput lies between the two cases—try it in Virtual lab 12-2. If the work is divided into fixed segments by standard time, each person doing 1/3, throughput is set by the slowest person: 3 × 0.5 = 1.50 garments.

The advantage of the bucket-brigade rule is that it does not need to know anyone’s speed. A new operator who is a little slow naturally gets a slightly shorter segment; an experienced hand who is in good form today naturally does a longer one. The supervisor need only order people roughly by speed and leave the rest to the rule. The method is also used in other work such as warehouse order picking (a review of self-balancing production lines).

The model makes three important simplifications. First, the walk-back time is neglected—the U layout is meant precisely to keep this walk as short as possible; when walk-back time is not negligible, throughput falls below the sum of the speeds. Second, it assumes each person works at the same speed along the whole line, whereas in reality someone may be good at overlocking but not at coverstitching; sewing operations are also tied to specific machines, so hand-off points can only fall between operations, not in the middle of one, and the hand-off positions converge only approximately. Third, when a downstream operator catches up with the one in front she can only wait; two people cannot share one garment. Even so, the conclusion “order from slowest to fastest” is still useful in real cells. In Virtual lab 29-1 of Chapter 29, a cell of 7 operators produces about 60 pieces per hour ordered from slowest to fastest, but only about 50 ordered from fastest to slowest.

12.7 Design notes and limits of the model

The three topics of this chapter share a common thread: the times of people and machines must be staggered against each other without leaving too much idle waiting. A few key points follow. The value of an automatic unit lies in its fixtures and program, not its sewing speed. To decide how many units one person should tend, first calculate the balance point N* = (a + m)/(a + w), then consider whether labour or machines are more expensive; when working near the balance point, first reduce the variation in the times. A hanger system should use dynamic dispatching so that operators who can do the same operation cover for one another, and the WIP limit should be set where output has just stopped rising; extra carriers only lengthen the throughput time. A cell of multi-skilled operators can balance itself with the bucket-brigade rule, provided they are ordered from slowest to fastest.

All the models in this chapter are illustrative. The multi-machine model assumes the operator circulates in a fixed order and ignores machine breakdowns, thread breaks and bobbin changes; real units also often have one person doing pressing or inspection at the same time. In the hanger simulation the work times, skill matrix, efficiencies, the 15 s spur transfer time and the 60 s circulation time are all illustrative values, and only the three most basic dispatching rules are considered; commercial systems also take into account delivery dates, style priorities and operators’ piece-rate earnings. The main-rail chain speed and carrier spacing serve only to show how capacity is calculated. The bucket-brigade model ignores walk-back time and machine constraints. Readers applying these conclusions should recalculate with the work times of the actual product and data from their own sewing room.

Shop-floor notes

  • To decide how many automatic units one person should tend, first time the loading, automatic and walking times with a stopwatch and calculate the balance point; if near the balance point the machines often stop and wait for the operator, improve the loading table and the preparation of parts first rather than adding machines.
  • If carriers keep piling up at one work centre in a hanger room, first check whether it is an operation only one person can do; train a second person or switch to dynamic dispatching.
  • If a hanger line has many carriers but output is not rising, lower the WIP limit; the throughput time will drop markedly.
  • Order the people in a U-shaped cell from slowest to fastest: newcomers on the entrance side, experienced operators on the exit side.
