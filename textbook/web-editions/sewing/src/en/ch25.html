Chapter 23 surveyed the materials and processes of the key parts of a sewing machine and worked through the case of a resonating arm; Chapter 24 shows how the two “most precise small parts”, the needle and the rotary hook, are made. This chapter deals with two other kinds of part: the largest one and the one most sensitive to shape. The housing is the largest part in the machine. The bores for the main shaft, hook shaft, needle bar, presser bar and feed shafts are all in it, so it fixes the relative position of every moving part. The cam is the part most sensitive to shape: its profile is the motion law of the follower, and a few microns of shape error become a sizeable acceleration error at speed. We first deal with the housing: material and blank, casting and ageing, datums and fixtures, boring in one set-up, CMM inspection. Then the cam: what it is used for and how its profile is defined, the route from turning to CNC cam grinding, how profile error affects the follower and how compensation grinding reduces it. 25.1 Opening: the housing fixes positions, the cam fixes motion

Two generic problems from the shop floor, written up from first principles (all figures illustrative).

Problem 1: fine at assembly, skipping stitches three months later To meet a delivery date, a plant skipped the ageing operation on a batch of grey-iron housings and sent the fettled castings straight to the machining centre. Machining and inspection were in order, the hook-point clearance was set to 0.07 mm at assembly, and the machines sewed well at the end-of-line test. Three months later customers began reporting skipped stitches and broken needles on this batch. On return, the hook-point clearance had changed by twenty or thirty microns, larger on some machines and smaller on others. Nothing was worn and no screw had loosened; the housing itself had moved. Residual stress in the casting relaxed slowly after machining, the arm bent by a few tens of microns, and the needle-bar bore went with it.

Problem 2: lift error in tolerance, yet noisy at speed A batch of looper cams for a chainstitch machine had already been compensation-ground and measured about 5 μm P-V lift error on the cam gauge, within the drawing limit. Fitted to the machine, they were noticeably noisy at 6000 r/min and the ball joint of the looper holder soon wore slack. A harmonic analysis of the measurement showed an order-24 component of about 2 μm, chatter left on the profile by grinder vibration. It contributes only a few microns of lift, but more than a fifth of the nominal peak acceleration.

The two problems match the two halves of this chapter. The housing is the part that “fixes positions” (Fig. 25-1): once the main-shaft, hook-shaft, needle-bar, presser-bar and feed-shaft bores are machined, they settle most of the links of the tolerance chain in Chapter 23. Their positions must be right when machined and must stay right for years. The cam is the part that “fixes motion”: each point on the profile corresponds to the follower position at one shaft angle, so shape error turns directly into motion error, and the effect of that motion error on the machine depends on its order and on speed, not only on its amplitude.

25.2 The housing: material and blank

Sewing-machine housings are almost always castings, because only casting can make such complex, hollow shapes economically. There are two common routes: grey-iron sand casting and aluminium die casting. Industrial lockstitch and overlock housings have traditionally been grey iron; household machines, some light industrial models and many covers and face plates are die-cast aluminium, and some industrial models also use aluminium housings to save weight. Worked example: thermal expansion of an aluminium housing After running for a while the housing warms up by 30 K (illustrative). A Ø20 mm bushing bore in an aluminium housing grows by 20 × 23×10⁻⁶ × 30 ≈ 13.8 μm, while the steel bushing grows by only 20 × 11.5×10⁻⁶ × 30 ≈ 6.9 μm, so the interference falls by about 6.9 μm. If the cold interference is only a dozen microns, the bushing may come loose and turn in its bore when hot. Bushing fits in aluminium housings must therefore be checked hot; common remedies are a larger interference, adhesive or an anti-rotation pin, or putting the critical bores in steel inserts cast into the die casting. Iron and steel expand at similar rates, so the problem is much smaller, one reason industrial machines favour cast iron.

25.2.1 Designing the casting

The shape of the housing is set first by the mechanisms, but the same function can be given different casting designs. A good one is easy to cast, easy to machine and does not distort. Look at the drawing together with the foundry and machining engineers; the right-hand side of Fig. 25-1 lists the main points:

  • Keep walls even. Thick sections cool slowly; the metal that solidifies last cannot be fed and leaves shrinkage cavities and porosity. Large differences in thickness also shrink unevenly and leave residual stress and distortion. Bosses and bearing seats that must be thick should be cored out or blended gradually, so that no isolated “hot spot” remains.
  • Fillets. Round every inside and outside corner. Sharp corners concentrate stress and the casting tends to crack there as it cools; sharp corners in the sand mould are also easily washed away by the metal, causing sand inclusions.
  • Draft. Walls perpendicular to the parting plane need a taper so the pattern can be drawn from the sand: commonly 1°–3° for sand moulds and less for die casting (follow the foundry standard; to be verified). Draft on machined faces is absorbed by the machining stock.
  • Ribs. Ribs raise the bending stiffness of the arm and column. Ribs are usually somewhat thinner than the main wall to avoid hot spots at intersections; the arm resonance in Section 23.6 was cured by ribs that raised the natural frequency from 190 Hz to about 217 Hz.
  • Core holes. Internal cavities are formed by sand cores, which need core prints for support, and the core sand has to be cleaned out through openings after pouring. These openings are plugged after machining. They must be planned at the design stage, or the cores cannot be supported and the sand cannot be removed.
  • Datums and clamping points. Provide flat, large enough rough-datum faces and clamping bosses, ideally close to the future finish datums, so that the first operation can locate reliably (Section 25.4).
  • Oil passages and sump. Sewing machines are lubricated from a sump through oil passages (Section 8.6). Passages can be cast or drilled; cast passages are hard to clean, drilled ones leave burrs where they intersect. Either way they must be cleanable and inspectable (Section 25.5).

25.3 Casting the housing, and its defects

Fig. 25-2 shows the main steps of the two routes, with the defects each step tends to leave in red.

Sand casting The pattern and core boxes are made from the part drawing: the pattern is larger than the part by the shrinkage allowance (roughly 1% linear for grey iron, depending on grade and design; to be verified), machined faces carry machining stock and vertical faces carry draft. Moulding sand is rammed around the pattern in a flask; removing the pattern leaves the mould cavity, and cores form the internal cavities. The cope and drag are closed and molten iron is poured through the sprue; it fills the cavity through the runners, and risers feed the regions that solidify last. When the casting has cooled sufficiently in the mould it is shaken out, the gates and risers are removed, the surface is shot-blasted and the core sand is cleaned out. Most of the carbon in grey iron precipitates as flake graphite; the expansion of the graphite during solidification offsets part of the shrinkage, which is why grey iron casts so well and suits complex thin-walled parts like housings.

Die casting Aluminium alloy is melted and degassed in a holding furnace, then driven by the shot plunger at high speed and pressure into a steel die. The cavity fills in tens of milliseconds, the metal solidifies under pressure, and the die opens to eject the part. Die castings are accurate, smooth, can be very thin-walled and are produced quickly. Their main problem is gas entrapped during the fast fill, which forms pores just under the skin; machining removes the skin and exposes them, and near an oil sump or passage they leak.

When a new pattern is proved or the process is changed, castings are X-rayed to check bearing seats and the sump for shrinkage and porosity; in series production they are sampled according to a sampling plan. Tightness of the sump and oil passages is checked by a leak test: the cavity is sealed, pressurised with air and the pressure drop over a set time is measured; too large a drop means a leak. The leak test is best done after machining, which may have opened sub-surface pores.

25.3.1 Why ageing cannot be skipped

As a casting cools, thin walls and the skin cool and contract first; thick sections and the interior cool later, and their later contraction is resisted by the metal that has already solidified. At room temperature the casting is left with self-balancing residual stresses, typically compressive in the skin and tensile inside, or compressive in thin sections and tensile in thick ones. As long as nothing changes, these stresses balance and the casting does not move. But machining removes material, or the stress slowly relaxes on its own; the balance is upset and the casting distorts to a new equilibrium. The first shows at once (the bore positions change when the fixture is released); the second appears over weeks or months, exactly as in Problem 1 of Section 25.1.

Ageing reduces the residual stress before machining. Grey iron is usually given a thermal ageing (stress-relief anneal): the casting is heated slowly, held for some hours and cooled slowly with the furnace, so the stress is released by local plastic flow and creep (temperature and time follow the material and the company process, commonly 500–600 °C; to be verified). There is also natural ageing (several months outdoors) and vibratory stress relief (vibrating the casting near resonance for a while), whose effect varies from part to part and must be verified by measurement. Important housings are often aged twice: after casting and again after rough machining, because removing a lot of material redistributes the stress.

Worked example: needle-bar bore drift from stress relaxation Treat the arm as a box-section cantilever from the column (illustrative values): length L = 250 mm, section 60 mm front-to-back by 70 mm high, 7 mm wall, grey-iron modulus E = 120 GPa. Suppose a 2 mm skin of the front wall carries an unbalanced residual stress σ (the back wall does not, which is what “unbalanced” means). Its resultant force and moment about the neutral axis are

\[ F=\sigma\,t\,h,\qquad M=F\left(\frac{B}{2}-\frac{t}{2}\right),\qquad \kappa=\frac{M}{EI},\qquad \delta=\frac{\kappa L^{2}}{2} \]

where t = 2 mm is the skin thickness, h = 70 mm the wall height, B = 60 mm the section width and I the second moment of the box section about the vertical axis (8.06×10⁵ mm⁴ here). If the stress is fully released, the arm takes curvature κ and the needle-bar bore at the end of the cantilever moves front-to-back by δ. With an unbalanced stress of σ = 40 MPa without ageing (illustrative), full release gives δ ≈ 52 μm. At room temperature the stress does not all go; assuming half of it is eventually released with a time constant of 3 months, the bore has drifted about 26 μm after a year (Fig. 25-3). After thermal ageing reduces the unbalanced stress to 10 MPa (illustrative), the same conditions give only about 6 μm after a year.

What does 26 μm mean? Section 23.3 gave the allowed hook-point clearance as 0.04–0.10 mm, i.e. 0.03 mm either side of the nominal 0.07 mm. Set to 0.07 mm at assembly, a drift of a few tens of microns eats most of that ±0.03 mm window; add the error from needle changes and skipped stitches are no surprise. Worse, this error arises in the customer’s hands, where assembly adjustment cannot absorb it. So ageing, an operation that “adds no dimension at all”, decides whether the housing keeps its accuracy. The example is only an order-of-magnitude estimate: the real residual-stress distribution must come from measurement (e.g. the hole-drilling method) or casting simulation, and how fast and how much it relaxes depends on temperature, vibration and stress level.

25.4 Machining the housing

25.4.1 Datums and location

Before machining a part we decide which of its faces will locate it; these are the locating datums. The first operation can only locate on as-cast faces, the rough datum; later operations locate on machined faces, the finish datums. The old rules still hold: choose as rough datum a flat as-cast face with the least stock and the most important relationship to key machined faces, and use it only once; make finish datums coincide with design datums where possible (coincident datums), and let as many operations as possible use the same datums (common datums).

A rigid body has six degrees of freedom. The basic locating scheme is 3-2-1 location (Fig. 25-4, left): three supports on the primary face remove one translation and two rotations, two points on the secondary face remove one translation and one rotation, and one stop on the tertiary face removes the last translation. Clamping forces must push toward the locators and must not be so large that they distort the thin-walled housing; otherwise a bore machined round and in position springs out of place when the clamps are released. In series production housings are often located on “one face and two pins”: bottom face A is milled flat and two widely spaced locating holes are drilled and reamed in it; every later operation then locates on that face and two pins (one round, one diamond), which gives common datums, reliable location and quick loading.

25.4.2 One set-up on a horizontal machining centre

The housing bores lie on several faces: main-shaft and hook-shaft bores enter from the two ends, needle-bar and presser-bar bores are at the top and bottom of the head, feed and feed-lift shaft bores are at the front and back of the bed. If the part were re-clamped for every face, each set-up would add a few to a few tens of microns of location error and the bore-to-bore positions would be hard to hold. The table of a horizontal machining centre (HMC) rotates and indexes about a vertical axis (the B axis): with one clamping, the table turns to 0°, 90°, 180° and 270° and the spindle machines the four sides in turn (Fig. 25-4, centre). The relative accuracy of the bores then depends only on the positioning and indexing accuracy of the machine, not on repeated clamping. This is what Section 23.2 meant by “boring the key shaft bores in one set-up on a machining centre”.

25.4.3 Holding coaxiality and parallelism

The main shaft runs in three bearing bores (one in the head, two in the column) that must be coaxial, and so must the hook-shaft bores. Bores on one axis can be machined in two ways (Fig. 25-4, right).

Boring through in one pass A boring bar long enough to reach is fed from one side through each bore in turn and machines them in the same pass, with a guide bush in the middle or at the far end if the bores are far apart. Coaxiality then depends only on the straightness of the feed and the stiffness of the bar, around 0.003–0.005 mm in our example. A long bar sags and chatters, so the length-to-diameter ratio and cutting data must be controlled, or a guide bush used.

Boring from both ends If the bar cannot reach the far end, the near bore is bored from one side, the table is turned 180° and the far bore is bored from the other side. In theory both are on one axis; in practice two errors are added: the error δc in locating the table’s centre of rotation (the bore offset after turning is twice this) and the indexing error Δθ times the distance L from the bore to the centre of rotation. With δc = 5 μm, Δθ = 5″ and L = 150 mm: 2 × 5 + 150 000 μm × 2.42×10⁻⁵ ≈ 10 + 3.6 ≈ 14 μm, more than boring through. So the housing should be designed with this in mind: bores on one axis should be equal or step down in diameter from one end to the other, so that a bar can pass through from one side.

Parallelism and squareness The main-shaft and hook-shaft bores must be parallel and at an accurate centre distance, which sets the timing-belt tension and the hook-to-needle relationship; the needle-bar bore must be square to the main-shaft bore. All of these are held by machining in one set-up and by the machine’s coordinate motions. The machining centre has errors of its own: thermal growth of spindle and slides makes bore spacing drift during the shift, so the machine is warmed up first and probed for compensation; and bores are semi-finished before finishing so that cutting forces and heat are steady.

25.4.5 CMM inspection and the inspection report

Geometric tolerances such as bore position, coaxiality and parallelism cannot be measured with calipers and plug gauges; they need a coordinate measuring machine (CMM). Roughly: place the housing on the CMM table, probe bottom face A, side B and end C and set up the part coordinate system according to the drawing’s datum system; probe each bore in two or three sections (say 8 points per section) and fit a cylinder to obtain its axis; then evaluate coaxiality, position, parallelism and centre distance as defined on the drawing. Once the measuring program is written, every housing of that model is measured automatically and the report is generated automatically (Table 25-4).

25.4.6 Worked example: an operational dimension chain

On the drawing, the distance from the hook-shaft axis to the bed top face is H0 = 32 ± 0.03 mm (illustrative), because the hook height and the throat-plate position are measured from the bed top face; that face is the design datum. But the housing is located on bottom face A during machining: operation 50 mills the bed top face from A, giving dimension A1 (bottom to top face); operation 70 bores the hook shaft from A, giving A2 (bottom to bore axis). The design dimension H0 is not produced directly; it is the difference of A1 and A2, the closing link of the chain: H0 = A1 − A2. This is a case of non-coincident datums, and the design dimension has to be converted into operational dimensions.

import math
H0, T0 = 32.0, 0.03      # design dimension: hook-shaft axis to bed top face (closing link), ±T0
A1, T1 = 115.0, 0.02     # operational dimension: face A to bed top face (milled), ±T1
A2 = A1 - H0                       # operational dimension: face A to hook-shaft axis (bored)
T2_ext = T0 - T1                   # worst case: T0 = T1 + T2
T2_rss = math.sqrt(T0**2 - T1**2)  # statistical (normal links, tolerance = ±3σ): T0² = T1² + T2²
print(A2, T2_ext, round(T2_rss, 4))   # 83.0  0.01  0.0224

Result: by worst case the boring dimension must be A2 = 83 ± 0.010 mm, three times tighter than the design’s ±0.03; the statistical method allows ±0.022 mm. A Monte Carlo check with 200 000 parts each gives about 99.99% in tolerance for the worst-case allocation and about 99.7% for the statistical one; if no conversion is made and ±0.03 is simply used for the boring, only about 98.7% pass. A better answer is to change the process: locate or probe on the bed top face when boring the hook shaft (coincident datums). Then A2 is H0 itself and the whole ±0.03 goes to the boring. That is the value of coincident datums: when datums do not coincide, the tolerance has to be shared among intermediate links and every link must be made more accurately. The script is in figsrc/ch25_calc.py; virtual lab 23-1 in Chapter 23 lets you practise the same idea on a longer chain.

25.5 Surface treatment and preparation for assembly

Between the end of machining and assembly there are a few jobs that look minor but directly affect service life and warranty claims.

Deburring Burrs are left where drilled oil passages break into bearing bores, at tapped-hole mouths and along milled edges. The worst burr is the one that falls off in the customer’s machine: a chip of cast iron a few hundred microns across in a main-shaft bushing scores the journal and blocks the oil passage. Burrs inside intersecting holes cannot be reached by hand; special deburring tools, brushes, high-pressure water or thermal deburring are used depending on part and volume.

Washing and cleanliness After machining, the cavities hold chips, residual sand, coolant and sludge. They are washed by spraying, immersion or ultrasonics, and the passages are blown dry. Sewing-machine oil passages are fine, so cleanliness should be controlled quantitatively: the particles flushed from the part are filtered and weighed, and the largest particles are sized under a microscope; limits are set on residual mass and maximum particle size per part. The automotive industry has a component-cleanliness standard (ISO 16232) whose methods can be borrowed; the limits themselves must be set by test for your own product (to be verified).

Oil passages Every passage is checked for being through, by blowing air and watching the exit or with an endoscope looking at intersections for burrs and sand; then plugs or steel balls are fitted as drawn, with a leak test where needed.

Painting and rust protection Unmachined surfaces of grey-iron housings are painted. A common practice is to prime and fill the fettled casting before machining and apply the top coat after machining, masking bearing bores, joint faces and tapped holes. Machined grey iron rusts quickly, so rust-preventive oil is applied between operations and before storage, especially in humid shops. Aluminium housings do not rust readily and are powder-coated or painted for appearance and protection. Paint-curing temperatures can also distort the housing, so finish boring is best done after curing, or the effect of curing on bore positions must be confirmed.

Shop-floor notes

  • Check stock and distortion of incoming castings; if stock is uneven, choose the rough datum for the first operation carefully, or later faces will not clean up.
  • Do not skip ageing; the second ageing after roughing matters especially for precision housings.
  • Clamp housings with a torque wrench in the prescribed sequence; too much force on thin walls distorts the bores after release, however round they were machined.
  • Warm up the machine before finish boring the bore system; put the first part, and the first part of every shift, on the CMM.
  • Burrs at oil-passage intersections are a common cause of seized machines in the field; deburring, washing, blowing out and endoscope checks belong in the process sheet.

25.6 Cams in sewing machines and their profiles

A cam turns the uniform rotation of a shaft into a reciprocating follower motion with a prescribed law. Its strength is that the motion law can be almost anything (dwell here, move fast there); its weakness is the higher pair at the contact, which wears and makes more noise than a linkage. Modern high-speed machines therefore use linkages wherever they can, yet cams are still everywhere (Table 25-5).

Motion laws Section 11.3 compared three common laws: simple harmonic (cosine acceleration), cycloidal (sine acceleration) and modified trapezoid. For a rise h completed in shaft angle β the peak acceleration is

\[ a_{\max}=C\,\frac{h\,\omega^{2}}{\beta^{2}} \]

where C is the law coefficient: about 4.93 for simple harmonic, 2π ≈ 6.28 for cycloidal and about 4.89 for the modified trapezoid. The worked example of this chapter is a looper cam for a chainstitch or coverstitch machine (illustrative): base radius r0 = 20 mm, roller radius rr = 6 mm, lift h = 6 mm, 150° rise, 30° outer dwell, 150° return, 30° inner dwell, modified trapezoid, at most 6000 r/min (ω = 628 rad/s). The peak acceleration comes out at about 1690 m/s².

The profile For a central translating roller follower, the path of the roller centre is the pitch curve, at a distance Rp(φ) = r0 + rr + s(φ) from the cam centre; offsetting the pitch curve inward along its normal by one roller radius gives the actual cam profile (the “inversion method”: hold the cam still and let the follower travel round it the other way). Design and manufacture both need two geometric quantities, the pressure angle α and the radius of curvature ρ of the profile:

\[ \tan\alpha=\frac{s'}{r_0+r_r+s},\qquad \rho_p=\frac{\left(R_p^{2}+R_p'^{2}\right)^{3/2}}{R_p^{2}+2R_p'^{2}-R_p R_p''},\qquad \rho=\rho_p-r_r \]

where s′ and s″ are the first and second derivatives of lift with respect to cam angle. A large pressure angle means large side load on the follower and risk of jamming; if the radius of curvature of the actual profile is negative (concave) or close to zero, cusps or interference appear, and a grinding wheel cannot get in. In the example the maximum pressure angle is about 9° and the smallest radius of curvature of the profile about 20 mm; the whole profile is convex.

Library entry C-CAM-DISC The WenQuest Parts and Robot Library already has a disc-cam entry, C-CAM-DISC (disc cam with translating roller follower). Its parameters are base radius, roller radius, lift, rise angle, outer dwell, return angle and cam thickness, with defaults of 40 mm, 8 mm, 20 mm, 120°, 60°, 120° and 10 mm. Its generator (generators/c_mech.py) computes the lift with the cosine-acceleration (simple harmonic) law, obtains the pitch curve by inversion and offsets it inward by the roller radius, just as above. The entry has no specification table (specs.csv) yet, and only the simple harmonic law; for this chapter we propose adding a “motion law” parameter (simple harmonic, cycloidal, modified trapezoid) and manufacturing fields (material, heat treatment, profile tolerance), as a proposal planned for the library.

25.7.1 Blank, turning and profile milling

Sewing-machine cams are small, so blanks are usually sawn from bar, larger ones forged. Carburised cams are typically made of a low-carbon alloy steel (such as 20CrMnTi), induction-hardened ones of a medium-carbon or medium-carbon alloy steel (such as 45 or 40Cr); the company drawing sets the grade. Turning first produces the bore and faces, the datums for everything that follows. The profile is milled on a CNC mill or machining centre with the cutter centre following an offset of the profile; grooved cams are milled with an end mill slightly narrower than the groove; one-offs, small batches or complex shapes can be wire-cut. The phase reference (keyway or pin hole) must be machined in the same set-up as the profile, or the cam will be out of phase on its shaft and the whole timing of Chapter 8 will shift.

25.7.2 Heat treatment and distortion

Cam and roller are in line contact with high contact stress and repeated rolling, so the profile surface must be hardened. Carburising gives a deep case and a tough core, but the whole part is held at high temperature for a long time and then quenched, so distortion is relatively large: typically the bore goes oval and the profile acquires second- and third-order shape errors. Induction hardening heats only the profile skin and distorts less, but the uniformity of the case depends on the inductor shape and the process. Either way the distortion must be ground off, so the stock left before heat treatment must exceed the distortion plus the set-up error, or some areas will not clean up and leave black or soft spots.

How much distortion does grinding remove? Here we need the idea of error replication. After heat treatment the profile has a shape error; where the stock is larger the grinding force is larger and wheel and part deflect more, where it is smaller they deflect less, so part of the previous error is “replicated” in the next. The replication coefficient ε is less than 1, and each additional pass reduces the shape error to ε times the previous one. The example uses ε = 0.25 (illustrative): after carburising the second-order error is 20 μm and the third-order 8 μm; one finish pass leaves 5 μm and 2 μm, two passes 1.25 μm and 0.5 μm. More passes mean more accuracy but longer cycle time and more wheel dressing, a trade-off between cost and accuracy.

25.7.3 CNC cam grinding

A CNC cam grinder has two interpolating axes: the C axis rotates the cam and the X axis moves the wheel in and out. For every cam angle the wheel centre moves to where the wheel is just tangent to the profile. This is the same geometry as a cam driving a roller follower, except that the “roller” is a wheel with a radius of tens to over a hundred millimetres: the path of the wheel centre is the actual profile offset outward along its normal by the wheel radius Rg, so the X stroke equals the cam lift.

As with the follower, the contact point between wheel and profile (the grinding point) generally does not lie on the line joining wheel centre and cam centre. In the example it is up to 8°–10° off that line in the middle of the rise and return (Fig. 25-6, centre); the bigger the wheel, the closer the offset approaches the “slope” of the profile itself. This has two consequences. First, the speed at which the grinding point moves over the cam surface is not constant: with the C axis at constant speed some places are ground more and others less, so better cam grinders vary the C-axis speed to keep the surface speed or the removal rate roughly constant. Second, the grinding point also moves back and forth over the wheel face, and uneven wheel wear transfers its own shape error to the profile.

Wheel-radius compensation Each dressing makes the wheel a little smaller. The CNC computes the X path from the wheel radius held in the controller, so after dressing the radius must be measured and updated; this is wheel-radius compensation. If the actual radius is smaller than the stored one by d and the program is not updated, the wheel is d further from the profile at every position and the part is left d oversize everywhere (along the normal). For a roller follower, d along the normal means d/cos α more lift:

\[ \Delta s(\varphi)=\frac{d}{\cos\alpha(\varphi)}\;\approx\; d\;+\;d\left(\frac{1}{\cos\alpha}-1\right) \]

The first term is a size error (a larger base circle); the second is a shape error appearing only where the pressure angle is large. With d = 0.2 mm the example gives a size error of about +201 μm but a shape error of only about 2.5 μm (Fig. 25-6, right). So forgetting to update the radius is mainly a size problem, making the cam oversize all round and the groove of a grooved cam narrower; the shape error is too small to reveal whether the radius is right, so the radius must be measured after every dressing as a rule. Conversely, if the wheel is merely advanced by d without recomputing the path, the error is about d(1 − cos γ), where γ is the angle between the normal at the grinding point and the line of centres; for the large wheel in the example γ is only about 1°, and with d = 0.05 mm this term is under 0.1 μm and negligible. One more limit on wheel radius: if the profile has concave portions, the wheel radius must be smaller than their smallest radius of curvature, or the wheel cannot reach them.

25.7.4 Profile measurement

The most direct way to measure a cam profile is to imitate its working conditions on a cam gauge: the cam is mounted on a precisely indexed arbor, a probe with the same radius as the follower roller presses on the profile, and the probe displacement is recorded every 0.5°–1° of rotation and compared with the nominal lift to give the lift-error curve. What is measured is then exactly the error the follower will see, with no conversion. Alternatively the profile can be scanned on a CMM and the lift computed by simulating the roller in software.

Three kinds of information are separated from the lift-error curve. First, the mean, i.e. the size error, showing whether the base circle is too large or too small. Second, the low orders (first, second, third), usually from eccentricity, heat-treatment distortion and set-up, which mainly affect lift, i.e. “positions” such as loop-catch timing and clearances. Third, the high orders, usually from grinder vibration, wheel unbalance and C–X following error; they are small in amplitude but large in their effect on acceleration. Evaluation commonly uses Fourier analysis to split the error into harmonics and, as in roundness measurement, evaluates only components up to a certain order (orders 1–50 in this chapter). The next section shows why the orders must be looked at separately.

25.8 Worked example: how profile error affects the follower

From lift error to acceleration error Write the lift error as a sum of harmonics:

\[ e(\varphi)=e_0+\sum_{k\ge 1} e_k\cos\left(k\varphi+\psi_k\right) \]

With the cam turning steadily at angular speed ω, φ = ωt, and the acceleration error of the follower is the second time derivative of e:

\[ \Delta a(t)=-\sum_{k\ge 1} e_k\,(k\omega)^{2}\cos\left(k\omega t+\psi_k\right) \]

The acceleration error due to harmonic k has amplitude ek(kω)²: proportional to the amplitude, to the square of the speed and to the square of the order. Table 25-7 gives the acceleration error per micron of lift error at 6000 r/min: the first order is negligible, while at order 24 one micron is worth 227 m/s².

A model of the error sources The example looper cam is made by the route of Table 25-6, and its as-ground lift error has four parts (all illustrative): a first-order error of 1 μm from set-up eccentricity; the residue of carburising distortion (20 μm second order, 8 μm third order) after replication through finish grinding, multiplied by 0.25 for one finish pass; one harmonic of machine error (by default order 6, 3 μm); and d/cos α when the wheel radius has not been updated (when updated, only a 2 μm measurement residue remains). Three criteria are used: size error within ±10 μm; lift shape error over orders 1–50 no more than 10 μm P-V; and peak acceleration error at maximum speed no more than 10% of the nominal peak acceleration, i.e. 169 m/s².

Compensation grinding Once the lift error is measured, it can be subtracted from the grinding path and the cam reground; this is compensation grinding. Compensation does not remove the error completely: the measurement has errors, the grinder executes the correction with errors, and the contact arc between wheel and profile has a finite width that acts as a low-pass filter, so higher orders are harder to correct. The example assumes one round removes ηk = 0.85 exp[−(k/20)²] of order k: 84% at order 2, 78% at order 6, only about 20% at order 24.

import numpy as np
from ch25_model import CAMS, error_curve, compensate, evaluate, n_allow   # figsrc/ch25_model.py

    r = evaluate(cam, law, ee, 6000)       # size, P-V, acceleration error (spectral 2nd derivative × ω²)
    print(name, round(r['size'], 1), round(r['pv'], 1), round(r['aemax']))

Reading the figure and table First, low-order errors show up mainly in the lift. The default case has 16.6 μm P-V, mostly the second- and third-order residue of heat-treatment distortion; they contribute little to acceleration, and of the 56 m/s² most comes from the 3 μm at order 6 (about 43 m/s²). Low orders are easy to correct: one more finish pass or one compensation round both pass, and after compensation the P-V is down to 3 μm. Second, high-order errors show up mainly in the acceleration. The 2 μm order-24 chatter adds less than 1 μm to the P-V (it overlaps the other components), but the acceleration error jumps from 56 to 469 m/s², nearly 28% of the nominal peak; one compensation round only brings it to 366 m/s². By the acceleration criterion the allowed maximum speed of this cam falls from 6000 to about 3600 r/min, and only to about 4080 r/min after compensation (Fig. 25-7, right). High-order error can only be removed in the process: balance and dress the wheel, raise the stiffness and damping of the grinder, add spark-out passes after finish grinding, and control the speed ripple of the C axis. Third, size depends on the right wheel radius. With the wheel 0.2 mm small and not updated, the size error is +201 μm; one compensation round corrects 85% of it in proportion, leaving 30 μm. Only with the radius updated and then compensation do all three criteria pass.

What it does to the machine What do these acceleration errors mean at the looper? Take 60 g as the mass of looper holder and looper referred to the follower (illustrative): an acceleration error of 469 m/s² is an additional inertia force of 28 N, acting at 24 × 100 Hz = 2400 Hz on the ball joint and the cam roller, which explains the noise and the fast ball-joint wear in Problem 2 of Section 25.1. By Hertzian line contact, the peak contact stress between cam and roller rises from about 349 MPa nominal to about 382 MPa (8 mm cam width, illustrative). For a take-up cam, follower acceleration error becomes fluctuating thread tension; for a feed-lift cam, it becomes feed-dog bounce. Different cams run at different speeds, so the same error matters differently; virtual lab 25-1 lets you switch to a take-up cam (2500 r/min max) or a feed-lift cam (5000 r/min) and compare.

Accepting cams on “maximum lift error” alone is not enough. Look at the harmonics as well: low orders decide whether positions are right, high orders whether the motion is smooth at speed. For high-speed cams, the drawing should set separate limits on high-order components, or specify the acceleration error at maximum speed directly.

25.10 Limits of the model

The models of this chapter are order-of-magnitude estimates. The ageing example treats the arm as a uniform cantilever and reduces the residual stress to one unbalanced resultant in the front-wall skin; the release fraction and time constant are assumed, and real residual-stress distributions must come from measurement or casting simulation. The operational dimension chain is a single three-link linear chain; real housing bore systems form spatial chains that also involve geometric tolerances and measurement error. The cam example uses a central translating roller follower, whereas real looper and take-up mechanisms mostly have oscillating followers with linkages that magnify or reduce motion, so errors must be scaled by the transmission ratio; the cam is treated as rigid and turning at constant speed, ignoring shaft torsional vibration, roller-to-profile clearance and elasticity, and the impact when a grooved cam changes flanks. The replication coefficient 0.25, the compensation efficiency 85%·exp[−(k/20)²] and the harmonic phases are illustrative and must be calibrated from measurements on your own grinder. Contact stress is estimated only by Hertzian line contact, without lubrication, roughness or the fatigue limit of the material. The acceleration criterion “10% of nominal peak” is illustrative too; real limits must come from tests of loop catching, thread tension and noise.

Shop-floor notes

  • Machine the cam’s phase reference (keyway, pin hole) in the same set-up as the profile; after fitting, check phase first, then lift.
  • Leave more stock before heat treatment than the distortion; after heat treatment grind bore and faces first, then grind the profile from them.
  • Measure and update the wheel radius after every dressing; check the size of the first part to confirm.
  • Accept high-speed cams on their harmonics, not only on maximum error; if high orders are large, check wheel balance, dressing and grinder vibration first.
  • Give compensation tables version numbers; measure and regenerate them after changing wheel or machine.
