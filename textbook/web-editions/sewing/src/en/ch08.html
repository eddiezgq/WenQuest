Chapters 3 to 7 each dealt with one mechanism: needle penetration, thread take-up, loop catching and feed, plus thread tension. Treating them separately made each one easier to explain, but on the machine they all hang on the same main shaft and are driven by the same motor. This has two consequences. First, when the timing of one mechanism changes, the margins of the others change with it: a mechanic retards the rotary hook by a few degrees, the skipped stitches may go away, but now the thread starts breaking. Second, the inertia forces of all the mechanisms grow with the square of the speed, and at high speed they add up to vibration and noise of the whole machine. This chapter brings together the worked examples of the previous chapters. It first draws the whole-machine timing diagram, lists the constraints that cross mechanism boundaries and works out the usable phase window of each mechanism; it then computes the total inertia force of the machine and the choice of counterweight; and it closes with lubrication and the upper limit on speed.

8.1 Opening: pull one thread and the whole machine moves

Things like this happen on the shop floor all the time. A lockstitch machine skips stitches on knit fabric; the mechanic retards the rotary hook by seven or eight degrees, the skipping becomes rarer, but before long the needle thread starts breaking. On another machine the speed is raised from 4000 r/min to 5000 r/min; the head visibly shakes, the table top hums, and the people nearby have to raise their voices to talk.

The first case shows that hook timing affects not only loop catching but also whether the take-up lever releases enough thread for the loop (Chapter 4). The second shows that inertia force is produced by all the parts of the machine together, and that raising the speed by 25% multiplies the inertia forces by 1.56. To understand both cases, the mechanisms have to be looked at together.

8.2 The whole-machine timing diagram

Fig. 8-1 plots the worked examples of Chapters 3–7 against one shaft-angle axis. At the top is the height of the needle point; below it the thread supplied by the take-up mechanism and the thread consumed by the loop; further down the angle of the hook point, then the height and horizontal position of the feed dog; at the bottom an illustrative curve of needle-thread tension. The vertical dashed lines mark the key instants within one stitch.

Going through these instants in order makes the course of one stitch clear. The needle bar is at top dead centre at 0°. The take-up lever is at its tightest at 55°; the stitch is set around this instant, and at the same time thread for the next stitch is drawn from the tension assembly. The needle point enters the fabric at 101.7°, the upper edge of the eye reaches the throat-plate surface at 114.2°, and the feed dog drops at the same moment. The needle bar reaches bottom dead centre at 180°, rises about 2 mm, and the hook point catches the loop at 206°. The take-up lever has released the most thread at 293°, while the loop passes around the bobbin case. The needle point leaves the fabric at 258.3°, and the feed dog rises above the throat plate at 325.8° and begins to push the fabric. After 293° the take-up lever starts drawing the needle thread back; the loop slips off the hook at about 341°, and the take-up lever then quickly pulls it tight, beginning the next stitch.

To see how the mechanisms share these 360°, one stitch can be drawn as a disc (Fig. 8-2). The outermost ring is the needle in the fabric; working inward come the hook point carrying the loop, the take-up lever releasing and taking up thread, and the feed dog gripping the fabric.

Three things stand out most clearly on the disc. First, the needle in the fabric (101.7°–258.3°) and the feed dog gripping the fabric (325.8°–114.2°) almost meet end to end, overlapping only by 12.5° between 101.7° and 114.2°, by which time the horizontal motion of the dog is essentially finished (Chapter 6); at 5000 r/min these 12.5° last only 0.42 ms. Second, the 135° during which the hook point carries the loop, from 206° to 341°, span the instant at which the needle leaves the fabric, and only after cast-off can the take-up lever set the stitch. Third, when the take-up lever is at its tightest at 55°, the feed dog is still pushing the fabric: the stitch is set while the fabric is moving. Whenever the phase of any mechanism is moved, one must go back to this disc and check whether it now collides with another mechanism.

8.3 Cross-mechanism timing constraints and phase windows

The previous chapters each gave their own criteria; taken together they form a set of constraints that cross mechanism boundaries.

The loop-catching instant of the rotary hook is set by the needle-bar rise of Chapter 5. In the service manual cited in Chapter 5, the rise for different sub-models lies between 1.6 and 2.5 mm; this chapter borrows that range as an illustrative allowable range, corresponding to a shaft angle of 203.2°–209.2°. The loop-catching instant also decides when the loop starts to demand thread in quantity, so it is tied to a criterion of Chapter 4 as well: from loop catching to cast-off, the thread released by the take-up lever must exceed the thread used by the loop by at least 1 mm.

The phase of the take-up mechanism is set by the mounting angle of the take-up crank on the main shaft. Chapter 4 gave three criteria, in which all slack is counted from the tightest point (55°): before the eye enters the fabric (about 109° for a fabric thickness of 1.5 mm), the slack released in advance by the take-up lever must not exceed the stroke of the check spring (10 mm in the worked example); after the eye enters the fabric and before loop catching, the thread released by the take-up lever must not fall noticeably short of what the needle uses (a shortfall of no more than 1 mm); and the margin from loop catching to cast-off must be no less than 1 mm.

Feed timing is set by Chapter 6: if the dog drops too late, the fabric is pushed while the needle is in it (movement no more than 0.05 mm); if the dog rises too early, the needle is not yet out of the fabric (this chapter requires the dog to rise at least 20° after the needle point leaves the fabric; illustrative value).

Moving the phase of each of the three mechanisms in turn, with the others kept at standard, gives the usable window of each mechanism (Fig. 8-3).

The window for hook timing is about −2.8° to +3.2°, with both ends set by the range of needle-bar rise (if the loop-catching window from the loop model of Chapter 5 is used instead, it is about −3.5° to +5°). Looking at the take-up margin alone, the margin drops below 1 mm only once loop catching is retarded by about 6.5°. So on the example machine, the loop-catching instant is limited first by loop catching itself, and the take-up margin still has a little reserve. That reserve is not large, however: each degree of retard in loop catching costs about 0.1–0.2 mm of margin. The mechanic at the start of this chapter retarded the hook by seven or eight degrees, which is exactly what used up the take-up margin.

The window for the take-up phase is about −3° to +6°, and the two ends have different causes. If the take-up crank is advanced, the take-up lever starts releasing thread earlier, and the slack released before the eye enters the fabric exceeds what the check spring can absorb (at −3° the slack reaches 10 mm). If the take-up crank is retarded, the take-up lever cannot release thread fast enough while the needle penetrates, and the shortfall exceeds 1 mm (1.13 mm at +7°). On a real machine the mounting angle of the take-up crank is fixed in the design and is not adjusted in the field; what this window tells the designer is that the phase error of the take-up mechanism must be held to within a few degrees.

The window for feed timing is very asymmetric. It can be retarded by only about +1°, because the standard timing already sits at the edge of “the fabric moves 0.05 mm while the needle is in it” (Chapter 6; 0.04 mm at standard timing). It can be advanced as far as −47°, being limited only when the dog rises less than 20° after the needle leaves the fabric. This matches what the instruction manual says: to prevent uneven feeding, the feed timing may be advanced (Chapter 6).

The three windows also show the order in which phases are fixed in design: first the needle-bar mechanism (the reference for all timing), then the loop-catching instant from the needle-bar rise, then the take-up mechanism from the loop-catching instant and the loop’s thread consumption, and finally the feed, fitted into the gap while the needle is out of the fabric. Work in the reverse order, and what was fixed earlier has to be redone.

8.4 Inertia forces of the whole machine

Chapter 3 calculated that the reciprocating inertia force of the needle bar at 5000 r/min is about 654 N at bottom dead centre. Several other parts in the machine also produce inertia forces: the needle-bar crank, crank pin and big end of the connecting rod rotate with the main shaft; the take-up lever moves in a complex plane motion; and the feed-dog carrier reciprocates back and forth and up and down. This chapter takes a set of illustrative masses: reciprocating mass of the needle bar 120 g (Chapter 3), rotating part of the crank 60 g, take-up lever 25 g (centre of mass at 45% of the way from the crank pin to the thread hole), feed-dog carrier 50 g. The take-up lever is light, but the link-type take-up of Chapter 4 moves violently: the maximum acceleration of its centre of mass is about 10.7 km/s², and its inertia force can reach around 270 N, which cannot be neglected.

Opposite the needle-bar crank sits a counterweight. Its centrifugal force has the same frequency as that of the crank pin and acts in the opposite direction. Following the analysis of Chapter 3, the counterweight should fully cancel the centrifugal force of the rotating mass and, in addition, cancel part of the first-order inertia force of the reciprocating mass. This chapter writes the size of the counterweight as “rotating mass + k × reciprocating mass”, where k is called the balance ratio: k = 0 balances only the rotating part; k = 1 cancels the whole first-order force of the reciprocating mass in the vertical direction, at the cost of creating a new unbalanced force of the same size in the horizontal direction.

Fig. 8-4 shows the vertical and horizontal components of the total inertia force at 5000 r/min and k = 0.45, together with the locus traced by the tip of the resultant force vector during one revolution.

At k = 0 the locus is stretched vertically, with a maximum resultant of about 582 N, mostly vertical; at k = 1 it is stretched horizontally, with a maximum resultant of about 609 N, mostly horizontal; at k = 0.45 the locus comes closest to a circle, and the maximum resultant falls to about 368 N. The left side of Fig. 8-5 shows how the maximum resultant varies with the balance ratio.

The best balance ratio is about 0.45. Counting only the needle bar and crank, the optimum k is about 0.6; adding the take-up lever lowers it to around 0.45. The peak inertia force of the take-up lever is about the same vertically and horizontally, but its first-order component leans toward the horizontal, so a larger counterweight, which shifts still more force into the horizontal direction, does more harm than good. The right side of Fig. 8-5 shows the harmonics of the two components at k = 0.45: vertically, about 234 N first order and about 119 N second order; horizontally, about 338 N first order; the third and fourth orders are all below a few tens of newtons. The vertical second-order component comes mainly from the needle bar (the λ cos 2φ term of Chapter 3), and a counterweight on the crank can do nothing about it.

Inertia force and speed　All inertia forces are proportional to the square of the speed. With the same k = 0.45, raising the speed from 5000 r/min to 6000 r/min increases the maximum resultant from about 368 N to about 530 N; lowering it to 4000 r/min leaves only about 236 N.

8.5 Vibration and noise

Inertia forces pass through the bearings into the housing, and through the housing into the table top and stand, causing vibration; the vibrating housing and table top radiate sound into the air, and that is noise. There are three ways to reduce vibration: reduce the mass of moving parts (hollow needle bar, lightweight take-up lever), choose the counterweight well, and fit elastic pads between the machine head and the table top to reduce the vibration transmitted to the table. The first two reduce inertia forces at the source; the third breaks the transmission path.

Noise is an important specification of an industrial sewing machine, and instruction manuals state it according to international standards. For one manufacturer’s standard lockstitch machine at 5000 sti/min, the A-weighted emission sound pressure level at the workstation is 83.5 dB and the A-weighted sound power level is 88.0 dB (both including an uncertainty of 2.5 dB), measured according to ISO 10821 and ISO 11204 (a manufacturer’s instruction manual). With dozens of such machines running at once in one workshop, noise control becomes a matter of occupational health and safety.

8.6 Lubrication

A lockstitch machine has many friction pairs running at high speed under alternating loads: the bearings of the main shaft and hook shaft, the needle bar and its bushing, the joints of the take-up mechanism and the links, and the rotary hook, which turns at twice the speed of the main shaft. The traditional solution is centralised lubrication (Fig. 8-6): an oil pan sits under the bed, and the hook shaft drives a small oil pump that sends oil through oil passages to the rotary hook and the bearings; surplus oil drains back into the pan.

The oil quantity for the rotary hook has to be adjusted separately. Too much oil, and oil is flung from the hook onto the fabric; too little, and the hook heats up, wears and gets noisier. The instruction manual does this by turning a screw on the front bushing of the hook shaft and checking with an oil-amount confirmation paper: immediately after the machine stops, the paper is held under the rotary hook and the width of the oil splash on it is examined. The suitable range given in the manual is about 0.5–1 mm for the light-material type and about 1–3 mm for the medium- and heavy-material types (the manual cited above).

Oil-stained fabric is one of the quality problems garment factories fear most. New-generation lockstitch machines therefore mostly use semi-dry or minimum-quantity lubrication, supplying oil only to a few points or only in tiny amounts; at the same time a servo motor drives the main shaft directly (direct drive), eliminating the belt and clutch. One manufacturer’s new-generation lockstitch machine, for example, uses a 400 W AC servo motor coupled directly to the main shaft, with a choice of semi-dry or minimum-quantity lubrication (a manufacturer’s product information). Chapter 4 mentioned that the slot cut in the face plate for the take-up lever must be designed so that oil is not flung out of it; the low-oil and oil-free rotary hooks mentioned in Chapter 5 rely on wear-resistant materials and surface treatments to depend less on lubricating oil. These materials and processes are discussed in Chapter 23.

8.7 The upper limit on speed

How fast a lockstitch machine can run depends on whichever mechanism reaches its limit first. The previous chapters have already given the individual limits: the inertia force of the needle bar (Chapter 3; about 654 N at bottom dead centre at 5000 r/min); the acceleration of the take-up lever’s thread hole (Chapter 4; about 30 km/s² for the link type), which determines the dynamic tension carried by the needle thread; the speed of the hook point (Chapter 5; about 14 m/s), which determines frictional heating between thread and hook; frictional heating of the needle in the fabric, which can soften synthetic thread (Chapter 7); and, from this chapter, the vibration and noise of the whole machine and whether lubrication can keep up.

The maximum speed given in the instruction manual depends on the subclass: 5500 sti/min for the standard type; 4000 sti/min for the light-, medium- and heavy-material types alike; and 3200 sti/min for the heavy-material type when the stitch length exceeds 5 mm (the manual cited above). The speed limit therefore does not come only from fabric thickness: the light-material type is also slower than the standard type, and the specific reasons for each model would have to be found in the design data. The lowest speed, for heavy material at long stitch lengths, can be partly explained from the earlier chapters of this book: heavy material calls for thicker needles, thicker thread and higher tension (Chapter 7), and the needle heats up more passing through thick material; a long stitch means a larger stroke and higher acceleration of the feed dog (Chapter 6). This is the book’s interpretation.

8.8 Limits of the model

The timing windows in this chapter were obtained by moving one mechanism at a time “with the other mechanisms kept at standard”. In real adjustment several quantities often change together, and the shape of the windows changes too (for example, if the take-up is retarded along with the hook, the margin can be preserved). The criteria for each mechanism come from the worked-example models of the previous chapters, and some of them, such as loop thread consumption and the feed criterion, contain illustrative parameters. The masses used in the inertia-force calculation are illustrative values, with the counterweight referred to the crank radius. Only the resultant force was computed, not the moments: the take-up lever is on the face-plate side of the head and the feed-dog carrier is under the bed, so their inertia forces act at different locations and produce couples that rock the head, which the counterweight on the crank cannot cancel. The rotating mass of the take-up crank itself and the rotary hook were not included either. Nor does the model consider the moments of inertia of the parts, the elasticity of the shafts and housing, or torsional vibration of the motor and belt. Real vibration also depends on the natural frequencies of the housing: when the speed approaches a resonance the vibration is markedly amplified, and this calls for measurement and finite-element analysis (Chapter 23).

Shop-floor notes

  • After adjusting hook timing, check at the same time that the take-up is working properly (thread breaks, loose stitches); after adjusting feed timing, check for needle drag and for uniform stitch length.
  • If the machine suddenly vibrates more, first check whether the fixing screws of the counterweight and the needle-bar crank have worked loose, then check the bearings and the belt.
  • Check the oil level in the oil pan regularly and confirm the hook oil quantity with the oil-amount confirmation paper as described in the manual; before sewing light-coloured fabric, run the machine and sew a test piece to make sure it does not throw oil.
  • Do not run the machine for long periods above the maximum speed specified in the manual; slow down further for heavy material and long stitch lengths.
