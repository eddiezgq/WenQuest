Model (same as the book’s model24.py): scores of good items follow N(0,1), those of defective items N(d′,1), where d′ is the detector’s discriminating power; an alarm is raised when the score exceeds the threshold t. Recall = fraction of defective items that raise an alarm; false-alarm rate = fraction of good items that raise an alarm; precision = fraction of alarms that are really defective. Cost per 1000 items = misses × cost of a miss + false alarms × cost of re-inspection. Default d′ = 3.53 (at threshold 1.88: recall 95%, false-alarm rate 3%); costs are worked-example values.

Model (same as the book’s model24.py): one indicator per machine per day (e.g. RMS vibration or motor current, normal value normalised to 1) plus noise (standard deviation 0.08); about half of the machines (89 in this example) start to degrade on some day between day 30 and day 150, the degradation grows exponentially and the machine fails when it reaches 1, 30 days later. An alarm is raised when the moving average exceeds the threshold (it can alarm again after falling back below the threshold). Alarms during healthy periods count as false alarms; the first alarm after degradation starts and before failure counts as a detection, with lead time = failure day − alarm day. The random seed is fixed; the data are a worked example.
