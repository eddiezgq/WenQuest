This is an optional chapter (★) and the last chapter of the book. Artificial intelligence changes quickly, so this chapter does not chase specific products; instead it teaches a way of judging an AI application: what data it uses, how it is evaluated, and where it goes wrong. All company sources cited are anonymised; detector parameters, costs, equipment signals and similar figures are worked-example values.

The preceding thirty chapters have taken us from a single stitch to an entire factory. This chapter takes up a question that cannot be avoided: how will artificial intelligence change the sewing machine and the apparel industry? In the press, AI can do anything; on the shop floor, AI often disappoints. The best way to see it clearly is to treat it like any other engineering technology and ask three questions: What data does it feed on? How do we judge whether it is doing well? Where will it go wrong?

31.1 Opening: what AI actually does in apparel making

Things become much clearer if AI applications in apparel making are sorted by what they do into four types (Fig. 31-1).

The first type is perception: recognising things in camera images and sensor signals — for example, vision inspection that finds skipped stitches, loose (floating) stitches, stains and holes; identifying the type and thickness of a fabric; tracking a fabric edge (Chapter 30). The second type is prediction: inferring the future from historical data — for example, predicting which style will sell, which order will be late, which machine is about to fail (the equipment data of Chapter 26 is its raw material). The third type is optimisation and generation: finding good solutions among a huge number of possibilities, or generating new ones — for example, marker making, production scheduling and work dispatching (Chapter 28), as well as generating design drawings and automatic pattern making. The fourth type is language assistants: reading documents, manuals and data, answering questions, drafting documents and explaining things to new workers.

Each of the four types has its own weak spot. Perception fears “never seen it before”: if the lighting changes, the style changes, or a defect appears that was not in the training data, it may fail to recognise it. Prediction fears “the world has changed”: with little data, or when operating conditions or the market change, predictions drift. Optimisation fears “the wrong objective”: if the objective only says “save fabric” and says nothing about delivery dates, the more precisely it is solved, the further off it goes. Language assistants fear “sounding fluent”: their answers read convincingly but are not necessarily correct, so in a factory they must cite their data sources, and any write operation must first be previewed and then confirmed by a person.

31.2 Design, pattern making and virtual samples

On the design side, generative models can produce style renderings from a text description or a sketch, letting designers quickly try many colourways and details. 3D virtual-sampling software “sews” the pattern pieces onto a virtual dress form and simulates the drape and wrinkling of the fabric, so that designers and customers can see the result on screen first and make fewer physical samples. The bending stiffness discussed in Chapter 30 is exactly one of the parameters such software needs to simulate fabric: if the parameter is wrong, the garment on screen will be stiffer or softer than the real one.

On the pattern-making side, the made-to-measure suit factory of Chapter 29 generates patterns automatically from body measurements; behind this are mainly mature rules and grading algorithms. Beyond the rules, AI can learn from large numbers of past orders how to correct for unusual body shapes. These tools save a great deal of sampling and pattern-revision time, but two boundaries must be clear. First, a virtual sample cannot replace a fitting: fabric properties, seam allowances and processes all make the real garment differ from the simulation. Second, generated designs raise questions of copyright and originality: training on other people’s work, or generating patterns similar to existing works, can both lead to disputes, and the designer remains responsible for the final work.

31.3 Marker making and production scheduling

Marker making (Chapter 28) is essentially a combinatorial optimisation problem: fit dozens of irregularly shaped garment pieces onto a length of fabric with as little waste as possible. The possible layouts are far too many to enumerate, so marker software has always used heuristic algorithms and search. Recent AI methods can produce good initial layouts faster, or learn from historical markers which pieces fit well together, but the final utilisation must still be verified by geometric calculation.

Scheduling and dispatching are similar. The line balancing of Chapter 28 uses dynamic programming to find the grouping, and the hanger system of Chapter 12 dispatches work dynamically according to which work stations are idle. These are “traditional” algorithms, and their strength is that the results can be explained and the constraints (operation precedence, machine types, head count) can be met strictly. AI is better suited to supplementing the parts that are hard to write as formulas, such as predicting the work content of a new style or predicting workers’ learning curves on a new style (Chapter 29). A sensible division of labour is: AI provides predictions and suggestions, the optimisation algorithm solves under explicit constraints, and a person makes the final decision.

31.4 Vision inspection

The confusion matrix　Vision inspection is one of the most common AI applications in apparel making: a camera photographs every cut piece or every stretch of seam, the model gives it a “looks defective” score, and an alarm is raised when the score exceeds a threshold. To evaluate it, the outcomes are sorted into four cells (Fig. 31-2): a defective item that raises an alarm (caught), a good item that raises an alarm (false alarm), a defective item that is passed (miss), and a good item that is passed. From these, three ratios are defined: the recall is the fraction of defective items that are caught; the false-alarm rate is the fraction of good items that raise an alarm; the precision is the fraction of alarms that really are defective items.

This chapter uses a simple model to show how these are related: the scores of good items follow a normal distribution with mean 0, the scores of defective items a normal distribution with mean d′; the larger d′, the better the detector separates the two. The worked example takes d′ = 3.53 and a threshold of 1.88, giving a recall of 95% and a false-alarm rate of 3%. Moving the threshold to the right gives fewer false alarms but more misses; moving it to the left does the opposite. Plotting the (false-alarm rate, recall) pair for every threshold and joining the points gives the ROC curve.

The base rate　A recall of 95% and a false-alarm rate of 3% sound very good. But put the detector in a workshop with a 2% defect rate: of 1000 items, 20 are defective and 19 of them are caught; of the 980 good items, 3% raise false alarms, which is 29 items. There are 48 alarms in total, of which only 19 are really defective, so the precision is about 39% (Fig. 31-3). The same detector has a precision of about 78% at a 10% defect rate, and only about 24% at a 1% defect rate. The detector itself has not changed at all; what has changed is the proportion of defective items among all products — this is the effect of the base rate.

The consequences on the shop floor are very practical: if workers find that six out of ten alarms are false, they gradually stop taking alarms seriously, and genuine defects slip through as well. So when evaluating an inspection model, do not look only at the “accuracy” the supplier quotes (at a 2% defect rate, a detector that always says “good” also has 98% accuracy). Look at what fraction of alarms are real at your own factory’s defect rate, and at how much misses and re-inspections each cost.

Setting the threshold　The threshold should be set by cost. Let the cost of one miss be C_m (rework or returns when the item reaches the next operation or the customer) and the cost of one false alarm be C_f (manual re-inspection). The cost per 1000 items is

C = 1000\left[\pi\,(1-\text{recall})\,C_m + (1-\pi)\,\text{false-alarm rate}\,C_f\right]

where π is the defect rate. The worked example takes C_m = 20 yuan and C_f = 0.5 yuan. At a 2% defect rate the cheapest threshold is about 1.82, costing about 34 yuan per 1000 items; at a 10% defect rate the cheapest threshold drops to about 1.34 (better to raise a few more alarms), and at a 1% defect rate it rises to about 2.02. Under this equal-variance normal model, the cheapest threshold is t* = d′/2 + ln[(1−π)C_f /(πC_m)]/d′: the fewer the defects or the higher the cost of a false alarm, the larger t*. Making precision and recall both high at the same time cannot be done by adjusting the threshold alone at a 2% defect rate and d′ = 3.53; the only way is to raise the detector’s discriminating power d′. At a 2% defect rate, achieving 80% precision and 90% recall requires raising d′ from 3.53 to about 3.9.

Data and style changes　d′ is not a fixed number; it depends on the training data. A change of fabric colour, a change of style, or a new type of defect can all lower d′. So once a vision inspection system goes live, false-alarm and missed samples must be collected continuously and the model retrained periodically; at a style change, validate first on a small batch. This is the same problem as the robots in Chapter 30 needing to be “recalibrated”: fabrics and styles vary endlessly, and the model must keep learning with them.

31.5 Predictive maintenance

Chapter 26 showed that networked sewing machines can send speed, stoppage reasons, motor current and other data to the factory’s data bus; Chapter 18 covered early failures and service life of electronic control systems. Combining the two gives predictive maintenance: spotting components that are degrading from the trend of the equipment data (for example, rotary-hook wear or bearing damage that makes vibration and motor current creep upwards), and scheduling repair before they fail.

The simplest approach is to set an alarm threshold on an indicator, but the signal is noisy: set the threshold low and there are many false alarms; set it high and the alarm comes late. A common improvement is to apply a moving average first to suppress the noise. This chapter uses a worked example to show the trade-off (Fig. 31-4): 200 machines over 180 days, with one normalised indicator per machine per day (1 when normal, noise standard deviation 0.08); about half of the machines (89 in this example) start to degrade on some day and fail 30 days later. Using only each day’s value with a threshold of 1.15, every degrading machine raises an alarm before failure, on average about 22 days ahead (but about 30% of these alarms occur in the first few days of degradation, when the signal has hardly changed yet — in reality the noise happened to cross the line, so the lead time is overestimated); however, there are about 0.85 false alarms per machine per month, which for a 200-machine workshop is about 170 a month. With a 3-day moving average and a threshold of 1.12, the average lead time is about 18.5 days and false alarms fall to about 0.09 per machine per month. A wider window can push false alarms even lower, but under the same requirement of “no more than 0.1 per machine per month” the average lead time actually falls slightly (about 17 days with a 7-day window).

Real equipment data is much more complicated: normal values differ between machine models and operating conditions, and machines degrade in different ways. The value of AI methods (such as anomaly-detection models) is that they learn what “normal” looks like from a combination of several signals, without hand-setting a threshold for every machine and every indicator. But they face the same trade-off, and must be evaluated in the same terms: how many days ahead, how many false alarms, how many machines missed.

31.6 Case study: how one lockstitch machine adapts automatically to different fabrics

The technical problem　 On a single garment, some places are one ply and others four or five plies (for example, at the yoke seam of jeans, where seams overlap); on the same line, sheer fabric may be sewn today and heavy wool tomorrow. On a traditional lockstitch machine the stitch length, tension and presser-foot pressure are fixed once set; when the fabric changes, the machine has to be stopped and readjusted, or the operator has to control the speed by feel. Where the thickness changes abruptly, uneven stitch length, skipped stitches, thread breaks and needle breaks are likely. Can the machine sense the change in fabric itself and adjust its parameters automatically?

Principle　The basic structure of this kind of “AI sewing machine” is a closed loop (Fig. 31-5): sense, recognise, adjust, and keep measuring after the adjustment. Sensing relies on sensors: the change in presser-foot height directly reflects the fabric thickness; the main-shaft motor current reflects the resistance as the needle penetrates the fabric (Chapter 13), and the load on the feed motor reflects the feeding resistance; some models also add a camera. Recognition means judging from these signals what has changed in the fabric: more plies, a cross seam, or a different fabric. Adjustment is carried out by the electronic feed, electronic tension and electronic presser foot of Chapter 14: changing the stitch length, presser-foot pressure, needle-thread tension and speed.

Published patent literature gives a concrete example. A patent held by one company (grant publication No. CN121496668B) proposes a control method for uniform stitch length: within each stitch, at the moment t1 when the feed dog emerges from the throat plate and lifts the material, and at a set moment t3 between t1 and the feed dog’s highest point, a thickness-sensing mechanism measures the material thickness, H1 and H3; from these two thicknesses the controller looks up a stitch-length correction and turns the stitch-length adjusting motor to the corresponding swing angle, so that the stitch length does not jump when the material becomes thicker or thinner (patent report (in Chinese)). The method itself is “measure + look up a table” — nothing mysterious. A brokerage’s weekly industry report relays that a company claims its new model can detect changes in fabric thickness tens of thousands of times per second and dynamically adjust thread tension, needle speed, presser-foot pressure and feed force; the report also claims that equipment running AI algorithms can raise efficiency by more than 30% (industry weekly (in Chinese)). Figures like these are corporate marketing and need to be checked by measurements in the factory.

Rules, look-up tables or learning?　From this book’s point of view, most of the work in “adapting automatically to the fabric” is sensors, actuators and control logic — the subject of Chapters 13 and 14; the “AI” lies mainly in the recognition step. If the fabric changes come in only a few kinds with clear patterns, rules and look-up tables are enough, and they are easy to verify; if many fabrics and operating conditions have to be distinguished from several signals, learned models have the advantage, but they need large amounts of labelled data, and their errors are harder to explain. Whatever the method, the same question must be answered: what happens if recognition is wrong? For example, if four plies are mistaken for two and the presser-foot pressure is reduced, the feed may slip. So adjustments need upper and lower limits, and critical actions need protection (the safety design of Chapter 18).

31.7 Make-to-order production and factory organisation

Chapter 29 discussed small-batch quick response: batches fall to one or two hundred pieces and the factory changes style several times a day. The role of AI here is to turn “make first, then sell” gradually into “sell first, then make”: demand forecasting decides how large the first order is, and when sales data comes back it decides whether to reorder; the scheduling system fits small orders into suitable cells; equipment networking (Chapter 26) and electronic parameters (Chapter 14) allow stitch length, tension and patterns to be downloaded with one click at a style change. The whole factory looks more and more like a data-driven system.

Language assistants have their place in such a factory. In the design of the WenQuest Digital Factory, the AI factory assistant can answer “Where is the bottleneck this week?” or “Which order will be late, and why?”; its answers come from the data bus and the historian and cite their data sources. The orders and purchase requisitions it drafts are only “proposals” and are executed only after a person confirms them. These two rules — cite the source, and preview before confirming — are the bottom line for using language assistants in a factory: they let people check the basis of what the AI says, and they keep responsibility with the person who makes the decision.

31.8 Jobs, skills and industrial shift

The impact of automation and AI on employment in the garment industry is a contested topic; here several views are set side by side.

One view holds that the impact will be large. Garment sewing is a labour-intensive industry and, in many countries and regions, an important source of jobs, especially for women. The Cambodia country brief (2017) in the International Labour Organization’s “ASEAN in Transformation” research series estimated that about 88% of textile, clothing and footwear jobs in Cambodia were at high risk of automation, including nearly 500 000 sewing-machine operators (estimated from the technical feasibility of automating each occupation; ILO, 2017). If sewing robots and AI inspection spread widely, low-skill jobs will decline, and regions that compete on low wages will be hit.

Another view holds that the impact will come much more slowly than expected. Its proponents point out that, as of the writing of this book, sewing robots are still held back by the physical properties of fabric and by payback on investment, and humanoid robots are still at the stage of single-operation trials (see Chapter 30); “at high risk” is an estimate of technical feasibility and does not mean that the jobs actually disappear. It is generally thought that automation will first replace steps that are easy to standardise — cutting, material handling, inspection and pressing — while sewing itself changes more slowly.

A third view looks at how jobs change rather than how many there are: machine operators need to be able to set electronic parameters and understand equipment alarms; mechanics need to understand electronic controls and networks; team leaders and IE staff need to be able to analyse the line with data; and factories need people who understand both sewing processes and data. Others focus on industrial shift: automation lowers the share of labour in total cost, which may bring some production back closer to the market to shorten lead times; it may also make it harder for regions with lower labour costs to attract investment on low wages alone.

Each of these views has its basis; the outcome depends on how fast the technology advances, on wages and policies in each place, and on how companies and society organise training and redeployment. For the readers of this book, one thing is fairly certain: people who understand mechanisms, electronic controls, processes and data will be at an advantage in most scenarios.

31.10 Limits of the model

The vision inspection model in this chapter assumes that the scores of both classes of product follow normal distributions with equal variance; the score distributions of real detectors are often skewed with long tails, and the discriminating power differs between defect types; the defect rates and costs are worked-example values. The predictive-maintenance example assumes that all machines have the same normal value, that the noise is independent, and that degradation grows exponentially; real equipment degrades in many ways, and there are also repairs, part replacements and changes in operating conditions. The section on AI sewing machines relies on published patents and corporate marketing material; actual performance needs to be verified by measurement. The section on jobs cites a 2017 estimate; readers should weigh it against the latest statistics.

Shop-floor notes

  • To evaluate an inspection model, calculate the precision at your own factory’s defect rate; don’t look only at “accuracy”.
  • Set the alarm threshold by the costs of misses and false alarms; with too many false alarms, workers stop trusting the alarms.
  • After a change of style or fabric, re-validate the vision model and the automatic parameter adjustment.
  • For predictive maintenance, watch three numbers together — lead time, false alarms and missed failures — and start with the components whose signals are clearest.
  • When using a language assistant, require it to cite its data sources; anything written into the system must be confirmed by a person.
