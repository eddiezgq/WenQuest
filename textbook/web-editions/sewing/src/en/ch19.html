This chapter is the backbone of Part VI (Chapters 19–22). It assumes Chapter 13 (motors, drives, sensors, needle positioning, solenoids), Chapter 14 (lockstitch control timing) and Chapter 18 (designing, building and testing the control box). Module parameters, prices and criteria in this chapter are illustrative; the sewing-control modules carry proposed IDs in the WenQuest parts library — they are planned for the library and are not in it yet.

Part V covered the principles of control: how a servo stops the needle, how long a solenoid takes to pull in, why the spindle slows down before trimming, and how a control box is cooled, protected against interference and burned in. A natural question follows: can we take those principles and build, with our own hands, an electronically controlled prototype that actually sews? Not a block diagram on paper, but real wiring, real firmware, real fabric under the foot — then round after round of testing and improvement until it is as dependable as an industrial product.

Part VI answers that question. This chapter lays the common foundation: one reference architecture in which the same controller board, the same classes of module and the same firmware framework make four prototypes — a lockstitch machine, an overlocker, a programmable pattern sewer and a scaled flat-knitting machine (together the WenQuest Sewing-Control prototyping platform, WQ-SC); the modules organised as entries in the WenQuest parts library; a procedure that goes from a requirements table to the modules to choose and generates the bill of materials, the wiring table and the firmware configuration. It then shows how the firmware schedules actions on spindle angle, how a prototype first “sews” on a computer, how it joins the digital factory, and which gates lie between a lab prototype and an industrial product. Chapter 20 builds the lockstitch and overlock prototypes, Chapter 21 the pattern sewer and the flat knitter, and Chapter 22 is devoted to automated testing; all of them rest on the definitions made here.

19.1 Opening: from a textbook to a prototype that sews

Picture a team of three or four students with one semester — about 12 weeks — to build an electronically controlled lockstitch prototype. They buy a lockstitch head with its clutch motor removed, add a servo motor, solenoids and a controller board, and must make it follow the pedal, trim, wipe, stop needle-up and lift the foot when the pedal is released, backtack automatically at the start and end of a seam, and reach 5000 r/min. Chapters 13 and 14 have explained how each of these actions should be done. The difficulties lie elsewhere: which motor and which solenoids, how big a power supply, whether the controller has enough inputs and outputs; where to start the firmware; and once the prototype runs, how to know that it is “done”, and how to know that the next change has not broken something else.

Experienced engineering teams handle this with a short, fast loop: build – test – change (Fig. 19-1, left). “Build” means assembling the system from the bill of materials and wiring table and loading the firmware configuration and initial event-table values. “Test” runs from unit tests and software-in-the-loop on a computer, through hardware-in-the-loop with the real controller board, to a bench with a real head; scripts run unattended and every result refers to one line of the requirements table. “Change” follows a fixed order: parameters first, then modules, mechanics last. Every round leaves data behind: test reports, firmware versions, parameter versions. Without that data the loop degenerates into trial and error, and after three rounds nobody can say which change did what.

The right of Fig. 19-1 is a 12-week plan. The first two weeks go to the requirements table and rapid configuration (Section 19.4), and the modules are ordered. In weeks two to five the team assembles and wires the machine as modules arrive while getting the firmware framework running in software-in-the-loop on a computer. In week six it reaches P0, “runs”: the spindle follows the pedal and stops needle-up, and the event table drives the solenoids. Week nine brings P1, “functionally complete”; the last weeks go to automated testing on the bench, iteration and the report. This pace is possible only because the modules need not be designed: the library holds ready-made modules with clear parameters and uniform interfaces, and the firmware framework is ready too. Preparing exactly that is the purpose of this chapter.

How does a lab prototype differ from an industrial product? Table 19-1 lists some differences. The gap is mostly not in function but in certainty: whether the same action is right every time under supply variations, temperature changes, part tolerances and ten years of wear — and whether that can be measured and demonstrated. The thermal design, EMC, burn-in and production testing of Chapter 18 close this gap; Section 19.8 arranges them along a route.

19.2 Reference architecture: one platform, four machines

A lockstitch machine, an overlocker, a pattern sewer and a flat knitter look very different, yet their control skeletons are the same: one “master” (the spindle or the carriage) sets the time, several actuators act at positions of the master, sensors report the state, a controller schedules everything in between and passes data on to the shop-floor systems. WQ-SC fixes this common skeleton in five layers, plus the mechanics underneath (Fig. 19-2).

Actuators and sensors. The spindle is driven by an AC permanent-magnet synchronous servo motor with a 2500-line incremental encoder — 10 000 counts per revolution after quadrature decoding, with an index (Z) pulse. Feed, X–Y, stitch cams and take-down use closed-loop steppers; trimming, wiping, backtacking, presser-foot lift and needle selectors use push-pull solenoids; clamps, feet and air blasts use pneumatic cylinders and solenoid valves. A Hall sensor reads the pedal, photoelectric sensors the fabric edge and thread breaks, proximity switches the home and limit positions, plus current and temperature sensing. Drives are servo drives (speed or torque mode, linked to the controller by CAN FD or RS-485), stepper drives (step/direction or bus), solenoid driver boards (low-side MOSFETs, boost plus chopped hold, Zener freewheeling; Chapter 13) and valve drivers. Real-time control is the WQ-SC controller board: a Cortex-M4F-class microcontroller at about 170 MHz with encoder-interface timers, high-resolution PWM and CAN FD, running an RTOS; for per-needle selection on the knitter a small FPGA can be added (Chapter 17). HMI and network is a gateway module (Ethernet or Wi-Fi, running an MQTT client and a web parameter page) and an optional 7-inch touch panel. On top is the digital-factory interface: machine messages go onto the bus with the WenQuest Digital Factory’s topics and envelope (Section 19.7).

Table 19-2 shows the modules each prototype uses. The controller, gateway, solenoid driver, power supply and firmware framework are fully shared; the differences are concentrated in what the master is, how many position axes there are and which actions the solenoids perform. That is what “one platform, four machines” means: once the lockstitch prototype works, the overlocker is a new requirements table, fewer solenoids and one more valve; the pattern sewer adds two stepper axes; the knitter swaps the spindle for the carriage as master.

Why spindle angle is the time base. Chapter 15 made the point once: spindle speed is not constant — slow at the start, fast in the middle, slow again before trimming — and load changes make it vary within a revolution. The trim solenoid, the backtack switch-over and the X–Y moves must line up with the position of the mechanisms (is the needle in the fabric, is the feed dog below the throat plate), not with a moment in time. Firmware written as “energise 8 ms after top dead centre” goes out of step as soon as the speed changes; written as “energise when the spindle reaches 205°”, the action always lines up with the mechanism whatever the speed. Time enters in one place only: actuators have delays, and a delay t at speed n corresponds to 6·n·t degrees of spindle angle (Chapter 13), so every actuator that acts while the spindle turns must be checked for that angle at its operating speed. The knitter has no spindle and its master is the carriage position, but the reasoning is identical: selectors fire by needle position, not by time (Chapter 17).

19.3 Control modules in the parts library

Rapid configuration needs a library whose parameters are clear and searchable. The WenQuest Parts and Robot Library is shared by the courses and the digital factory and currently has four parts: A, standard parts (such as A-BRG-DG deep-groove ball bearings, A-BSC-SFU ball screws, A-LGD-RAIL linear guideways, A-PUL-HTD timing pulleys, A-CPL-JAW jaw couplings, A-SPR-CMP compression springs); B, complete robots; C, mechanisms (such as C-CAM-DISC disc cams, C-LNK-SLIDER slider-cranks, C-BLT-BELT belt drives, C-SCN-LEAD lead screws); and D, vendor devices (currently robot actuators and sensors such as D-ACT-ROBOTIS-X and D-LDR-SICK-TIM).

Entry format. Each entry is a directory. entry.yaml holds the identity (ID, kind, Chinese and English names, category, tags, standards), the parameter definitions, sources and licence, teaching notes and factory links; specs.csv is the specification table, one row per size. The parameter definitions are what matter: every parameter has a key, Chinese and English names, a unit and a role. There are four roles: key distinguishes the sizes (nominal diameter and lead of a ball screw), dim is an outline or mounting dimension (for 3D models and installation drawings), perf is a performance parameter (dynamic load rating, torque, speed — what selection criteria compare), and info is descriptive (a catalogue model number). In A-BSC-SFU, for instance, d0_mm and Ph_mm are keys, nut diameter and flange dimensions are dims, Ca_N and C0a_N are perf; the row for size 16x10 gives a nominal diameter of 16 mm, a lead of 10 mm, a ball diameter of 3.175 mm and a dynamic load rating of 10 817 N, with the catalogue it came from. An entry must pass the library’s schema check (schema/entry.v1.json) before it is published.

Sewing-control modules proposed for the library. The sewing-control modules are not in the library yet. Table 19-3 gives the proposed IDs and key parameters used in this book — all are proposed IDs, planned for the library. IDs follow the library’s “part-category-name” rule, and sizes are distinguished by a suffix: D-MOT-PMSM/400 is the 400 W size. A few entries in the table go beyond the usual catalogue parameters because selection calculations cannot do without them: the motor torque constant kt_NmA (to turn torque into current), the drive’s braking-energy capacity brake_energy_J (how much energy the DC link can absorb in an emergency stop; Chapter 13), the stepper’s corner speed corner_speed_rpm (where the torque–speed curve starts to fall; Chapter 13), and the solenoid hold current hold_A together with pull-in and release times t_on_ms, t_off_ms measured with the standard drive (24 V boost, 48 V Zener freewheeling). The last few are not intrinsic to the component but to the combination “module + this platform’s driver board”, so the entry must state the test conditions.

Mechanical parts refer to existing entries. The prototypes’ mechanics use existing library entries wherever possible: A-LGD-RAIL (e.g. HGH15CA), A-PUL-HTD (e.g. 3M-24-15, pitch diameter 22.918 mm) or A-BSC-SFU (e.g. 16x10) for the X–Y table and the knitting carriage, A-CPL-JAW between motor and spindle, A-BRG-DG for bearings, C-CAM-DISC for cam analysis. Their dimensions and load ratings come straight from the library’s tables. What the library lacks must be said just as plainly: timing belts themselves are not in the library and are bought out; the smallest jaw coupling in the library, A-CPL-JAW/LM1, takes 12–25 mm shafts and has an inertia of 2×10⁻⁴ kg·m², which is large for a small screw shaft (Section 19.4 shows the consequence).

19.4 Rapid configuration: from requirements to BOM

Rapid configuration is a fixed pipeline (Fig. 19-3): write the requirements table; use the formulas of Chapters 13–17 to compute the criteria each class of module must meet; filter the library on perf parameters and take, in each class, the cheapest size that meets every criterion; generate the bill of materials (BOM), the wiring table (I/O map) and the firmware configuration automatically; verify on the bench; and feed the test results back into the requirements or the criteria. Below, the lockstitch prototype goes through it once. All numbers are computed by figsrc/ch19_calc.py; Virtual lab 19-1 uses the same model and data.

19.4.2 Calculation

Spindle torque. Accelerating from rest to nmax takes the total inertia times the angular acceleration, plus the Coulomb friction Tf = 0.15 N·m (Chapter 13):

\[ T_{acc}=\left(J_{load}+J_{rotor}\right)\frac{2\pi n_{max}/60}{t_{acc}}+T_f=6\times10^{-4}\times\frac{523.6}{0.15}+0.15\approx 2.24\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \]

The angular acceleration is about 3491 rad/s². The criterion is Tacc ≤ 0.8 Tpeak, leaving 20 % for low supply voltage and for the angle-periodic inertia torque of Chapter 8. Three more criteria: the top speed must not exceed the motor’s maximum speed; the ratio of load inertia to rotor inertia must not exceed 10 (an illustrative value for a rigidly coupled direct drive; too large a ratio makes the velocity loop hard to tune — the lesson of doubling the inertia in Chapter 13); and the equivalent torque of the intermittent duty must not exceed the rated torque. The last one uses the duty of Chapter 18: 30 starts per minute, each with an acceleration and a deceleration of tacc, so acceleration and deceleration take 30 × 2 × 0.15 / 60 = 15 % of the time and steady sewing 35 %, with a steady load of 0.35 N·m (illustrative):

\[ T_{rms}=\sqrt{0.15\times2.24^2+0.35\times0.35^2}\approx0.89\ \mathrm{N\cdot m} \]

Drive. Current is torque divided by the torque constant: 0.89 / 0.5 ≈ 1.79 A continuous and 2.24 / 0.5 ≈ 4.49 A during acceleration. In an emergency stop from 5000 r/min the kinetic energy ½Jω² is about 82.2 J; with 70 % returned to the DC link (Chapter 13) that is about 57.6 J, which the drive’s DC-link capacitor and braking resistor must absorb. Table 19-5 gives the results for three motor sizes: the 250 W motor lacks peak torque and its equivalent torque is too high; the 550 W motor meets everything but costs more; the 400 W motor meets every criterion and is the cheapest that does. Its peak torque is 3.2 N·m, while the torque limit used when tuning the servo in Chapter 13 was 2.5 N·m; acceleration needs only 2.24 N·m, so the firmware configuration keeps the 2.5 N·m limit and the needle-positioning parameters of Chapters 13 and 20 need not change. With that limit the shortest acceleration time is 6×10⁻⁴ × 523.6 / (2.5 − 0.15) ≈ 0.134 s, as in Chapter 13.

Why not the cheaper 1.5 A drive? Its 1.5 A is below the 1.79 A continuous current, and the 57.6 J of braking energy is far above the 30 J it can absorb. The second point is easily overlooked: the prototype idles happily on the bench, then gets its handwheel, is run up to top speed, and trips on “over-voltage” at the first emergency stop (Chapter 13).

Solenoids. Each action has three criteria: enough stroke, enough force at the end of the stroke and, for actions taken while the spindle turns, an acceptable equivalent angle (Fig. 19-4, right). Trimming acts at 300 r/min. The M15 size in the library pulls in in 6.9 ms and releases in 5.1 ms (it is the solenoid of the Chapter 13 example), equivalent to 6 × 300 × 0.0069 ≈ 12.4° and 9.2°, both within 20°. S8 has the stroke but only 8 N, short of 12 N; L30 has the force and pulls in in 11 ms, 19.8°, right at the limit. Backtack switching at 1800 r/min with L30 takes 6 × 1800 × 0.011 ≈ 118.8°, inside the 201.6° idle angle (Chapter 14); M15 lacks the 8 mm stroke. Wiping and foot lift act after stopping, so only stroke and force count. Table 19-6 sums up.

With M15 on the trimmer, the trim speed can go up to 20 / (6 × 0.0069) ≈ 483 r/min, the same conclusion as in Chapter 13. The solenoid driver needs four channels, a peak current of at least the largest boost current 24 V / 4 Ω = 6 A (XL60) and a hold current of at least 1.5 A; D-DRV-SOL/4CH meets this exactly.

Power supply. The 24 V supply feeds the controller, gateway, sensors and solenoids. The budget assumes the worst case: 8 W of logic; all four solenoids holding at once, with hold power Ih²R totalling about 30.0 W; and boost, which lasts only about ten milliseconds and is partly supplied by the capacitors behind the supply, counted at half of the largest one (XL60, 24²/4 = 144 W), i.e. 72 W. That is about 110 W; with a factor of 1.2 it becomes 132 W, so the 150 W size is chosen. The spindle servo drive is fed directly from the 220 V mains and is not part of this budget.

I/O count. One encoder input (the servo drive’s A/B/Z encoder output goes to the controller’s encoder interface, which is where the firmware gets the spindle angle), four digital inputs (E-stop, head-tilt switch, backtack button, thread-break sensor), four digital outputs (the four solenoids, via the driver board), two analogue inputs (pedal, box temperature), and two CAN FD nodes (servo drive, gateway). Controller size S has 2 encoder inputs, 12 DI, 8 DO and 4 AI — enough.

For another machine type the pipeline stays the same and only the requirements change. The overlock prototype (6000 r/min, load inertia 3×10⁻⁴ kg·m²) also gets the 400 W motor and the 3 A drive, for an illustrative 2415 CNY. Raise it to 7000 r/min and two criteria turn ✗: the 400 W motor’s maximum speed is only 6000 r/min, and the braking energy 0.7 × ½ × 4×10⁻⁴ × 733² ≈ 75.2 J exceeds the 3 A drive’s 70 J. With the 550 W motor (total inertia 4.4×10⁻⁴ kg·m², braking energy about 82.8 J) the drive must also move up to the 5 A size, and the illustrative total rises to 2735 CNY. This is Task 2 of Virtual lab 19-1.

The pattern-sewer prototype (X–Y travel 300 × 200 mm, top speed 0.5 m/s, acceleration 25 m/s², moving masses 2 kg on X and 5 kg on Y) is more instructive. Both axes get timing belts: X a GT2-20-9 pulley with a NEMA 23 closed-loop stepper, Y a 3M-24-15 pulley with a NEMA 34 closed-loop stepper; the Y axis needs about 2.01 N·m at 417 r/min, and the lowest natural frequency of its belt axis is about 93 Hz. Put a 16x10 ball screw on Y instead and 0.5 m/s means 3000 r/min at the motor, with an angular acceleration of 2π × 25 / 0.01 ≈ 15 708 rad/s² — more than seven times that of the belt (about 2182 rad/s²). Multiplied by the inertia of rotor, screw and coupling, the required torque exceeds 4 N·m, while a stepper has only a few tenths of a newton-metre left at 3000 r/min. A pattern-sewer stitch is a few millimetres long and calls for short, highly accelerated moves, which suits a belt; a screw’s strengths — stiffness and positioning accuracy — suit short, slow, precise axes (Chapter 15). This is Task 3.

19.4.4 The AI selection assistant: use and rules

The WenQuest Digital Factory has an “AI selection” function (factory/digital/hub/select.py in the repository). The user states a need in one sentence, such as “X-axis timing belt, 24-tooth 3M pulley”; it finds the entries by keyword and filters sizes by the numbers in the sentence. It has three hard rules: pick only from the parts library; write every recommendation as “ID/size” together with the row of parameters it relied on and the source; and if nothing in the library fits, say so and invent no IDs or parameters. After an answer is generated, every ID in it is checked; IDs not in the library are removed from the result and listed separately. Without a language model configured, it answers by rules, again only from the library.

In rapid configuration the assistant is good for two things: turning a verbal need into numbers for the requirements table (“sewing jeans” prompts questions about top speed, fabric thickness and stitch length), and explaining, alongside the rule-based result, why something was chosen and what the alternatives are. The criteria calculations and the final choice are made by deterministic code, and every AI answer must trace back to a row in the library. Its most valuable sentence is often “the library has nothing suitable”: asked for “a coupling for a 16 mm screw with an inertia below 3×10⁻⁵ kg·m²”, the right answer is that the library has only the A-CPL-JAW jaw coupling, whose smallest size LM1 has 2×10⁻⁴ kg·m² and does not qualify — and that answer is itself a request to add an entry.

19.5 Firmware framework: angle-driven event scheduling

The firmware layers are those of Chapter 18: drivers, motion control, process, and interface and communication. WQ-SC organises the process layer around three things: turning encoder counts into angle, looking up the event table by angle, and holding the whole working cycle together with a state machine. All three are data-driven: the angles of actions are in the parameter table, so changing the head means changing parameters, not code.

From counts to angle. The spindle encoder gives 10 000 counts per revolution. The firmware uses 0.1° as its angle unit, 0–3599 per revolution, converted as angle = counts × 3600 / 10 000 = counts × 9 / 25 in integer arithmetic. At 300 r/min 0.1° lasts about 56 μs, at 5000 r/min about 3.3 μs, so events cannot be found by a task polling the angle; they use the compare function of the encoder timer: the firmware computes the count of the next event angle in advance and writes it to the compare register, and the count reaching it raises an interrupt.

The event table. One row per action: start angle, end angle, output, condition (Table 19-9, Fig. 19-5). Conditions let the same table express logic such as “only in the trim stitch” or “only during backtack stitches”. The trim solenoid is energised from 205.0° to 330.0°. The prototype’s head has a cam-driven trimmer: the solenoid only pushes the roller into the cam groove and the cam decides when the knife enters, so the solenoid is energised early; at 300 r/min its 6.9 ms pull-in is 12.4°, home by 217°, ahead of the cam-groove entry. With a head whose knife is driven directly, the start angle moves to 290°–300° as computed in Chapter 14, and the code does not change. The wiper is energised from 15° to 60° in the revolution after the trim; the backtack solenoid is set or reset at 114.2° (feed dog below the throat plate; Chapters 6 and 14) according to whether backtack stitches remain. All angles are initial values, to be checked against the timing chart of the actual head and calibrated on the prototype in Chapter 20.

Below are the event-table data structure and the scheduling in the angle interrupt. The interrupt only compares and sets outputs; it calls nothing that can block, and conditions only read variables maintained by the state machine.

/* ---- event table: one action per row, angles in 0.1 deg (0-3599) ---- */
typedef enum { EV_WINDOW, EV_LATCH } ev_type_t;
typedef struct {
    uint16_t start;      /* start angle */
    uint16_t end;        /* end angle; end < start means it wraps through 0 deg */
    uint8_t  out;        /* output: SOL_TRIM, SOL_WIPE, SOL_BT ... */
    uint8_t  cond;       /* condition: TRIM_REV0, TRIM_REV1, BT_LATCH ... */
    uint8_t  type;       /* EV_WINDOW: on inside the window; EV_LATCH: set/reset at start */
} evt_t;

/* is a inside (from, to] in the forward direction, possibly wrapping through 0? */
static int crossed(uint16_t from, uint16_t to, uint16_t a)
{
    return (from <= to) ? (a > from && a <= to) : (a > from || a <= to);
}

/* ---- encoder compare interrupt: entered when the count reaches the next event angle ---- */
static uint16_t last;                         /* angle handled last time */
void ENC_CMP_IRQHandler(void)
{
    uint16_t now = enc_angle_x10();           /* counts * 9 / 25 */
    if (now < last) rev_count++;              /* passed 0 deg: one more revolution */
    for (unsigned i = 0; i < P.n_evt; i++) {
        const evt_t *e = &P.evt[i];
        int c = cond_true(e->cond);           /* reads state variables only */
        if (e->type == EV_LATCH) {
            if (crossed(last, now, e->start)) sol_set(e->out, c);
        } else {
            if (c && crossed(last, now, e->start)) sol_set(e->out, 1);
            if (!c || crossed(last, now, e->end)) sol_set(e->out, 0);
        }
    }
    last = now;
    enc_set_compare(next_event_count(now));   /* schedule the count of the next event angle */
}

The state machine. A state machine manages the working cycle (Fig. 19-6): IDLE (needle up) → RUN (speed from the pedal) → SLOW (down to the trim speed) → TRIM (the trim stitch; the event table fires trim and wipe) → POSITION (position loop to needle-up) → IDLE. A fault detected in any state (over-current, encoder lost, time-out, E-stop, head tilted) leads to SAFE: all solenoids off, spindle coasting or braked, fault code logged. Only when the fault is cleared and the pedal is back in neutral does it return to IDLE, so the machine cannot start the moment it is reset (Chapter 18).

void sew_task(void)                           /* RTOS task, every 1 ms */
{
    if (fault_pending() && state != SAFE) enter_safe();
    switch (state) {
    case IDLE:
        if (pedal() >= P.pedal_run) { pfl_down(); bt_start(P.bt_start_n); state = RUN; }
        break;
    case RUN:                                 /* the event table runs stitch by stitch in the angle ISR */
        spindle_speed(pedal_to_rpm(pedal()));
        if (pedal() <= P.pedal_trim) { spindle_speed(P.n_trim); state = SLOW; }
        break;
    case SLOW:
        if (spindle_rpm() <= P.n_trim + 20 && enc_angle_x10() >= 1800) {
            rev_count = 0; state = TRIM;      /* TRIM_REV0 is true from this stitch on */
        }
        break;
    case TRIM:
        if (rev_count >= 1 && enc_angle_x10() >= P.pos_engage_x10) {   /* rev 1 at 25 deg */
            spindle_position(P.up_pos_x10); t0 = now_ms(); state = POSITION;
        }
        break;
    case POSITION:
        if (spindle_settled(5, 5)) { pfl_up_after(P.t_wipe_ms); state = IDLE; } /* +-0.5 deg, 5 ms */
        else if (now_ms() - t0 > P.t_pos_max_ms) fault_raise(F_POS_TIMEOUT);
        break;
    case SAFE:
        if (fault_cleared() && pedal_neutral()) state = IDLE;
        break;
    }
}
static void enter_safe(void) { sol_all_off(); spindle_free(); log_fault(); state = SAFE; }

Parameter table and versions. The event table, speeds, needle-stop angle and time-outs live in the parameter table, which carries a version (such as LS-P1-001) and a checksum and is kept in non-volatile memory; it is checked at power-up and, if corrupt, replaced by defaults with an alarm (Chapter 18). The firmware has its own version (such as 0.3.0); if the parameter-table format changes, its format number goes up and old parameters must be migrated. The fault-code table is versioned too. All three versions are reported to the digital factory: every test report of Chapter 22 must state which firmware and which parameters were tested, or the results cannot be compared.

19.6 Sewing on the computer first: software- and hardware-in-the-loop

Most of the firmware logic can be tested before the prototype is assembled. Testing has three levels that run the same test cases and change only the unit under test and the plant model (Fig. 19-7).

Software-in-the-loop (SIL). The process layer, state machine and event table are compiled as a PC program, the HAL is replaced by simulated drivers, and a machine model is attached. The models need not be written from scratch: this book’s virtual labs already contain models of servo needle positioning (Virtual lab 13-1), solenoid pull-in and release (13-2), trimming timing (14-1), the pattern sewer’s X–Y motion and needle-out window (Chapter 15) and knitting selection (Chapter 17); the prototype model of Chapter 20 joins several of them with one set of parameters. The simulation advances in 50 μs steps, tens of times faster than real time, and runs automatically on every commit (continuous integration), catching logic errors, wrong timing tables and parameters out of range.

Hardware-in-the-loop (HIL). Now the real controller board with the real firmware is used; the plant is still a model, but it runs on a real-time simulator that talks to the board with real electrical signals. Encoder emulation is the hardest part. At 5000 r/min the spindle produces 833 000 counts per second (A and B square waves of about 208 kHz each), while one 50 μs model step covers 1.5°, about 42 counts. If each step emitted its 42 pulses in one burst, the controller would see a staircase angle and events would bunch at certain moments. The simulator must therefore spread the counts evenly between steps at the speed given by the model, in the manner of direct digital synthesis, and output the index pulse at a fixed count each revolution. Solenoids can be real coils or R–L dummy loads (e.g. 8 Ω, 20–80 mH; Chapter 13); the simulator records the current and the model decides when the armature arrives. The servo drive is replaced by a drive model on CAN FD. HIL’s greatest value is fault injection: encoder loss, over-current, under-voltage, E-stop — hard or dangerous to reproduce on a real machine, but repeatable thousands of times overnight on the simulator to check that the firmware always enters SAFE with the right fault code.

Machine test bench. Finally come the real head, servo and solenoids, with a torque/speed transducer, an encoder log and a camera on the seam, to verify how mechanics and control work together. Acceptance criteria are measured here: needle-stop error ≤ ±1°, trimming success ≥ 99.5 % over 1000 trims, solenoid angle ≤ 20°. Scripts for all three levels are written in Python with pytest; they send commands and read measurements through the gateway, each test case corresponds to one requirement (REQ-…), and the results are written to a report automatically. Chapter 22 is devoted to this test system.

19.8 From prototype to industrial grade

Turning a prototype into an industrial product usually passes five stages — P0, P1, EVT, DVT, PVT — before mass production (Fig. 19-8). Each stage has its own goal, and the exit criteria are pass rates of automated tests and measured indicators, not “everyone feels it is about ready”.

P0, runs: one unit; the spindle follows the pedal and stops needle-up, the event table drives the solenoids, all SIL tests pass; exit criteria can be relaxed, e.g. needle-stop error ≤ ±3° and HIL smoke tests passing. P1, complete: all actions and networking present, every requirement has a test case, the bench runs unattended; exit criteria are ≥ 95 % of cases passing, needle stop ≤ ±1°, trimming ≥ 99.5 % over 1000 trims. A lab prototype stops at P1; both prototypes of Chapters 20 and 21 aim at P1.

EVT, engineering validation: the modules are merged into one control board in an enclosure, 3–5 units are built, thermal, supply and preliminary EMC are measured, and the machine runs 4 h at top speed; exit criteria are all requirement cases passing and an acceptable temperature rise. DVT, design validation: the design is frozen, 10–20 units are built, EMC, electrical safety, environmental and life tests are run and certification samples are submitted; exit criteria are passing the standard tests and no critical failure in the life test. PVT, production validation: tens to hundreds of units are built on the line with production tooling, each passing EOL test and burn-in, with data flowing back to the digital factory; exit criteria are an EOL first-pass yield on target (e.g. ≥ 98 %, illustrative) and every repair cause closed.

Going from a modular prototype to a product requires four main kinds of additional work (Table 19-11), most of it covered in Chapter 18. Note that this work does not begin at DVT: supply, grounding and cable screening should follow product practice from P1, or the EVT EMC pre-scan will overturn much of the design; and requirements and test cases need IDs from P0, or at PVT nobody can show that every requirement was tested.

19.9 Design notes and limits of the model

The main points: the four prototypes share one five-layer architecture and differ in the combination of master and actuators; actions are timed on spindle angle (carriage position for the knitter), and actuator delays are checked as angles of 6·n·t; selection starts from a requirements table of numbers and takes the cheapest qualifying size from the library, with BOM, wiring table and firmware configuration generated from one set of data; the firmware is organised as event table plus state machine plus HAL, so a new head means new parameters and a new module means a new driver file; firmware, parameters and BOM are versioned and reported by the machine; testing proceeds through unit, SIL, HIL and bench, each case tied to a requirement; and the road from prototype to product is staged, with each stage’s exit criteria written as indicators.

The models have clear limits. Spindle sizing considers only one acceleration from rest to top speed; the angle-periodic load torque of Chapter 8 is not computed but covered loosely by the 0.8 × peak margin. The start rate and steady load of the intermittent duty are illustrative and must come from dynamometer data in practice. The stepper torque–speed curve is approximated by a broken line (constant torque below the corner speed, falling as 1/n above it); real curves depend on drive voltage, microstepping and resonance and must be taken from the vendor. Belt stiffness is taken as EA proportional to width, and the natural frequency is checked only with the carriage at mid-travel. Solenoid pull-in and release times are for “module + this platform’s driver board” at rated voltage; they lengthen with low voltage and hot coils (Chapters 13 and 14), so selection should use the worst case. Counting half the boost power in the supply budget is a rough allowance for the capacitors. All module parameters and prices are illustrative and the proposed IDs are not yet in the library; when they are added, their data sources must be stated according to the library’s rules.

Shop-floor notes

  • If the prototype trips on over-voltage at its first emergency stop, check the braking energy against the drive’s capacity first, then whether a heavier handwheel has been fitted.
  • If trimming is erratic, first check whether the event-table version changed or the trim speed was raised, then the solenoid supply voltage; parameter and firmware versions must match the test reports.
  • After changing the head, change the event angles and the needle-stop angle in the parameter table and recalibrate on the bench; do not change the code.
  • When the controller’s I/O is just used up, keep one or two spare or add the expansion board at once; every iteration of a prototype seems to add a sensor.
  • When the selection assistant says “not in the library”, do not invent a model number for the BOM; record it as a request for a new entry and complete the entry from a catalogue.
