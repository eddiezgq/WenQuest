Chapter 5The Hook Mechanism

As Chapter 2 explained, a lockstitch succeeds or fails at the instant of loop catch: the hook point must sweep past the scarf side of the needle exactly when the loop has opened, and catch it. This chapter is devoted to the mechanism responsible: the hook mechanism. We first look at the kinds of loop-catching device, why industrial lockstitch machines all use the full rotary hook, and why it must turn twice as fast as the main shaft. We then break “catching the loop reliably” into three adjustable quantities — loop-catch timing, needle-bar height and the clearance between hook point and needle — and use an illustrative loop model to draw the “window” within which the loop is caught, showing why the three must be adjusted together. Finally, in the virtual lab, you will set a badly adjusted machine right yourself.

5.1 Opening: skipped stitches

Skipped stitches are the most common fault on a lockstitch machine: a stitch is suddenly missing from the seam, the needle thread lies in a long span on the top of the fabric, the bobbin thread runs straight across on the underside, and the two threads do not interlace at that stitch. In mild cases this spoils the appearance; in bad ones the whole seam opens at a pull. On the shop floor skipped stitches have many causes: switching to a thin, elastic knitted fabric, changing to a needle of a different size, a blunt or crookedly installed needle, or a thread that does not match the needle can all produce them. But what mechanics check, and adjust, most often are three relationships between hook and needle: when the hook point arrives, at what height it arrives, and how close it passes to the needle. If any one of these drifts out of range, the loop slips past outside the hook point. Two further faults accompany skipped stitches: a hook point too close to the needle scrapes the needle or even breaks it, and a hook point too close to the needle eye splits the needle thread and causes thread breaks.

5.2 The task of the hook mechanism

Within one stitch the hook mechanism has four things to do. First, catch: as the needle rises from its lowest point, the needle thread bulges into a loop on the scarf side, and the hook point must pass between needle and loop at this moment so that the loop hangs on the hook point. Second, enlarge: the hook point carries the loop on round, drawing it larger, while the take-up lever keeps paying out thread (Chapter 4). Third, pass around the bobbin case: the loop is drawn around the bobbin case holding the bobbin thread, so that the bobbin thread passes through the loop. Fourth, cast off: the loop slides off the bobbin case, the take-up lever draws it up, and the two threads interlace in the middle of the fabric. Chapter 4 calculated with a simplified model that loop catch occurs at 206° of the main shaft, the loop is largest when the hook point reaches its lowest position at about 296°, and the loop leaves the bobbin case at about 341°; over this interval the hook point turns about 270°.

Of these four tasks, the last three depend mainly on the shape of the hook and its coordination with the take-up mechanism, and seldom need adjustment once the design is fixed. The first, “catching”, is highly sensitive to position and timing and is the focus of this chapter.

5.3 Types of loop-catching device

By their motion, loop-catching devices fall into three classes (Fig. 5-1).

Oscillating shuttle　The shuttle holds the bobbin and swings back and forth in a curved race, once per stitch; the shuttle’s point catches the loop and carries it around itself. The oscillating shuttle is the form used in early domestic sewing machines. Because the shuttle and bobbin reciprocate together, the moving mass is large and the motion reverses twice every stitch, so the speed cannot be raised much.

Half rotary hook　The hook body no longer translates back and forth as a whole but turns back and forth about an axis, one round trip per stitch, through an angle of more than half a turn. It is lighter and handier than the oscillating shuttle and is still used in many domestic sewing machines.

Full rotary hook　The hook body rotates continuously in one direction, while the bobbin case holding the bobbin thread is held stationary by a positioning finger. With no reversal impact it can run at very high speed, which is why industrial lockstitch machines almost universally use it. As Chapter 1 explained, the continuously rotating hook was introduced in Wilson’s patents of 1851–1852; a popular account holds that the rotary hook won out precisely because it can run at higher speeds with less vibration (Wikipedia, “Rotary hook”). Full rotary hooks are further divided by axis direction into horizontal-axis and vertical-axis types: industrial lockstitch machines mostly use a horizontal axis, with the bobbin case inserted and removed from the front of the machine; vertical-axis hooks hold the bobbin lying flat and are common in domestic machines and some heavy-duty machines. For heavy fabrics and thick threads there are also larger “jumbo” hooks, whose bobbins hold more bobbin thread and need changing less often.

The same Wikipedia article also points out an inherent dilemma of the rotary hook: the bobbin thread must pass completely through the needle-thread loop, so either the bobbin must be very small or the take-up lever stroke must be very long. This is exactly why Chapter 4 found that the take-up lever must pay out nearly eighty millimetres of thread per stitch.

5.4 Why the hook turns twice as fast as the main shaft

In an industrial lockstitch machine the full rotary hook makes two turns for each turn of the main shaft, a speed ratio of 2:1 (Chapter 2). The timing from Chapter 4 shows why (Fig. 5-2).

From catching the loop to the loop leaving the bobbin case, the hook must turn about 270°. At a 2:1 ratio this occupies only 135° of the main shaft: loop catch at 206°, cast-off at 341°, and there are still over a hundred degrees before the needle point pierces the fabric for the next stitch (at 102° of the next stitch). The take-up lever has ample time to draw up the loop and set the stitch, and the feed dog feeds the fabric during this period too. The hook’s second turn carries no thread; it is an idle turn. At 1:1, the hook’s 270° would occupy 270° of the main shaft, and the loop would not leave until 476°, i.e. 116° of the next stitch — but the next needle has already pierced the fabric at 102°: the next stitch begins before the previous one has been set. With this catch-and-take-up timing, 1:1 simply does not leave enough time; the full rotary hook uses 2:1, trading an idle turn for sufficient take-up time.

A direct consequence is that the hook runs at twice the main-shaft speed. With the main shaft at 5000 r/min the hook turns at 10000 r/min; taking the radius of the hook point as 13.5 mm (worked-example value), the linear speed of the hook point is about

\[v = 2\pi R_h\,n_h = 2\pi \times 0.0135 \times \frac{10000}{60} \approx 14\ \mathrm{m/s}\]

This is the speed at which the hook point sweeps past the needle, less than 0.1 mm away from it.

5.5 The three adjustable quantities of loop catch

Fig. 5-3 shows the relationship between needle, hook point and loop at loop catch. On a lockstitch machine whose design is fixed, only three quantities can be adjusted in the field.

Loop-catch timing　This is expressed as how far the needle has risen from its lowest point when the hook point reaches the needle centreline, called the needle rise. As derived in Chapter 3, near bottom dead centre the rise is related to the main-shaft angle by

\[h(\delta) \approx \frac{r}{2}(1+\lambda)\,\delta^{2}\]

where δ is the angle by which the main shaft has passed bottom dead centre. In the worked example r = 15.5 mm and λ = 0.282; a rise of 2 mm gives δ ≈ 26°, i.e. loop catch at about 206° of the main shaft (206.1° by exact calculation). Because the curve is flat near bottom dead centre, expressing loop-catch timing as a rise in millimetres is more intuitive than as an angle; but note that a difference of only 5° of main-shaft angle changes the rise from 1.3 mm (201°) to 2.8 mm (211°), beyond the entire range in the manuals. Loop-catch timing is adjusted by loosening the hook’s set screws on the hook shaft and turning the hook; one degree of hook rotation corresponds to half a degree of the main shaft. The needle rise given in service manuals varies with model and fabric class; one model’s manual specifies 1.6 mm, 2.0 mm and 2.2–2.5 mm for its sub-models (A to D, roughly from light to heavy materials) (a manufacturer’s service manual). Broadly, the heavier the fabric and the thicker the thread, the larger the rise.

Needle-bar height　At the moment of loop catch, the lower edge of the hook point must be a little above the top of the needle eye, so that the hook point passes through the loop above the eye without catching the thread in the eye. The same manual requires the lower edge of the hook point to be 1.0 mm above the top of the eye. Needle-bar height is adjusted by loosening the needle-bar connecting stud (Chapter 3) and moving the needle bar up or down; this changes only the absolute height of the eye, not the motion law of the needle bar. Some instruction manuals give no millimetre values but use marks engraved on the needle bar: with the needle bar at its lowest position, one mark is aligned with the lower end of the lower needle-bar bushing to set the needle-bar height; when the bar has risen until another mark is aligned, the hook point is set to the needle centre to set the loop-catch timing (a manufacturer’s instruction manual).

Hook-to-needle clearance　As the hook point sweeps past, a small clearance must be left to the needle surface at the scarf. One model’s instruction manual requires 0.04–0.1 mm, and 0.06–0.17 mm for the H and L types of the same series (same instruction manual); the service manual above requires 0.1 mm. The clearance is adjusted by moving the hook along its axis. The hook also carries a needle guard, which touches the needle lightly to stop it from striking the hook point (same service manual); when sewing heavy fabric or crossing thick seams, the needle is easily deflected by the fabric, and this is when the needle guard proves its worth.

The clearance is set for a particular needle size. When a thicker needle is fitted, its surface moves towards the hook point by half the difference in diameters; with a finer needle it moves away. For example, changing from Nm 90 (diameter 0.9 mm) to Nm 110 (1.1 mm) moves the needle surface 0.1 mm closer to the hook point, so an original clearance of 0.08 mm becomes −0.02 mm and the hook point strikes the needle. Re-setting the clearance after changing needle size is therefore an important shop-floor rule.

5.6 The loop-catch window: why the three must be adjusted together

To show how the three quantities interact, we need to know how large the loop is at the moment of catch and where it lies. Loop formation is affected by many factors — the stiffness and twist of the thread, how the fabric grips the thread, the shape of the needle — and is hard to calculate accurately. This chapter uses an illustrative model: after the needle starts to rise, the loop bulges out on the scarf side; the bulge distance first increases with needle rise, is greatest at a rise of about 2.2 mm, and then gradually decreases as the rise increases further (the loop is lifted and closes up). Vertically, the loop starts at the top of the needle eye and extends upward by a height that increases with the rise, bulging most in the middle. The hook point catches the loop if, at the height of the hook point, the distance of the loop from the needle surface exceeds the clearance plus the thickness of the hook point:

\[w(h, y_b) > g + t_b\]

Here w is the distance of the loop from the needle surface at the hook-point height y_b, g is the clearance, and t_b is the thickness the hook point needs to pass through (taken as 0.15 mm in the model). In addition, a hook-point lower edge too close to the top of the eye (less than 0.2 mm in the model) will cut the thread in the eye, and a clearance down to zero means the hook strikes the needle.

Checking this model point by point over the plane “hook installation angle – needle-bar height” gives the loop-catch window of Fig. 5-4.

Three points stand out in Fig. 5-4.

First, the window is slanted. If loop catch is delayed (to the right), the needle eye has risen further by the time the hook point arrives, so the hook point is relatively lower with respect to the eye and quickly enters the purple “too close to the eye” zone; to compensate, the needle bar must be lowered (downward). Conversely, if loop catch is advanced, the eye has not yet risen into place and the hook point is relatively high, so the needle bar must be raised to bring the hook point back to the right height above the eye. Hook timing and needle-bar height therefore cannot be adjusted independently: change one, and the other usually has to follow. This is the reason why service manuals generally prescribe the order: loop-catch timing first, then needle-bar height, and clearance last.

Second, the window has a width. With the needle-bar height at standard, the window for woven fabric runs from a hook installation angle of about −7° to +10°, corresponding to a needle rise of about 1.5–2.8 mm, broadly in line with the manual’s range; in main-shaft angle this is only a little over eight degrees, less than 0.3 ms at 5000 r/min. The manual places the standard position in the middle of the window (the white dot), leaving a margin to absorb variations in loop size, needle deflection and wear of parts.

Third, the window for knitted fabric is narrower. Knitted fabric is elastic and grips the thread poorly, so the loop is small and unstable, and the dark green zone (margin above 0.15 mm) is clearly narrower in the figure. This is why knits suffer more skipped stitches. Common countermeasures in practice are: use ball-point needles and a thread suited to knits, set the clearance to the lower limit, and place the working point in the middle of the window; if necessary, use special needles or hooks designed for knitted fabrics.

5.7 After the catch: passing around the case, cast-off and lubrication

Once it has caught the loop, the hook must carry it around the bobbin case. The bobbin case (together with the bobbin-case holder) is held by the positioning finger and cannot rotate, so the thread has to pass through the gap between the positioning finger and the holder. Some hooks also have an opener, which nudges the bobbin-case holder slightly aside as the loop passes, to open a way through. The service manual requires that the needle thread jam neither between the opener and the bobbin-case base nor between the holder’s positioning lug and the throat-plate slot (the service manual above). If this gap is too small the thread jams, causing breaks, fraying or uneven stitches; if too large, the bobbin case rattles, making noise and leaving the loop-catch position unstable.

The hook rotates in its race at ten thousand revolutions per minute and must be reliably lubricated. On industrial lockstitch machines an oil pump usually delivers oil to the hook through the hook shaft, and the amount can be increased or decreased with an adjusting screw (the instruction manual above); too much oil soils the fabric, too little makes the hook run hot and wear. In recent years micro-lubricated and oil-free hooks have also appeared, using wear-resistant materials and surface treatments to reduce dependence on lubricating oil; these are discussed in Chapters 8 and 23.

5.8 Limits of the model

The loop model in this chapter is illustrative: the laws by which the bulge distance and height vary with needle rise, and the effective thickness of the hook point, are assumed only to make the interaction of the three quantities clear, and cannot be used to predict the actual window of a particular machine. A real loop also twists (depending on the twist direction of the thread), is affected by air and inertia at high speed, and the needle is deflected in the fabric. It should also be said that the model parameters were set with reference to the manuals’ ranges in the first place, so the “broad agreement” of the window in Fig. 5-4 with the manuals is partly built in and is not an independent validation. The reliable practice is still to adjust to the standard position according to the service manual, then sew test seams with the actual fabric, needle and thread, and fine-tune according to skipped stitches. In research, the loop at the instant of catch can be observed with a high-speed camera, or skipped-stitch rates can be counted at different settings to map the real loop-catch window.

Shop-floor notes

  • Order of hook adjustment: set the loop-catch timing (needle rise) first, then the needle-bar height, and the clearance last. After all three, turn the handwheel by hand to check again.
  • Always re-set the clearance after changing needle size; fitting a thicker needle without adjusting will at best scrape the needle, at worst strike and break it and damage the hook point.
  • For skipped stitches on knits, first change to a ball-point needle and check that the needle is installed straight, then set the clearance to the lower limit and check the loop-catch timing; do not simply keep raising or lowering the needle bar.
  • If you hear a metallic clicking at the hook, stop the machine and check the hook point and needle for damage; a burred hook point should be lightly dressed with an oilstone or replaced.
