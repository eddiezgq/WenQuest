A shirt has seven or eight buttonholes, seven or eight buttons, several bartacks, and perhaps a decorative zigzag line at the collar and cuffs. Each of these jobs is done by a dedicated machine: the buttonholer, the button sewer, the bartacker and the zigzag machine. Embroidery on outerwear and the quilting lines on a down jacket come from two further kinds of machine. Together they are called special machines. Their control (X–Y servo drives, pattern programming, knife interlocks) is left to Chapter 15. This chapter deals only with their mechanisms, and focuses on one problem they all share: any sideways motion of the needle or the fabric can only take place within the range of shaft angle during which the needle is out of the fabric. We first work this problem out quantitatively with the needle-swing mechanism of the zigzag machine, and then look at the mechanisms of the buttonholer, button sewer, bartacker and embroidery machine in turn.

11.1 Opening: a sewing head plus a planar motion

Take these special machines apart and their structures are strikingly similar (Fig. 11-1). There is a sewing head whose needle bar, take-up and rotary hook or looper are essentially no different from those of a lockstitch or chainstitch machine. There is a mechanism that moves the needle or the fabric in a plane: in the straight (shirt-type) buttonholer the needle bar swings sideways while the fabric is fed lengthwise; in the button sewer and the bartacker a clamp carries the fabric in X–Y; in the embroidery machine the embroidery frame moves in X–Y. And there are some additional actions, such as the buttonhole knife or the colour change in embroidery.

The planar motion and the needle penetration must be coordinated by the angle of the same main shaft, and between them there is a hard constraint: while the needle is in the fabric, there must be no horizontal relative motion between needle and fabric. Otherwise the fabric bends the needle — at best the stitches are misplaced, at worst the needle strikes the throat plate and breaks. We already met this constraint in the discussion of feed in Chapter 6 (the feed dog rises only after the needle has left the fabric), and Chapter 15 states it as “the clamp must not move while the needle is in the fabric”. This chapter looks at it from the side of the mechanism: how wide is the range of shaft angle in which motion is allowed, how should the motion be arranged within it, and what does it cost?

11.2 The zigzag stitch and the needle-swing mechanism

The zigzag stitch is numbered 304 and is a kind of lockstitch: needle and bobbin threads still interlace within the fabric, but the interlacing points alternate between left and right, so that from the face the seam is a zigzag line (Fig. 11-2, right). The stitch length is set by the feed, the bight (zigzag width) by the left–right swing of the needle bar. It is more elastic than the straight 301 and can butt-join the edges of two pieces of fabric, so it is widely used for decorative stitching, elastic attaching, underwear and joining.

In a mechanical zigzag machine the needle bar is mounted in a swing frame (needle-bar rocking frame) that can swing left and right about a pivot at its upper end. The needle bar moves up and down inside the frame, the frame swings about the pivot, and so the needle point moves left and right. The frame is driven by a zigzag cam through a rocker arm and a link (Fig. 11-2, left). In a 2-step zigzag the needle changes sides at every stitch, so the cam runs at 2 : 1 reduction and makes one revolution every two stitches; changing the effective radius of the link on the rocker arm changes the bight. An electronic zigzag machine drives the swing frame directly with a stepper motor, so bight and pattern are set by the program, and it can sew 2-step and 3-step zigzag, scallops, blind stitch and other patterns. A manufacturer’s electronic zigzag machine has a maximum bight of 8 mm as delivered (10 mm after changing gauge parts), a maximum of 5000 sti/min (some models 4500 sti/min or less), a needle-bar stroke of 33.4 mm, and 14 patterns of 8 types (a manufacturer’s zigzag machine engineer’s manual).

11.3 Needle-swing timing

11.3.1 The window while the needle is out of the fabric

The needle may swing only while its point is out of the fabric. Take the worked example of Chapters 3 and 8 (needle-bar stroke 31 mm, fabric thickness 1.5 mm): the needle point leaves the fabric surface at 258.3° and touches it again at 101.7°. To leave some margin, this chapter requires the needle point to be at least 0.5 mm above the fabric while swinging, which narrows the usable range to 260°–100°, about 200° (Fig. 11-3, top). The feed must also be completed in this interval (Chapter 6), so in a zigzag machine the feed and the needle swing share the same window.

11.3.2 Motion law and inertia force

What motion law should the swing frame follow in moving from one end to the other within the window? Three are commonly used in cam design: simple harmonic (cosine acceleration), cycloidal (sine acceleration) and modified trapezoidal. Their displacement curves look much alike (Fig. 11-3, middle), but their accelerations differ greatly (Fig. 11-3, bottom). The simple harmonic law has a jump in acceleration at the start and end, which excites shock and vibration; the cycloidal law has zero acceleration at both ends and runs smoothly, but has the highest peak; the modified trapezoid has a flat-topped acceleration that is also continuous at both ends, with a peak comparable to the simple harmonic and about 22% lower than the cycloidal. The modified trapezoid thus combines the strengths of the other two.

The peak acceleration can be written as

\[a_{\max} = C \, \frac{w \, \omega^{2}}{\beta^{2}}\]

where w is the bight, ω the angular velocity of the main shaft, β the main-shaft angle occupied by the swing (in radians), and C a coefficient of the motion law: about 4.93 for simple harmonic, 2π ≈ 6.28 for cycloidal and about 4.89 for modified trapezoidal. The equation says three things: the peak acceleration is proportional to the bight, proportional to the square of the speed, and inversely proportional to the square of the swing angle.

As a worked example take a bight of 8 mm, 5000 r/min and a swing interval of 275°–85° (170°). The peak acceleration is about 1220 m/s² for the modified trapezoid and about 1570 m/s² for the cycloidal law. Assuming the needle-bar swing frame has a mass of 150 g referred to the needle point, the sideways inertia forces are about 183 N and 235 N respectively. Taking 250 N as an illustrative limit that the swing frame and cam mechanism can withstand, both are within the limit. If the swing interval is squeezed to 120°, the acceleration roughly doubles and the inertia force under the cycloidal law exceeds the limit. The swing interval should therefore use as much of the out-of-fabric window as possible, while leaving margin for higher speed, heavier needles and thicker fabric — the thicker the fabric, the narrower the window. Feed and needle swing take place simultaneously in the same window, and both must start and finish inside it. This is one of the reasons zigzag machines are limited in speed at wide bights.

11.4 Loop catching at both ends of the swing

The zigzag machine has another problem that the lockstitch machine does not: the rotary hook must catch the loop with the needle in both the left and the right position. On industrial zigzag machines the hook shaft usually runs front to back, so that as the hook point passes beneath the needle it moves left–right, in the same direction as the needle swing (Fig. 11-4, left). As a result the hook point reaches the needle at different moments at the two ends: a bight w corresponds to w / R radians of hook rotation (R being the hook radius), and since the hook turns at twice main-shaft speed, in main-shaft angle this is

\[\Delta\varphi = \frac{w}{2R}\]

With the hook radius of 13.5 mm from Chapter 5 and a bight of 8 mm, Δφ is about 17°. As the hook point passes the two ends from right to left, the right end is caught first and the left end later, and the needle rise at the two catching moments differs by nearly 3 mm.

Both ends must be caught while the loop is large enough, so the bight is limited by the hook’s loop-catching window — and the conclusion depends heavily on how wide that window is. With the window of the lockstitch hook in Chapter 5 (a rise of about 1.5–2.8 mm, a little over eight degrees of main-shaft angle), the maximum bight would be only about 4 mm, far below the 8–10 mm of real zigzag machines. With the illustrative criterion of Chapter 10 (loop larger than 60% of the maximum, rise 1.0–4.2 mm), the left end is caught at the latest at a rise of 4.2 mm and the right end at the earliest at 1.0 mm, Δφ is at most about 20°, and the maximum bight is about 9.5 mm (Fig. 11-4, right) — close to the manual’s maximum bight of 10 mm. Evidently the range over which a zigzag machine can actually catch the loop is much wider than the conservative window of Chapter 5. Borrowing the criterion of Chapter 10 here is only a rough plausibility check; the true window must be measured.

The different needle rise at the two ends also creates a height problem. The manual specifies that with the needle at the leftmost end of its swing, the distance from the top of the needle eye to the hook point is set to 0.2–0.5 mm, and with the needle at the rightmost end, the clearance between hook point and needle is set to 0–0.05 mm (the engineer’s manual cited above). The leftmost end is caught later, when the needle has risen further and the eye is closest to the hook point, so the hook-point height is set at this end to make sure the point cannot pass below the eye. At the right end the needle has not yet risen much; if the hook point still passed on a horizontal path, it would be about 3 mm above the top of the eye and risk sliding over the top of the loop. If the hook is arranged so that at the catching position the point is moving upward along its circle, then at the right end, reached earlier, the point is also somewhat lower, which makes up part of the height difference; this chapter’s model does not take that into account, and it should be verified against an actual machine. At the right end the hook point passes a higher part of the needle, where the blade is already at full diameter and the point comes closest to the needle, so the clearance is checked at this end. The manual sets the hook-point position using a timing gauge that raises the needle 3.4 mm from its lowest point; this chapter’s worked example takes a rise of 3.8 mm at the left end and about 1.1 mm at the right end. The manual’s values and this chapter’s illustrative model do not agree completely; this has been put on the list of items to be verified.

11.5 Buttonholers

Buttonholers come in two kinds: straight (square-end) and eyelet (keyhole) (Fig. 11-5).

The straight buttonholer uses the 304 zigzag lockstitch and is common on shirts. A buttonhole is sewn in five steps: the first row of zigzag, with the needle bar swinging sideways and the fabric fed lengthwise; a bartack at one end, with the bight increased and the feed stopped; the second row of zigzag, with the feed reversed and the centre of the swing shifted to the other side; a bartack at the other end; and finally the knife drops and cuts the buttonhole open. It is also possible to cut first and sew afterwards: the cut edge is then wrapped by the thread and does not fray easily, while sewing first and cutting afterwards gives a neater edge and is more common on shirts. In a manufacturer’s electronic straight buttonholer both the needle swing and the feed are driven by stepper motors; the standard speed is 3600 sti/min and the maximum 4200 sti/min, the maximum buttonhole length is 41 mm, knife lengths are 6.4–31.8 mm, and the cloth-cutting knife is driven by a motor through a crank (a manufacturer’s straight buttonholer instruction manual). Mechanically it is simply a zigzag machine with lengthwise feed and a knife added, and the analysis of Sections 11.3 and 11.4 applies in full.

The eyelet buttonholer is used for suits, coats and denim. One end of the buttonhole is a small round eyelet that leaves room for the shank of the button. Its stitch is a single-needle two-thread zigzag chainstitch (ISO 404, in the class 400 discussed in Chapter 10): the needle swings left and right as it penetrates, and a gimp (filler cord) is enclosed in the stitch to make the buttonhole full and hard-wearing. When sewing round the eyelet, the needle and the looper (together with the spreader mechanism) must rotate about the eyelet centre so that the stitches always remain perpendicular to the buttonhole edge. A manufacturer’s electronic eyelet buttonholer runs at 400–2200 sti/min with buttonhole lengths of 10–38 mm; cut-before or cut-after is selectable on the operation panel, the cloth-cutting knife is driven up and down by a stepper motor, and the feed is driven intermittently by stepper motors as well (a manufacturer’s eyelet buttonholer product information). Its speed is far lower than that of the straight buttonholer, one reason being that the entire stitch-forming mechanism must rotate around the eyelet.

11.6 Button sewers and bartackers

The heart of the mechanical button sewer and bartacker is a pattern cam (Fig. 11-6). The main shaft drives it through a high-ratio worm and worm wheel, so that the cam makes exactly one revolution per pattern. The cam has two grooves, which through X and Y levers move the button clamp (button sewer) or the work clamp (bartacker). Each time the cam turns through one “step”, the clamp moves by one stitch; this movement too can only be fitted into the main-shaft angle during which the needle is out of the fabric. The rise portions of the cam grooves must therefore be aligned with the main-shaft angle, and the reduction ratio and number of steps must match the number of stitches in the pattern.

The button sewer holds the button in a button clamp; fabric and button move together while the needle sews back and forth between the holes. Mechanical button sewers commonly use a single-thread chainstitch (class 101 from Chapter 10), which from the back is a row of chain links: the construction is simple, with only one thread and no bobbin. Since a 101 stitch unravels when its end is pulled (Chapter 10), the button sewer needs a dedicated anti-unravelling device to lock the last loop. A manufacturer’s single-thread chainstitch button sewer has a maximum of 1500 sti/min, can sew 8, 16 or 32 stitches, has a lateral feed of 2.5–6.5 mm and an anti-unravelling device; on one of its models, two-hole and four-hole sewing are switched with a changeover lever (a manufacturer’s button sewer product information).

The bartacker is used at stressed points such as belt loops, pocket openings and the ends of flies. It uses a lockstitch, first laying an underlay and then covering it, the lower layer being wrapped by the zigzag on top. The electronic bartacker replaces the pattern cam with two stepper motors, and the work clamp moves intermittently in X–Y. A manufacturer’s electronic bartacker has a sewing area of X 40 mm × Y 30 mm, a maximum of 3200 sti/min (for stitch lengths under 5 mm in X and under 3.5 mm in Y), a needle-bar stroke of 41.2 mm, and a feed driven intermittently by stepper motors on two axes (a manufacturer’s bartacker instruction manual). With long stitches the speed must be reduced, for the same reason as in Section 11.3: each step of the clamp is longer, yet must still be completed within the shaft angle in which the needle is out of the fabric, so the acceleration is higher.

The move from mechanical cam to stepper motor is the literal meaning of the “electronic cam” of Chapter 15: the profile of a steel cam is replaced by a displacement table in the program. Mechanically, the design problems of the pattern cam (step alignment, motion law, acceleration) carry over unchanged into the motion planning of the stepper motors.

11.7 Mechanisms of quilting machines and computerised embroidery machines

The quilting machine sews face fabric, filling and lining together, for example in down jackets and quilts. A multi-needle quilting machine carries dozens of needles on one cross-beam, with a looper under each needle, and sews many parallel rows of chainstitch (401). The fabric is fed full-width underneath, and the pattern is formed by the sideways motion of the needle beam or by the motion of the fabric. Its mechanism is the chainstitch machine of Chapter 10 multiplied in parallel.

The head of a computerised embroidery machine is a box of needle bars (Fig. 11-7). The needle-bar case holds 9 to 15 needle bars in a row, each threaded with a different colour. The head has only one needle-bar driver, which grips only the needle bar aligned with it and moves it up and down; the other needle bars are held still by springs and stops. For a colour change the whole needle-bar case shifts sideways, bringing the needle bar of the next colour into line with the driver and the rotary hook. Embroidery uses a lockstitch, with the rotary hook below the throat plate; the fabric is stretched in the embroidery frame, and the frame moves in X–Y to trace the design. A multi-head embroidery machine arranges anywhere from a few to several dozen heads in a row, sharing one upper shaft and one lower shaft, with all heads embroidering the same design at once. On some models, when the thread breaks on one head, that head’s driver can be disengaged so that it alone stops while the others carry on; other models stop the whole machine. A manufacturer’s single-head embroidery machine comes in 9-, 12- and 15-needle versions, with a maximum of 1200 r/min (a manufacturer’s embroidery machine product information).

An embroidery machine runs at only about a quarter of the speed of a lockstitch machine, for the same reason that runs through this chapter: the embroidery frame, together with the fabric stretched in it, is far heavier than the swing frame of a zigzag machine, every stitch must travel a full stitch length within the shaft angle in which the needle is out of the fabric, and a single satin stitch in embroidery is often several millimetres long. Motion planning of the frame and multi-head synchronisation are covered in Chapter 16.

11.8 Design notes and limits of the model

The essence of this chapter fits in one sentence: the planar motion and the needle penetration of a special machine share the angle of one main shaft, and every sideways motion must be squeezed into the range of angle during which the needle is out of the fabric. Several design principles follow. The interval of sideways motion should use as much of this window as possible and be properly shared with the feed; the motion law should have continuous acceleration and a low peak; moving parts should be light; and bight, stitch length and speed must be decided together, with speed reduced for wide bights and long stitches. The zigzag machine adds one more: the needle must be caught at both ends, so the bight is limited by the hook’s loop-catching window.

The model in this chapter has clear limits. The needle-bar motion is borrowed from the worked example of Chapter 3 (stroke 31 mm), whereas the manufacturer’s zigzag machine has a needle-bar stroke of 33.4 mm. The 150 g swing-frame mass and the 250 N inertia-force limit are both illustrative values. The loop window borrows the illustrative criterion of Chapter 10, which is much wider than the rotary-hook window of Chapter 5; which of the two is closer to a real zigzag machine must be measured. The catching moments are calculated only from the left–right distance between hook point and needle; the hook-point height is discussed only qualitatively, and needle deflection is not considered. Real zigzag cams and stepper-motor drives also have elasticity and backlash. The sections on buttonholers, button sewers, bartackers and embroidery machines describe structure only, with parameters taken from manufacturers’ literature; detailed design must be based on specific models.

Shop-floor notes

  • If a zigzag machine breaks needles or the stitches are crooked, check the needle-swing timing first: the swing must start only after the needle has left the fabric and be finished before the needle penetrates.
  • Skipped stitches after increasing the bight usually appear at the right end (the end caught first). First reset the hook-point height at the leftmost end as the manual describes, then check the clearance at the right end.
  • The most common cause of needle breakage on a button sewer is a mismatch between the button-hole spacing and the pattern; check the hole spacing whenever the button is changed.
  • If an embroidery machine skips the first stitch after a colour change, check the height of the new needle bar and its clearance to the rotary hook; each needle bar must be adjusted individually.
