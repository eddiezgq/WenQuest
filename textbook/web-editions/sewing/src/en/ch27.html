Chapter 26 looked at how sewing machines are connected and how their data reaches the dashboards of a garment factory. This chapter changes viewpoint and stands inside the plant that builds sewing machines, asking how the information systems of a complete machine maker should be put together as a whole. The questions are practical ones. How do the model and options on an order become a bill of materials and work orders? How do the routings for housings, rotary hooks and cams get into the manufacturing execution system? How does the hook-point clearance measured at an adjustment station become a control chart and an alarm? When a machine has a problem, how can we find out within minutes which hook lot and which firmware version it carries? And how do fault codes sent back by connected machines in the field find their way into design and spare-parts planning?

Rather than draw an ideal blueprint from scratch, the chapter builds on the book’s companion WenQuest Digital Factory. Today it is a teaching plant that makes the WQR-105 gear reducer: ERPNext holds items, BOMs, routings and work orders; a unified data bus (MQTT) connects machines, software and AI; a historian stores every message on the bus; and a hub service on top provides dashboard metrics, an AI factory assistant, shop-floor execution, design release and teaching scores. On that foundation we design a plant that builds the WQ-LS series of industrial lockstitch machines, and ask what can be reused, what must be built, in how many phases, at what cost and with what payback.

27.1 Opening: a day in a sewing-machine plant

Picture a mid-sized machine maker. It machines its own housings, rotary hooks, cams and shafts; buys in needles, servo motors, control boxes and standard parts; and runs one assembly line with a 2.4-minute takt, planned at 180 industrial lockstitch machines a day. At half past eight in the morning, sales takes an order for 200 WQ-LS-TED machines, that is, with automatic thread trimming (T), electronic stitch length (E) and a micro-oil hook (D), to be delivered in 10 days.

If the plant’s information still lives in spreadsheets, paper job tickets and the PCs next to each test bench, the trip from order to shipment breaks in at least seven places (Fig. 27-1):

  1. Options are copied by hand. Sales writes the options into a spreadsheet and sends it to the planner, who copies them into the production plan; “micro-oil hook” becomes a standard hook, and nobody notices until assembly.
  2. MRP does not know about the subcontract. A micro-oil hook is finished in-house and then sent out for a diamond-like carbon (DLC) coating (Chapter 24), a subcontract of about 5 days. The material requirements plan (MRP) has no such step, so the schedule uses the lead time of the standard hook and the due date is lost from the start.
  3. Nobody owns the firmware version. The control boxes are built by a subcontractor to the plant’s specification, and the plant’s own firmware is flashed only at the end-of-line (EOL) test. Which version this order should get is written on no document; it depends on the tester’s memory.
  4. Stoppages are discovered after the shift. The horizontal machining centres that bore housings in a single setup are the plant’s bottleneck (Section 27.4). One stops for 3 h in the morning; the paper tickets reveal it only after the shift, and the day is short of housings.
  5. Measurements have no trend. The adjustment stations measure hook-point clearance on every machine and write it on paper. The numbers creep up day by day, but nobody plots them until something goes out of tolerance.
  6. Test data cannot be matched. EOL results are stored on the test-bench PC by date; file names carry no serial number, so finding one machine’s record means opening file after file.
  7. Traceability stops short. Three months later a customer reports skipped stitches on a batch of machines. The plant wants to know which hook lot these machines carry and which other machines got the same lot, but cannot find out, and ends up treating every machine shipped in that period as suspect.

The seven breaks have one thing in common: the information did exist, but it was never recorded in one place that everyone can query and cross-reference. Integrated IT means recording each piece of information where it is created, labelling it with an identifier (order, work order, serial, lot or version number) and carrying it along a single channel to wherever it is needed. The bottom row of Fig. 27-1 shows how the digital factory closes each gap; the following sections take them in turn.

27.2 Overall architecture

Layers　Fig. 27-2 splits the plant’s information systems into five layers. At the bottom are machines and lines: machine tools, the gauges at the adjustment stations, the EOL test benches, and connected sewing machines already sold to customers. These are where data comes from. Above them is the unified data bus: every machine and every piece of software only publishes messages to the bus and subscribes to messages from it; none calls another directly. Next is the historian, which keeps every message on the bus exactly as received and is the plant’s single source of truth. Then come the business systems, enterprise resource planning (ERP), product lifecycle management (PLM), manufacturing execution (MES), quality management (QMS) and warehouse management (WMS), which own records and workflows. At the top are dashboards and AI: metrics for people to look at, and proposals drafted on their behalf.

Which part serves each layer　In the WenQuest Digital Factory repository (the factory/ directory) every one of these layers already has a working implementation; its product just happens to be a gear reducer (Table 27-1). The bus is Mosquitto (MQTT), reached from browsers over WebSocket. The historian is PostgreSQL: hub/historian.py subscribes to wq/# and writes every message into the bus_message table, recording invalid messages too with valid=false, extracting machine state changes, and opening a nonconformance report (NCR) automatically when a measurement fails. The ERP is ERPNext v16, with a Node-RED bridge translating fields between ERPNext and the bus. Dashboard metrics (hub/kpi.py), the AI factory assistant (hub/ai.py), the MES (hub/mes.py), design release and approval (hub/plm.py, design.py), the parameter configurator (hub/configurator.py) and AI selection (hub/select.py) all live in the hub service. Alongside them sit the Parts and Robot Library (library/ at the top of the repository) and a finite-element and fatigue service (cae/).

Two rules　Two rules run through every module of the WenQuest Digital Factory, and the sewing-machine plant keeps both. First, no component calls another directly; data moves only over the bus. That is why replacing a simulated machine tool with a real one, or the gear reducer with a sewing machine, needs no change elsewhere as long as the messages keep the same format. Second, writes need a human: AI and rule engines may only draft proposals (ai.proposal, with requires_confirm set to true); only after someone confirms on the workbench do the bridge or the MES execute them. In teaching mode the AI only points the way and never does the work for the student.

27.3 Product data: parts library, BOM and configuration

Model × options　Industrial lockstitch machines are usually sold as a base model plus options. The WQ-LS series in this chapter has three: automatic trimming T (adds a trimmer cam and trimming and wiper solenoids, Chapter 14), electronic stitch length E (a closed-loop stepper replaces the mechanical stitch regulator, Chapter 14) and micro-oil hook D (a DLC-coated hook, Chapters 5, 23 and 24). Each option is either fitted or not, giving 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 variants: WQ-LS, WQ-LS-T, -E, -D, -TE, -TD, -ED and -TED. In ERPNext, WQ-LS can be set up as an item template with T, E and D as attributes; the template generates eight variant items, each with its own BOM. Add a few more options (automatic presser-foot lift, needle cooling, different stitch-length ranges) and the number of combinations multiplies; at that point it no longer makes sense to build a BOM for every combination in advance, and a configurator should generate it by rule when needed.

Engineering BOM and manufacturing BOM　Design’s BOM (EBOM) is organised by function: needle and take-up, hook, feed, drive, frame, control (Fig. 27-3, left). The shop floor needs a BOM (MBOM) organised by the sub-assemblies and operations of the assembly line: housing assembly, arm-shaft and needle-bar assembly, hook-shaft and hook assembly, feed assembly, trimmer assembly, plus the motor, control box, firmware and needle (Fig. 27-3, right). The parts are the same; only the structure differs. Going from EBOM to MBOM adds things the design does not contain: subcontracted steps (DLC coating), process consumables, and the firmware version bound at assembly. In the WenQuest Digital Factory the BOM follows design releases: when the design desk releases a new revision, the bridge increments the item’s design revision in ERPNext and attaches the files, and creates a new BOM if quantities changed. In production mode a release must first be approved (hub/plm.py).

Parameter configurator　hub/configurator.py turns a product into a “parameter template”: parameters and ranges, check rules, 3D model, drawing, quotation and ordering. Two templates exist today: output shaft SH-301, and reducer WQR-105 (choose ratio, input power and speed and service factor; check output torque, bearing life and key crushing stress). The quotation is the standard cost (material plus hours times work-centre rates) multiplied by one plus the margin, and ordering goes through propose-and-confirm: once confirmed, the bridge creates the sales order in ERPNext. A WQ-LS template (planned, to be built) follows the same format. Its parameters are the three options and a few selectable ranges (top-speed class, stitch-length range, fabric class); its rules are constraints between options (for example, electronic stitch length needs a certain hardware revision of the plant’s control box or later), which variants exist, and how options affect lead time (option D adds the subcontract time). It outputs the variant code, BOM, quotation and an estimated lead time. That closes breaks 1 and 2 of Section 27.1 at the moment of order entry: sales clicks options in the configurator and gets the code WQ-LS-TED and its BOM rather than a line of text, and when MRP explodes that BOM it naturally meets the DLC subcontract step and its lead time.

Firmware is an item too　Give the firmware its own BOM line, FW-WQSC-LS, quantity 1, with a version. That buys three things: once a work order is released, everyone knows which version this batch should get; the EOL bench can check that the version it flashed is the one the BOM requires; and the serial-number record automatically shows each machine’s firmware at shipment. Section 27.6 covers how firmware, parameter-table and event-table versions are managed.

27.4 Process and manufacturing execution

Routings into the MES　The gearbox plant describes its processes in data.py with three tables: work centres (name, number of machines, hourly depreciation, labour and energy), operations (name and default work centre) and routings (each a sequence of “operation, minutes per part”). seed.py imports them into ERPNext; each work order generates job cards from its routing, and the MES dispatches operation by operation. The four key part families of the sewing-machine plant each get a routing (Fig. 27-4): the housing is datum milling, single-setup boring, drilling and tapping, and bore inspection (Chapter 25); the hook is turning, carburising, race grinding, point lapping and part inspection, with a DLC subcontract step added for the micro-oil hook (Chapter 24); the cam is turning, hardening, profile grinding and inspection (Chapter 25); the needle is bought in, so its routing is only incoming inspection and stocking by lot (Chapter 24). The complete machine’s routing is assembly, adjustment, run-in and EOL test.

Finding the bottleneck　As with the gearbox plant’s “work-centre time per product”, add up all made-part and assembly operation times per machine by work centre and divide by the effective capacity to get the load (Table 27-5). The worked example plans 180 machines a day; machining runs two shifts with time derated by an overall equipment effectiveness (OEE) of 60%, and assembly and test run one shift at 90% line efficiency. The most heavily loaded centre is the horizontal machining centre HMC-H: single-setup boring takes 16 min per housing, and five HMCs on two shifts have an effective capacity of exactly 180 a day, a load of 100% with no margin at all; the assembly line, paced by its takt, is also at 100%. If the HMCs’ OEE rises to 68%, their load falls to 88%, leaving room for breakdowns and rush orders. This is why phase 1 in Section 27.9 puts “connect the key machines and raise their OEE” at the top of the list.

Machine states on the bus　Each machine tool, through a gateway or PLC, sends its state as machine.status, one of run, idle, setup, down or fault, with the current work order, operation and part serial; and its events as machine.event: cycle_start, cycle_end (with the actual cycle time cycle_time_s), alarm, fault, recover and tool_change. The dashboard’s OEE is computed by kpi.py from these messages: availability = run time / loading time (run, setup, down and fault together), performance = sum of standard cycle times / sum of actual cycle times, multiplied by the quality rate. On race and cam grinders, wheel dressing is also logged as a tool_change event; Section 27.5 relies on it.

Reporting from the shop-floor terminal　The shop-floor terminal in the WenQuest Digital Factory is the workbench’s “shop-floor execution” page: the MES dispatches operations from the routing, operators press start, pause and reset, these go to the bus as machine.cmd, machines reply with cmd/ack, and the MES never touches a machine directly. Machining operations can be reported this way. The assembly line is different: it moves at takt, so stations should not have to press start; instead the line scans (planned, to be built). A serial-number label goes on the housing assembly at the start of the line, and each station that fits a part scans the part or its lot label, recording under that serial “which hook lot, which housing heat, which control-box serial”. This step is the foundation for traceability in Section 27.5; without per-machine binding, the scope can only be guessed from time windows.

Adjustment stations and EOL benches　The adjustment stations set hook timing, needle-bar height and hook-point clearance (Chapters 5 and 23), then measure them with a gauge and send the result as quality.measurement to wq/sewing/assembly/adj-<n>/measurement, one message per machine, carrying the hook lot. The test scripts on the EOL bench (Chapter 22) send commands and read measurements through the gateway; each test case maps to a requirement, and results go out as test.result by serial number to wq/sewing/test/eol-<n>/measurement. The bench also reads the firmware and parameter-table versions reported by the controller and compares them with the versions the work-order BOM requires; any mismatch is a failure. Chapter 22 covers how EOL data and the test bench are designed.

From measurement to alarm: what exists today　The WenQuest Digital Factory already has two safeguards. The first is the historian: when a quality.measurement result is fail, it automatically opens an NCR (numbered from NCR-0001) recording the characteristic, the value, the limits and the ID of the original message. The second is an AI alert: the control chart in kpi.py takes the last 20 values of a characteristic, and ai.py checks the last 5. If their mean is at least 2 µm above the middle of the tolerance and the largest value is within 25% of the tolerance band of the upper limit, it sends “measurements rising, approaching the upper limit”, attaching the IDs of those 5 measurement messages and suggesting a check of grinding-wheel wear. That rule was written for wheel wear in the gearbox plant, where the bearing-seat tolerance band is 16 µm and the measurement standard deviation about 1.2 µm, so the maximum approaches the upper limit only when there really is a drift.

Control chart and run rules (planned, to be built)　Hook-point clearance is a different situation: the tolerance band is 60 µm, the process standard deviation about 6 µm, and an individual reading near the upper limit is ordinary scatter. The classic chart of subgroup means (X̄ chart) suits it better: take the clearances of 5 consecutive machines as a subgroup and plot their mean, with the centre line and control limits computed from a stable baseline:

\[ CL=\mu_0,\qquad UCL/LCL=\mu_0\pm 3\,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

With μ0 = 0.070 mm, σ = 0.006 mm and n = 5, the limits are 0.070 ± 0.0080 mm. The run rules come from the Western Electric handbook; this chapter uses four: R1, one point outside the 3σ limits; R2, 2 of 3 consecutive points beyond 2σ on the same side; R3, 4 of 5 consecutive points beyond 1σ on the same side; R4, 8 consecutive points on the same side of the centre line. More rules find a drift sooner but also raise more false alarms when nothing has changed, so there is a trade-off. Alarms go out as ai.alert, with the measurement-message IDs of the triggering subgroups as evidence; following the de-duplication in ai.py, the same rule does not repeat within 2 h. After an alarm the system drafts a proposal to “quarantine the suspect lot”, executed only after the quality inspector confirms it.

def spc_alarms(vals, mu0, sigma, rules, n=5):
    """vals: hook-point clearances (mm) in serial order; returns [(serial, rule)]."""
    s, Z, out = sigma / n ** 0.5, [], []
    for i in range(n, len(vals) + 1, n):
        Z.append((sum(vals[i - n:i]) / n - mu0) / s)      # z value of the subgroup mean
        w3, w5, w8 = Z[-3:], Z[-5:], Z[-8:]
        if "R1" in rules and abs(Z[-1]) > 3: out.append((i, "R1"))
        if "R2" in rules and len(w3) == 3 and (sum(z > 2 for z in w3) >= 2 or sum(z < -2 for z in w3) >= 2): out.append((i, "R2"))
        if "R3" in rules and len(w5) == 5 and (sum(z > 1 for z in w5) >= 4 or sum(z < -1 for z in w5) >= 4): out.append((i, "R3"))
        if "R4" in rules and len(w8) == 8 and (all(z > 0 for z in w8) or all(z < 0 for z in w8)): out.append((i, "R4"))
    return out

Worked example: drifting hook clearance　The assembly line averages 22.5 machines an hour. From hour 2 (machine 46) the line starts using a new hook lot, H2610-07. When the races of this lot were ground, the wheel-dressing interval had been raised from 50 to 150 parts, so the distance from hook point to mounting datum grows hook by hook as the wheel wears. Assembly locates the hook on its datum, so the resulting clearance drifts by 4 µm per hour (all illustrative values). Assuming a normal distribution, the chance that a single machine is out of tolerance is about 1% four hours after the drift starts, 4.8% after five hours and 16% after six.

Simulating 16 h and 360 machines with one fixed set of random numbers (seed 5) gives Fig. 27-5 and Table 27-7. Relying on per-machine tolerance checks, the first out-of-spec machine is no. 174 (7.7 h), and only then does the historian open its first NCR. Withdrawing the lot 0.5 h later, 140 machines already carry it, and by the 8 h despatch 135 of them have been packed and shipped. With R1 alone the alarm comes at machine 100 (4.4 h); with all four rules, R2 fires at machine 70 (3.1 h), 4.6 h before the first out-of-spec machine, and only 36 machines (nos. 46–81) are affected, all still in the plant and none out of tolerance. The existing “approaching the upper limit” rule does poorly here: with the same random numbers and no drift it raises 3 alarms in 16 h, all false.

The cost of finding out late　The right of Fig. 27-5 counts from the R1–R4 alarm at machine 70. Every hour of delay adds about 22 affected machines; 4 h late, 126 machines need re-checking but none is yet out of tolerance; 5 h late, rework begins (6 machines), and the 8 h despatch has already sent 135 affected machines out, to be chased at customers’ sites. The value of a control chart is not saving a few reworks, but stopping while the problem is still only a trend and keeping the suspect scope small. The last column of the table is a reminder too: averaged over 1000 random sets, R1–R4 has a better-than-even chance of at least one false alarm in 16 h, and each false alarm means stopping to investigate. The choice of rules has to be weighed against the cost of false alarms.

NCR and 8D　After an alarm come two steps. Containment: quarantine the remaining stock of hook lot H2610-07 (in the example, 36 of 300 used, 264 left), list machines 46–81 as suspect and re-check their clearance, and open an NCR (quality.ncr has four states: open, rework, scrap and use_as_is). Root cause and correction: record the work in 8D steps (form a team, describe the problem, interim containment, find the root cause, choose and verify a permanent correction, implement it, prevent recurrence, close out). Here the root cause is that the dressing interval was relaxed without validation; the permanent correction is to write the interval into the hook’s process parameters, put each dressing on the bus as a tool_change event and have a rule warn when the interval is exceeded; preventing recurrence means routing process-parameter changes through the engineering change process of Section 27.6. The 8D template and the “quarantine suspect lot” proposal are planned (to be built); ERPNext’s quality module has documents such as Non Conformance and Quality Action that could carry the record, though the exact fields are to be verified.

Serial-number traceability　Tracing works in two directions (Fig. 27-6). Backward (trace-back): from machine 70 that raised the alarm to its hook lot H2610-07, then to that lot’s work order, the race grinder it was ground on and the wheel-dressing records of those days, which point to the cause. Forward (trace-forward): from the lot to the other machines that got it (nos. 46–81), how many hooks are left in stores, and whether any machine has shipped. Both directions depend on the scanning described in Section 27.4. Serial numbers and lots themselves are ERPNext Serial No and Batch documents, while “which lot went into this machine”, “what its clearance measured” and “which firmware it shipped with” are messages in the historian. The machine record (planned, to be built) is simply a page that assembles those messages by serial number, every item linking back to its original message.

Shop-floor notes

  • How deep you can trace depends on whether assembly scanned. Without per-machine lot binding, the scope can only be “everything built in those days”, several times larger.
  • Compute control limits from data taken while the process is stable; never substitute tolerance limits. Tolerances tell you whether a part is usable; control limits tell you whether the process has changed.
  • Log wheel dressing, tool changes, lot changes and parameter changes as events. When something goes wrong, the first thing to look at is what happened around the alarm.
  • After an alarm, contain first and analyse later: quarantine suspect lots and machines, then take time over the cause, never the other way round.

27.6 Version control for controls and software

The “software” of an electronically controlled sewing machine is more than firmware. In the firmware framework of Chapter 19, at least four things carry their own versions: the firmware (the program on the controller), the parameter table (stitch length, speeds, needle stop positions, trimming speed and so on), the event table (angle-triggered actions such as energising the trimming solenoid from 205° to 330°, Chapter 19) and the controller hardware revision. They depend on one another: new firmware may need an extra parameter, an event table may apply only to machines with option T, and electronic stitch length may need a given controller revision or later. These dependencies belong in a compatibility table enforced by both the EOL bench and remote updates (Table 27-8).

Engineering change (ECO)　Any change to a versioned object goes through one process: a change request (why: for instance, the excess of trimming-solenoid overcurrent codes in Fig. 27-7); an impact review (which variants, which work in progress and stock, which machines already sold); the effectivity (a serial-number break such as from LS2611-0001, or a date; whether work in progress is reworked); then approval and release. The PLM in the WenQuest Digital Factory already has submit–approve–release: in production mode a design first goes to review as design.submit; the reviewer views and annotates it in the browser, and only after approval is design.release published, so ERPNext and the shop follow. On receiving design.release, the AI checks whether open work orders are affected and asks the process engineer to decide whether parts already started continue on the old revision or are reworked. Treating firmware, parameter tables and event tables as releasable items (releasing a firmware image, its signature and release notes rather than a STEP file) lets the plant reuse this machinery (planned, to be built).

Remote update and rollback　Whether machines already sold should be updated is part of the ECO review. Section 26.7 described remote updates: signed firmware, dual-bank update (Chapter 18) and staged roll-out. For the sewing-machine plant the connected fleet can be split into three rings. Ring 1 is about 1% of machines, in the plant itself and at a few partner customers; after the update, watch 72 h of running and compare the fault rate per thousand running hours with the old version. Only if it has not risen does ring 2 (about 10%) follow, then everyone. If any ring shows a problem, roll back to the old version kept in the second bank. The update itself is a record: which machine, when, from which version to which, approved by whom, all on the bus and in the historian, so that the firmware in a machine’s record does not stay frozen at the day it shipped.

27.7 Service and data coming back

Data from connected machines sold to garment factories belongs first to those factories: it goes onto their own bus (for example wq/garment/…, Chapter 26). What the machine maker receives must be set out in the contract and agreed by the customer, and what is forwarded should be data about the machine itself (running hours, fault codes, firmware version, parameters), not personal data about operators. On that basis, service data coming back (planned, to be built) can do three jobs.

Fault codes by version　Counting fault codes per thousand running hours, rather than per machine or per month, is what makes machines with different workloads comparable. Grouping by firmware version then shows whether a change worked. Fig. 27-7 (left) is an illustration: trimming-solenoid overcurrent (E-31) runs at 0.62 per thousand running hours under 1.4.x and falls to 0.18 under 1.5.0, after the boost time in the parameter table was changed. With 6000 machines running about 5.4 million hours a year, that is a fall from about 3350 to about 970 events a year. Needle-positioning timeouts (E-44) also drop clearly, while the other codes stay about the same, so the change brought no side effects.

Reliability analysis back to design　Linking service records (which part was replaced, which failure) to the shipping record by serial number answers the questions design and process engineers care most about: is a failure concentrated in one lot of parts, one firmware version or one period of shipment? It is the same set of links as the traceability of Section 27.5; only the starting point changes, from an alarm on the shop floor to a customer’s repair call.

Spare-parts forecasting　The rotary hook is a wear part. Estimated from the mean life, demand is simply the fleet’s running hours in a quarter divided by the mean life; but for a fairly “young” fleet this overestimates, because most hooks have not reached their wear-out period. A better method computes, for each machine and its own accumulated running hours, the conditional probability that its hook fails in the next quarter, and adds them up. If hook life follows a Weibull distribution with shape β and scale η, a hook that has already run a hours fails within the next Δ hours with probability

\[ P=1-\frac{R(a+\Delta)}{R(a)},\qquad R(t)=\exp\!\left[-\left(\frac{t}{\eta}\right)^{\beta}\right] \]

The worked example takes β = 2.2 and η = 5000 h (mean life η·Γ(1+1/β) ≈ 4430 h), a connected fleet of 6000 machines whose annual running hours are lognormal with a mean of about 900 h, installed 0.5–3.5 years ago. From the mean life, the next quarter needs about 303 hooks; from each machine’s accumulated hours, only about 202, rising quarter by quarter as the fleet ages to about 285 after two years (Fig. 27-7, right). A hundred spare hooks are not worth much in themselves, but for expensive parts with many variants the same gap is real money tied up in stock.

27.8 Where AI fits in the digital factory

AI in the WenQuest Digital Factory does four things, each with clear limits.

Daily briefing　Each day ai.py picks the noteworthy items from the results of kpi.py and writes a briefing (ai.briefing, a retained message), every item with evidence: topic, document number or message ID. In teaching mode the briefing is written as questions (“Which machine is this week’s bottleneck? Look at the queue lengths”) rather than answers.

Proactive alerts　Rules check the whole plant: a machine down or faulted (with its queue and whether it is the bottleneck), tool or wheel life below 15%, measurements approaching a limit, a new NCR, an order at risk of being late, a key material below safety stock; the same item is not repeated within 2 h. The rules the sewing-machine plant adds (planned, to be built) are mainly the run rules of Section 27.5, the dressing-interval limit, and “the firmware version read by the test bench does not match the work order” from Section 27.6.

Answers and draft proposals　It answers questions such as “can this order be delivered on time?” or “why is this work order over cost?”, with every number taken from the historian and its source given. Anything that must be written into ERPNext (creating an order, a purchase request, quarantining a lot) is only drafted as a proposal; after someone confirms it on the workbench the bridge executes it, the result comes back on the bus as erp.doc, and the proposal’s status changes to executed or failed.

AI selection　hub/select.py takes a one-sentence requirement and picks only from the parts library. Its answer gives entry IDs, sizes and specification-table rows, and invents nothing the library does not have. Every ID in the answer is checked; IDs not in the library are removed from the result and listed under invalid_refs. The model goes through the factory’s existing AI channel (hub/llm.py): Claude in the United States, and only registered models (such as DeepSeek) in deployments inside China; with neither configured, a rule-based answer is used, still picking only from the library.

Some examples from the sewing-machine plant (the first two work with today’s capabilities; the last two need the planned parts):

  1. “Which bearing for the ends of the arm shaft, 12 mm bore?” AI selection lists the 12 mm-bore candidates in the A-BRG-DG specification table, such as 6001 and 6201, citing the entry and rows (for example 6201: 12 × 32 × 10 mm, GB/T 276); the engineer chooses by load.
  2. “Recommend a trimming solenoid with an 8 mm stroke.” AI selection should answer that the library has no push-pull solenoid entry yet (D-SOL-PUSH is a proposed ID, planned for the library) and give no part number. That is exactly the behaviour the rules require.
  3. “200 × WQ-LS-TED in 10 days: can we make it?” The AI explodes the variant BOM, finds the 5-day DLC subcontract and the HMCs at 100% load, answers “on the current schedule, about day 12”, lists its evidence, and drafts two proposals for the planner to choose from: add a shift, or agree a split delivery with the customer.
  4. “Why did hook clearance alarm?” The AI lists the triggering subgroups, the hook lot in those machines and the wheel-dressing events on GRD-H-02 while that lot was ground, and drafts “quarantine H2610-07” for the quality inspector to confirm.

Three rules for AI: use only data that is in the system (the historian and the parts library); cite the source of every conclusion, linking back to the original message or entry; and treat any write to ERPNext as a proposal that runs only after a person confirms it. In teaching mode the AI points the way and does not do the work.

Integrated IT should not be rolled out in one go. A familiar lesson is the plant that bought a very complete system before cleaning up its master data or connecting any machines; every module starved for data, and in the end only ERP finance and purchasing were used. Payback　Investment is 1.5, 1.2 and 0.8 million CNY for the three phases (software implementation, gateways and network, gauge upgrades, training; illustrative), spread evenly over each phase’s build time. After go-live, savings ramp linearly to their full value over 6 months, and running costs are 15% a year of the investment already live. The cumulative monthly cash flow is computed as follows:

def cashflow(phases, savings, horizon=60, ramp=6, run_rate=0.15):
    cum, c, pay = [], 0.0, None
    for m in range(1, horizon + 1):
        f = 0.0
        for ph, s in zip(phases, savings):
            st, bd = ph["start"], ph["build"]
            if st < m <= st + bd:                    # build: investment spread evenly
                f -= ph["invest"] / bd
            if m > st + bd:                          # live: savings ramp up, less running cost
                f += s / 12 * min(1.0, (m - st - bd) / ramp)
                f -= ph["invest"] * run_rate / 12
        c += f; cum.append(c)
        if pay is None and m > 1 and c >= 0 and cum[-2] < 0:
            pay = m
    return cum, pay

Fig. 27-8 shows the result. Cumulative cash flow bottoms out around month 15 at about −2.03 million CNY; with all three phases the whole investment is recovered in month 33, and the net gain over 5 years is about 5.65 million. Phase 1 alone pays back in month 23 but earns only about 4.10 million in 5 years. Phase 1 pays back fast because its savings come straight from the bottleneck and from inventory. Phases 2 and 3 push payback out by 10 months but add about 1.55 million over 5 years, and many of their benefits (smaller trace scopes, fewer complaints, design changes backed by data) are not counted at all. The example also shows that payback is sensitive to the phase-1 OEE target: if OEE only reaches 64%, payback moves to month 38; shortening the phases somewhat (for example, connecting only the bottleneck machines first) brings it back within 36 months. Page ③ of Virtual lab 27-1 lets you try these combinations.

Shop-floor notes

  • The hardest part of phase 1 is not software but master data: item codes, BOMs, routings and standard times must be cleaned up and owned by someone, or every later metric is untrustworthy.
  • Connect the bottleneck machines first. Raising the OEE of a non-bottleneck machine does not raise plant output.
  • Fix each KPI’s baseline and definition before go-live (for example, first-pass yield counted by first inspection per serial number, not per inspection document), then compute it automatically from the historian rather than from reports.
  • Agree the savings calculation with finance and review it 6 months after go-live; if a target is missed, find the reason rather than change the definition.

27.11 Limits of the model

The plant in this chapter is illustrative: standard times, machine counts, prices and KPI baselines and targets are chosen to explain the method and represent no real company. The load calculation uses average times and a single OEE, ignoring batch sizes, changeovers, work in progress between operations and mixed-model sequencing; real scheduling needs finite-capacity planning or simulation of the kind in Chapter 29. The quality-loop example assumes a known, constant process standard deviation, a drift that grows linearly from one machine on, hooks used in the order they were stocked, and 100% measurement after assembly with a perfect gauge. In practice a measurement system analysis must first show that the gauge’s repeatability and reproducibility are adequate, and control limits must be reviewed regularly against real data. False-alarm probabilities assume independent, normally distributed subgroup means; real data are often autocorrelated and raise more false alarms. The Weibull parameters in the spares forecast are assumed; real ones must be estimated from service records, handling the units that have not yet failed (censored data). The payback calculation counts only items that convert directly into money, applies no discounting, and leaves out the costs of system replacement, data governance and staff turnover. Finally, everything marked “planned (to be built)” has not yet been written or tested; the existing parts of the WenQuest Digital Factory were developed and accepted for the gearbox plant, and moving them to a sewing-machine plant means re-validating message fields, rule thresholds and pages.

You set when the drift starts, how fast it goes and any step change, choose run rules (R1–R4, plus the existing WenQuest “approaching the limit” rule), and see when an alarm is raised and an NCR generated, compared with “tolerance only”; the same random numbers without drift check for false alarms. Page ② is the serial-to-lot graph: from the alarming serial back to the hook lot and the lead to the cause, forward to the affected machines, the stock left and how many have shipped; you can open any machine’s record and see how many more machines must be re-checked or reworked if the problem is found N hours later. Page ③ is the phased roll-out calculator: investment, build time and KPI targets for each phase give the annual savings, cumulative cash flow and payback. Three auto-checked tasks: choose run rules that alarm at least 4 h before the first out-of-spec machine without any false alarm when there is no drift; work out the number of affected machines from the alarm; and, with OEE reaching only 64%, bring payback back within 36 months by shortening the build times alone. The model is the same as the worked examples in Sections 27.5 and 27.9 (figsrc/ch27_model.py).
