One is a consumable used up every day; the other is the most precise part in the machine. The needle is made good, piece after piece, by high-volume, high-rate forming and heat treatment; the hook gets a clearance of a few tens of micrometres, reliably, from fine grinding, lapping and selective assembly. 24.1 Opening: the two smallest and hardest parts

On a garment factory’s sewing floor the most common calls for the mechanic are of two kinds. One is “the needle broke” or “the needle is blunt and skipping”, fixed by changing the needle. The other is “the hook is noisy” or “the thread keeps breaking”: the mechanic takes the hook out, looks for burrs on the point and wear on the basket, and in bad cases replaces the whole hook. Neither part is large – a needle weighs a gram or two, a hook fits in the palm of a hand – yet together they decide whether a stitch forms at all, and how well.

The needle: a precision part consumed in huge numbers　In every stitch the needle pierces the fabric, carries the needle thread down, and lets the loop bulge out on its scarf side (Chapter 2); at 5000 r/min a lockstitch machine penetrates the fabric more than eighty times a second. Needles are bent and broken by hard yarns, buttons and zips, points go blunt, and the thread wears grooves into the eye. Changing needles is routine: for a factory with 300 machines, each using on average half a needle a day over 300 working days (illustrative), that is about 45 000 needles a year. Across all the world’s sewing machines needles are counted in hundreds of millions. Volumes like that mean needles must be made in batches on highly automated special machines at a cost of cents per piece – and yet every single needle must have an accurately placed scarf, a smooth eye, a correct point and adequate straightness, or it will skip stitches, break thread and damage fabric.

The rotary hook: the part that decides stitch quality and noise　The hook point passes within 0.1 mm of the needle at more than ten metres per second (Chapter 5), while the basket slides in the race at a similar speed. The shape of the point decides whether the loop is caught reliably; the clearance between race and basket decides whether the basket rattles, whether the thread passes freely and whether the hook is noisy. Chapter 23 called the hook the most precise part of the lockstitch machine, and this is why: its critical fit is only ten to a few tens of micrometres, and it must work for a long time at high speed with little oil.

The two parts stand for two ways of manufacturing. The needle is “high volume, cheap per piece, every piece good”, relying on consistent forming and 100% automatic inspection. The hook is “medium volume, expensive per piece, accurate fit”, relying on fine grinding, lapping and selective assembly. This chapter follows both routes.

24.2 Needle structure, sizes and quality requirements

24.2.1 Structure

We met the needle in Chapter 2 (Fig. 24-1). From top to bottom: the shank, the thick cylinder that goes into the needle bar and is clamped by the needle-clamp screw, whose diameter and length are fixed by the “needle system” so that needles of one system are interchangeable; the taper, the transition from shank to blade; the blade, the long slender working part whose diameter is the needle size; the long groove, running along one side of the blade, in which the descending needle thread is protected from the fabric; the short groove and the scarf, on the other side near the eye, the scarf being where the hook point passes; the eye, through which the thread runs; and the point, which opens the fabric. The usual needle system for industrial lockstitch machines is DB×1 (also called 16×231), with a shank diameter of about 1.62 mm (the needle maker’s catalogue governs).

24.2.2 Needle size and stiffness

The metric size Nm gives the blade diameter, measured above the scarf, in hundredths of a millimetre: an Nm 90 needle has a 0.90 mm blade (Wikipedia, “Sewing machine needle”). Shops also use a second numbering, with common equivalents Nm 70 ≈ 10, Nm 80 ≈ 12, Nm 90 ≈ 14 and Nm 110 ≈ 18.

The blade is a slender rod. Its bending stiffness is proportional to the second moment of area, and so is its buckling load, which also falls with the square of the length:

\[ EI = E\,\frac{\pi d^{4}}{64},\qquad F_{\mathrm{cr}} = \frac{\pi^{2} E I}{(\mu L)^{2}} \;\propto\; \frac{d^{4}}{L^{2}} \]

Because of the fourth power, needle size has a very strong effect on stiffness (Fig. 24-2): at equal length an Nm 110 needle is 2.23 times as stiff as an Nm 90, an Nm 70 only 0.37 times, and Nm 110 versus Nm 70 differ by a factor of 6.1 (worked-example values, figsrc/ch24_calc.py). This is one reason heavy fabrics need thick needles: a thin needle driven into thick material is easily pushed aside and bent, and a deflected needle hits the hook point (the needle guard mentioned in Chapter 5 is there to prevent this). It also explains why straightness is a critical characteristic: a needle that is already bent deflects even more under the penetration force.

Reducing the blade from shank diameter to needle size is a large deformation. For DB×1 at Nm 90 the blade keeps only about 31% of the shank cross-section, a reduction in area of about 69% (worked-example value). A reduction that large calls for a dedicated process (Section 24.3).

24.2.3 Point shapes

The point is chosen for the fabric. Wovens take a sharp point that pierces the fibres. Knits take a ball point that pushes a way between the yarns rather than cutting them – a knit with cut yarns ladders into holes; fine knits use a light ball point, coarse knits and elastic fabrics a rounder medium ball point. Leather and coated fabrics use cutting points whose edge slices the material; the orientation of the edge determines how the stitch line looks. Point shape is set by point grinding and by polishing; over-polishing rounds a sharp point, so polishing time and media are specified separately for each point type.

24.2.4 Critical quality characteristics

Matching the needle’s functions with its failure modes gives its critical characteristics (Table 24-1). The hardest to reconcile are hardness and toughness. A harder needle resists wear at the point and eye and is less easily grooved by the thread; but the harder it is, the more brittle, and when it strikes something hard it snaps instead of bending. A broken needle can leave its tip in the garment, which in a garment factory is a serious quality and safety incident – many factories stop work until every fragment of a broken needle has been found. Needle heat treatment therefore has to balance “wear-resistant” against “bend, don’t break”, a point we return to under tempering in Section 24.3.

Scarf position and depth deserve a further word. In the dimensional chain of Chapter 23, the needle’s scarf-face dimension is one of the eight links (worked-example tolerance ±0.02 mm), and it is a link that “changes with the needle”: the clearance adjusted at assembly holds only for the needle then fitted; with a new needle that link’s error comes back, and in Chapter 23’s example the in-tolerance rate falls to about 98% after a needle change. The machine maker cannot adjust every needle the customer buys; it can only specify the scarf tolerance to the needle maker. The scarf accuracy of the needle is thus effectively part of every machine’s hook clearance.

24.3 The needle manufacturing process

Needle manufacture falls into five stages: stock preparation (wire, straightening, cutting), forming (blade reducing, pressing the grooves and scarf, punching the eye, grinding the point), heat treatment (quenching, tempering, straightening), surface finishing (deburring and polishing, plating) and inspection and packing (Fig. 24-3). Makers differ in sequence, grouping and equipment; what follows is a typical route organised by process principle, and Table 24-2 lists the purpose, equipment, key parameters and typical defects of each operation, all values illustrative.

Stock　The raw material is high-carbon steel wire (the carbon content must be high for the needle to harden enough; illustrative), with a diameter about equal to the shank. Incoming wire is checked for diameter, surface cracks and decarburised depth: wire with a decarburised skin quenches to a soft surface, and the point and eye wear quickly. The wire arrives in coils; it is roller-straightened and cut into blanks of needle length plus allowance.

Blade reducing　The shank keeps the wire’s original diameter; the blade must be brought down to the needle size, with a taper in between. Two methods are common. Rotary swaging hammers the blank with a set of dies that spin around it and reciprocate radially at high speed, thinning the blade step by step; the grain flow stays continuous and the surface dense. Alternatively the blade is centreless-ground. With a reduction in area of about 69% for Nm 90, swaging is often done in several passes. The shank end is chamfered at the same time to ease needle insertion. This operation sets the blade diameter (the size) and the taper, and it is the first source of straightness error: uneven reduction bends the blade.

Pressing the grooves and scarf　The long groove, short groove and scarf are cold-pressed with form dies in special presses. Groove depth and position come from the die; as the die wears the grooves get shallower and the scarf drifts, so die life is managed by stroke count. Scarf position, which affects hook clearance, is the most important dimension here.

Flattening and punching the eye　This is the most critical and most trouble-prone operation in needle making (Fig. 24-4). The eye zone is first pressed thin from both sides, then pierced with a very fine punch. The punch is thinner than the eye and must pierce unhardened high-carbon steel, so it wears and breaks quickly. A worn punch leaves a growing burr and the eye width drifts; a slightly off-centre punch puts the eye off the blade centre, leaving one wall too thin, and the needle later breaks at the eye. Burrs and sharp edges at the eye are the main cause of cut and broken thread; polishing removes them, but a burr too big for polishing can only be prevented at the punching operation itself. So punching needs both in-process measurement of eye width (the control chart of Section 24.4) and punch replacement by stroke count.

Point grinding　The point is ground with form wheels; sharp, ball and cutting points each have their own wheel and fixture. The point must be centred, not offset to one side; feed and coolant must be controlled, since local overheating softens the tip (grinding burn).

Quenching and tempering　After forming the needle is still soft and must be hardened. Needles are thin, so they heat and cool fast; they are commonly heated continuously in a protective atmosphere to prevent oxidation and decarburisation, then oil-quenched. The hardening temperature depends on the steel; for high-carbon steels it lies around 760–820 °C (illustrative). As-quenched needles are hard and brittle and must be tempered: held at a fairly low temperature (illustrative: around 150–250 °C) to relieve quenching stresses and reduce brittleness. The tempering temperature is the main lever on the hardness–toughness balance: a little lower gives a harder, more wear-resistant but more brittle needle; a little higher gives a tougher needle that bends rather than breaks, but a point and eye that wear faster. The exact choice depends on the steel, the needle type and its use, and is among a needle maker’s core process data.

Straightening　A long, thin needle always distorts in quenching. After heat treatment each needle is checked for straightness and straightened; those that cannot be straightened are rejected. Straightness is a key inspection item in Section 24.4.

Deburring and polishing　Large batches of needles are tumbled with abrasive media for a long time in barrels or vibratory finishers, removing burrs from the eye, groove edges and point and smoothing the surface. Electropolishing is an alternative: in an electrolyte the tiny surface peaks dissolve first, which works well inside the eye where media struggle to reach. Polishing time has to be controlled: too short leaves burrs, too long rounds the point and reduces dimensions.

Plating　Finally a metal layer or coating is applied. Nickel plating is cheap and prevents rust; chrome plating is harder and smoother, with less friction against the thread; for demanding duty, hard wear-resistant coatings such as titanium nitride reduce wear at the eye and point and reduce thread sticking and heating when sewing synthetic thread at high speed. The plating must adhere well and be uniform, including inside the eye.

Inspection and packing　Needle volumes rule out looking at every needle by eye. Modern needle plants use in-line automatic optical inspection (machine vision) to check the geometry of every needle – blade diameter, straightness, point shape, eye position and width, plating appearance – and reject nonconforming pieces automatically; hardness, scarf dimensions, eye burrs and the like are sampled by lot (Section 24.4). Good needles are boxed by system, size and point type, with the lot number printed on the box for traceability.

24.4.2 Process capability: Cpk

Knowing whether each needle is good is not enough. The plant also wants to know whether an operation is capable of producing good parts consistently. That is measured by process capability indices. With lower and upper specification limits LSL and USL, process mean μ and standard deviation σ:

\[ C_p = \frac{\mathrm{USL}-\mathrm{LSL}}{6\sigma},\qquad C_{pk} = \min\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{USL}-\mu}{3\sigma},\ \frac{\mu-\mathrm{LSL}}{3\sigma}\right) \]

Cp compares only the width of the tolerance band with the spread of the process; Cpk also accounts for how far the mean sits from the centre of the band, so Cpk ≤ Cp, with equality when the mean is centred. Cpk = 1 means the nearer limit is exactly 3σ from the mean, about 0.135% out on that side; many industries take Cpk ≥ 1.33 (nearer limit 4σ from the mean) as a common requirement for “capable”, with more for critical characteristics (illustrative; customer and company rules govern).

Worked example　Take the eye width of an Nm 90 needle with a specification of 0.360 ± 0.020 mm (illustrative). The punching operation has a true mean of 0.362 mm and a standard deviation of 0.004 mm (illustrative). The in-line vision system takes a subgroup of 5 needles at intervals, and we first collect 25 subgroups (125 needles). In practice the true μ and σ are unknown and must be estimated: the grand mean X̿ estimates μ, and the average subgroup range R̄ divided by the constant d2 (2.326 for n = 5) estimates σ. Simulating these 25 subgroups with figsrc/ch24_model.py gives

\[ \bar{\bar X}=0.3622\ \mathrm{mm},\quad \bar R = 0.0091\ \mathrm{mm},\quad \hat\sigma=\frac{\bar R}{d_2}=0.0039\ \mathrm{mm} \]
\[ C_p=\frac{0.040}{6\times0.0039}\approx1.70,\qquad C_{pk}=\frac{0.380-0.3622}{3\times0.0039}\approx1.51 \]

From the true parameters, Cpk = 0.018/0.012 = 1.50 with about 3.4 ppm (parts per million) out of specification, so the estimate is close to the truth. The operation is capable. But capable now is not capable forever: punches wear. Suppose a new batch of punches, or punch wear, moves the mean up by 1σ (0.004 mm) to 0.366 mm. Cpk drops to (0.380 − 0.366)/0.012 ≈ 1.17 and the out-of-specification rate rises to about 233 ppm, roughly 70 times as much. The question is how to detect such a drift as early as possible.

24.4.3 Control charts: how soon is a drift seen?

That is the job of a control chart. Plot each subgroup mean X̄ in time order; the centre line is X̿ and the control limits are

\[ \mathrm{UCL},\ \mathrm{LCL} = \bar{\bar X} \pm A_2 \bar R,\qquad \mathrm{UCL}_R = D_4 \bar R \]

For n = 5, A2 = 0.577 and D4 = 2.114 (from the tables of constants in statistical quality control texts). The example’s limits are 0.3570–0.3675 mm. Control limits come from the operation’s own variation and have nothing to do with the specification: a point outside them says the process has changed, and even if that needle is still within specification the operation should be stopped and the cause found. The right half of Fig. 24-5 shows the example chart with a +1σ drift injected from subgroup 26; subgroup 29 (the fourth after the drift) is the first point outside the limits.

How soon the alarm comes can be calculated in advance. The limits of the X̄ chart lie at μ ± 3σ/√n; after the mean shifts by δσ, the probability that a subgroup falls outside is

\[ p = \Phi\!\left(-3+\delta\sqrt{n}\right)+\Phi\!\left(-3-\delta\sqrt{n}\right),\qquad \mathrm{ARL}=\frac{1}{p} \]

Φ is the standard normal distribution function and ARL, the average run length, is the average number of subgroups until an alarm. With no drift (δ = 0), ARL₀ ≈ 370: one false alarm every 370 subgroups on average. With a drift, the smaller ARL₁ the better. Two shop-floor rules follow from this table. First, small drifts are hard to see: a 0.5σ drift with 5 needles per subgroup still takes 33 subgroups on average, a day and a half at one subgroup per hour. To be faster, increase the subgroup size or add supplementary rules such as “several consecutive points on one side of the centre line” (statistical quality control texts give the full set of rules). Second, subgroup size trades cost against speed: going from n = 3 to n = 5 cuts the time to detect a 1σ drift from about 10 subgroups to 4.5, at the cost of measuring two more needles each time. For an operation like needle making with 100% vision inspection, measurement costs almost nothing, so n can be large and the interval short. For destructive items (hardness) only a few samples are possible, and prevention in the process has to carry the load: punches changed by stroke count, furnace temperature curves checked for every charge.

Shop-floor notes

  • Eye punching: change punches on a fixed stroke count, not when burrs appear; watch the first few subgroups after a punch change separately to make sure the mean has not jumped.
  • When the chart alarms, check people, machine, material, method, environment and measurement first; don’t reach for the machine adjustment straight away. Record every adjustment, or later changes on the chart cannot be explained.
  • Straightness is one-sided (smaller is better, upper limit only); compute capability as (USL − μ)/3σ only. It is also not normally distributed, so with small samples treat Cpk as indicative only (Section 24.9).
  • After a needle breaks, find all the fragments and keep the broken needle for the quality department to examine the fracture: did it break on a hard object, or because it was over-hard?

24.5 Rotary-hook structure and working conditions

Chapters 2 and 5 described the parts of the hook (Fig. 24-6). The hook body is mounted on the hook shaft and turns, with the hook point on its rim; the basket (bobbin-case holder) sits in the race of the hook body and is kept from turning by the positioning finger on the housing; the bobbin case with its bobbin fits on the basket’s centre post; and the gib (retaining ring) screwed to the front of the hook body keeps the basket in. From a manufacturing point of view the hook body and the basket are the two main precision parts, and the fit between them is the protagonist of the second half of this chapter: a rib around the outside of the basket runs in the race, an annular groove inside the hook body.

Speeds　A full rotary hook turns at twice the main-shaft speed (Chapter 5). Taking the hook-point radius as 13.5 mm (Chapter 5’s worked-example value) and the race radius as 12 mm (illustrative), the hook’s working conditions are as in Table 24-5. At a main-shaft speed of 5000 r/min the hook point moves at about 14.1 m/s and the basket rib slides relative to the race at about 12.6 m/s; over an eight-hour shift the race slides about 360 km. The hook point passes an Nm 90 needle (0.9 mm) in about 64 μs.

What this asks of manufacturing　Translated into part requirements, these conditions give four. First, the hook point: an accurate shape (it sets the loop-catching window, Chapter 5), a rounded edge free of burrs (otherwise it splits and scrapes the thread), and wear resistance so the shape stays the same over a long life. Second, race and rib: very hard and very smooth surfaces with low friction and slow wear that do not seize with little oil. Third, the race-to-rib clearance: too small and the hook heats up and binds, and the thread is obstructed as it passes through the gap between the positioning finger and the basket; too large and the basket rattles with a clicking noise, and the loop’s position as it passes is unsteady (Chapter 5, Section 5.7). Fourth, the position of the hook point relative to the hook’s mounting bore: it is the “hook point to mounting datum” link of Chapter 23’s chain (worked-example tolerance ±0.02 mm). Moving the hook at assembly can absorb this link, but if it is far off there is not enough adjustment.

In the case study of Section 23.6 the machine “screamed” after the speed increase because the arm resonated; in real machines the hook is also a major noise source, and a hook with a large race clearance gets noisier faster as speed rises. That is why hooks are run in and noise-tested before shipping (Section 24.6).

24.6 The rotary-hook manufacturing process

The hook body and the basket are machined on two lines that meet at the fitting station (Fig. 24-7, Table 24-6). Materials and heat treatment are illustrative of common practice: hook bodies are often made from low-carbon alloy steel and carburised after machining, giving a very hard case over a tough core; some are made from high-carbon chromium bearing steel and through-hardened; baskets are mostly through-hardened bearing-type steels (all illustrative; the drawing governs).

Blanks　The hook body has a complex shape with loads concentrated at the point, so a forged blank is common: the grain flow follows the point and the structure is dense. For small batches or simpler shapes bar stock can be used directly. Baskets are mostly made from bar.

Turning　CNC lathes turn the bores, outside diameters, faces, the mounting bore and the rough race, leaving stock for finish grinding. The mounting bore (which fits the hook shaft) is the datum for every later operation and must be turned in the same setup as the rough race so the two are concentric.

Multi-axis milling　The hook point, the blended surface behind it and the race contour are three-dimensional surfaces, milled on multi-axis (for example five-axis) machining centres. Located on the mounting bore and face, the point and race-related contours are finished in one setup, so the point’s position relative to the mounting datum is set by the machine’s accuracy rather than by stacked errors from several setups. This is a principle that Chapter 25 will stress repeatedly: machine related critical dimensions in one setup wherever possible.

Heat treatment　Carburising heats the part in a carbon-bearing atmosphere so that carbon diffuses into the surface layer, which then hardens on quenching; the low-carbon core stays tough, so the point does not chip easily under impact (a needle strike, for example). Heat treatment brings distortion and size change, so the final race and rib sizes are ground after it. Areas that must not be hard (threaded holes, for example) are masked or left with stock before carburising.

Race grinding and rib grinding　After heat treatment the hook-body race is ground on an internal grinder and the basket rib on an external grinder. These two operations set the clearance and are the most accurate in hook making: the worked example takes both as ±15 μm (σ = 5 μm). In-process gauges measure size during grinding; wheel wear and dressing both shift the mean size, which is exactly what selective assembly in Section 24.7 has to cope with. Ground parts are measured one by one and graded by size, and each measured value is stored against the part number.

Point lapping and polishing　The hook-point edge must be “pointed but not sharp”: pointed, to enter the loop precisely within a tiny clearance; not so sharp that it cuts thread, so the edge is lapped to a small radius, polished to a very low roughness, and must carry no burr at all. Traditionally skilled workers lap the point by hand and check it under a magnifier; it can also be machine-lapped and checked by vision. It is the most craft-dependent operation in hook making and one of the main sources of quality differences between hooks.

Surface treatment　For hooks that must run with little or no oil, the hook body and basket receive a diamond-like carbon (DLC) coating. The manufacturer’s product information cited in Chapter 23 states that a DLC hook has 86% higher surface hardness and 33% less heat generation than a standard hook and needs very little oil (a manufacturer’s product information, in Chinese). The coating is very thin and barely changes dimensions, but it demands very good roughness and cleanliness beforehand. Coating can come before or after grading (parts managed by group, coated, then fitted); the routing in this chapter places it before part inspection.

Selective fitting, assembly, running-in and noise test　Hook bodies and baskets from the same size group are paired and assembled (Section 24.7), the gib is fitted, and free rotation without binding is checked. The assembled hook is run on a running-in rig at a set speed for a set time, so that the microscopic peaks on the mating surfaces wear down (the running-in of Chapter 23), while noise and temperature rise are measured; hooks over the noise limit are opened to inspect race and rib, or refitted with another part from the same group. A final inspection checks clearance, point appearance and dimensions, and records the lot and group number.

24.7 Selective assembly: a tiny clearance from economical accuracy

Chapter 23 solved the hook-point clearance by adjustment at assembly: one adjustable link absorbs the errors of the whole chain. The clearance between hook body and basket has no adjustable link; it is set only by the two parts’ diameters (Fig. 24-8, left):

\[ c = D - d = c_0 + x - y \]

D is the race diameter, d the basket rib diameter, c0 the nominal diametral clearance, and x and y the deviations of the two parts from their nominal sizes. The worked example takes c0 = 20 μm with an allowed range of 10–30 μm (illustrative): the clearance may vary by only ±10 μm.

24.7.1 How accurate must full interchangeability be?

If any hook body can be assembled with any basket (full interchangeability), the clearance’s standard deviation combines both parts:

\[ \sigma_c=\sqrt{\sigma_D^{2}+\sigma_d^{2}} \]

In the example both parts are made to ±15 μm (σ = 5 μm, already a good economical accuracy for a hardened, finish-ground bore and diameter; illustrative). Then σc = 7.07 μm and the chance of the clearance falling within ±10 μm is only about 84%. A 99% in-tolerance rate requires σc ≤ 10/2.576 ≈ 3.88 μm, so each part’s σ must be brought below about 2.7 μm – nearly half. Under the rough rule that “doubling the accuracy doubles the cost” (illustrative), that is a heavy price.

24.7.2 The principle of grouped selective assembly

Grouped selective assembly takes another route: parts are still made to economical accuracy, but every part is measured, sorted into k groups by measured size, and paired only within its group (Fig. 24-8, right). Divide ±15 μm into k equal groups of width w = 30/k μm. Within a group, x and y both lie in the same interval of width w, so |x − y| < w and

\[ c_0 - w \;<\; c \;<\; c_0 + w,\qquad w=\frac{2T}{k} \]

where T = 15 μm is each part’s tolerance. With 3 groups, w = 10 μm and the clearance is always within 10–30 μm, regardless of the parts’ own accuracy. More groups give a tighter clearance. Measurement plus grouping replaces higher machining accuracy – and measuring is far cheaper than making parts more accurately.

There are three costs. First, every part must be measured, and stored and managed by group; the more groups, the more management, and the digital factory must track stock by group number (Section 24.8). Second, measurement error puts some parts in the wrong group: a part near a group boundary may be measured into the neighbouring group, and its assembly may then be out of tolerance. Third, and most important: the two parts must arrive in roughly equal numbers in each group, or the surplus cannot be matched and becomes leftover parts, either held for the next batch or scrapped. The groups are equally filled only when the two parts’ size distributions “look the same” – both means on the tolerance centre and similar σ.

figsrc/ch24_model.py simulates a batch of 2000 sets: 2000 of each part, normally distributed deviations, measurement error σm = 1 μm; parts measured outside ±15 μm are scrapped. Table 24-7 supports several conclusions.

First, selective assembly reaches a high in-tolerance rate with economical accuracy. With 3 groups the rate is 99.6% at 22.3 yuan per set; full interchangeability needs part σ squeezed to 2.6 μm for the same rate, at 39.8 yuan per set, about 80% more.

Second, more groups are not always better. With 2 groups the group width is 15 μm and the clearance can still reach 5 or 35 μm: only 97.9% in tolerance. Three groups are just enough; beyond that the gain is small while management cost and leftovers grow. The rule for choosing k is that the group width w should not exceed the allowed clearance variation (±10 μm here), with a little margin for measurement error.

Third, measurement error eats the margin. Raising the measurement error from 1 μm to 2 μm cuts the 3-group in-tolerance rate from 99.6% to 98.4%. The fitting-station gauges must therefore be much more accurate than the part tolerance – a common rule of thumb is a gauge error of no more than about a tenth of the tolerance (illustrative) – and must be calibrated regularly.

Fourth, and most often overlooked on the shop floor: the two parts’ distributions must match. After the race grinder’s wheel is dressed, the mean moves +4 μm. Pairs within a group are still fine (99.6% in tolerance), but the oversize races crowd into group 3, the group-1 baskets have no races to pair with, leftovers jump to 27% and the cost per set rises to 30.1 yuan (Fig. 24-9d, dark bars). The remedy is not to force pairs across groups but to make the other part follow: move the basket-rib grinding target up by 4 μm too, the groups fill equally again and leftovers drop back to 1.8% (Fig. 24-9d, light bars). The more fundamental remedy, of course, is to bring the race grinding mean back to centre. Unequal σ (6 μm and 4 μm) causes the same problem, with 17% leftovers: the part with the larger σ crowds the end groups, the part with the smaller σ the middle. That is why the two lines feeding the fitting station belong on the same dashboard, and why the two parts’ mean sizes and σ are controlled together (Section 24.8).

Selective assembly, adjustment at assembly and higher accuracy are the three ways of guaranteeing a small clearance. The hook-point clearance of Chapter 23 uses adjustment (there is an adjustable link); the race clearance in this chapter uses grouping (two parts fit directly, with no adjustable link); full interchangeability is used where volumes are highest and accuracy demands are modest. All three coexist in one machine. Which to use depends on whether there is an adjustable link, the batch size, the cost of measurement, and after-sales spares (a selectively fitted assembly has to be replaced as a set).

On page ① of Virtual lab 24-1 you can set both parts’ σ and mean, the number of groups and the measurement error, and see the clearance distribution, group counts, in-tolerance rate and cost per set. Two tasks: reach 99% in tolerance without grouping, by accuracy alone; then reach the same rate with economical accuracy and grouped fitting while keeping leftovers within 3%.

24.8.3 From measurement to historian to control chart

The WenQuest Digital Factory links the whole plant with a unified data bus: every machine and station publishes on topics of the form wq/<plant>/<area>/<unit>/<category>, every message has the same envelope (version, id, time, type, source, mode and data), and the historian subscribes to all topics and stores every message as received; the sewing-machine plant example uses the root topic wq/sewing (Chapters 19, 26 and 27). Measurements use the quality.measurement type, whose required fields are part serial, item, characteristic, nominal, lower and upper tolerance, measured value and result. When the fitting station has measured a hook body it publishes (illustrative):

The envelope specifies only required fields and allows extra ones, so the group number, gauge id and lot can simply be added to data without affecting older components. The needle eye width is published by the vision system beside the punching machine on wq/sewing/quality/ndl-eye/measurement, with part_serial written as “lot–sample number”, since needles are not serialised individually.

With the historian in place, the control charts of Section 24.4 and the fitting statistics of Section 24.7 can be computed automatically (Fig. 24-10): pull measurements by item and characteristic, form subgroups of n in time order, compute X̄, R, control limits and Cpk; count both parts by group number and compute leftovers. When a chart goes out of control, the AI factory assistant raises an alert in the way the gearbox plant already does, with evidence: which machine, which characteristic, from which subgroup, and what else happened at the same time (event messages such as a punch change or wheel dressing). In the gearbox plant’s teaching scenario, students use the control chart and the grinder’s tool-change events to find why a bearing-seat diameter is creeping up; in the needle and hook shop the matching questions are “why has the eye width moved up?” and “why are group-1 baskets piling up?”. Alerts only advise: changing process parameters, replacing a punch or re-targeting basket grinding is still done after a person confirms it, and is recorded as a new revision of the routing or inspection plan.

Shop-floor notes

  • Selectively fitted parts are stored and issued by group number; in the digital factory the group is a dimension of inventory, not just a total.
  • The leftover rate is the health indicator of a fitting line: when it rises, first check whether the two parts’ mean sizes have separated, rather than rushing to pair across groups.
  • Gauges belong in the digital factory too: id, calibration date and result go on the bus and every measurement carries the gauge id, so a problem can be traced to the gauge if that is where it lies.
  • Routings and inspection plans carry revision numbers; any change to a key parameter such as punch-change interval, tempering temperature or number of groups creates a new revision, and lots made under the old one remain traceable.

24.9 Limits of the model

The process flows in this chapter are typical routes organised by process principle; makers differ widely in sequence, grouping and equipment. Heat-treatment temperatures are given only as illustrative ranges, and materials, hardness, plating and coating parameters are governed by the manufacturer’s drawings and process sheets; the DLC figures come from the manufacturer’s product information cited in Chapter 23. The size–stiffness relation considers only blade diameter and ignores the weakening of the section by the taper, groove and eye. The Cpk and control-chart examples assume normally distributed, independent measurements; “smaller is better” characteristics such as straightness and burr height are often skewed, and with few samples the Cpk estimate has a large error, so more data or other methods are needed. ARL was calculated for the single “point outside limits” rule only. The selective-assembly model reduces race and rib to a single diameter each, while the real fit also depends on roundness, race cross-section, thermal expansion and wear; the cost rules (halving σ doubles cost, a group-management charge) are rough illustrative relations; and in practice “leftover” parts can often wait for the next batch, so they are not necessarily lost. The times, machine counts and rates in the shop master data are illustrative, used only to show the format and the method of bottleneck analysis. All of this needs calibration against the plant’s own measured data.

Chapter 24 exercises

  1. At equal length, how many times as stiff in bending is an Nm 80 blade as an Nm 100 blade? Using stiffness, explain why thin needles are unsuitable for heavy denim.
    Answer(0.8/1.0)⁴ ≈ 0.41 times. Stiffness falls with the fourth power of diameter, so a thin needle in thick fabric is more easily pushed aside and bent; a deflected needle hits the hook point and breaks, and the changing hook-point clearance causes skipped stitches.
  2. A needle operation has a blade-diameter specification of 0.900 ± 0.010 mm, process mean 0.903 mm and standard deviation 0.002 mm. Find Cp and Cpk. What is Cpk if the mean is brought back to 0.900 mm?
    AnswerCp = 0.020/0.012 ≈ 1.67; Cpk = (0.910 − 0.903)/0.006 ≈ 1.17; with the mean centred, Cpk = Cp ≈ 1.67.
  3. An X̄ chart uses subgroups of 4. On average, how many subgroups until an alarm when the mean drifts 1.5σ? And for 0.5σ? If a 0.5σ drift should be detected within 20 subgroups on average, what is the minimum subgroup size?
    Answer1.5σ: about 2.0 subgroups; 0.5σ: about 43.9. Trying ARL = 1/[Φ(−3 + 0.5√n) + Φ(−3 − 0.5√n)]: n = 7 gives about 21.4, n = 8 about 17.7, so at least 8 per subgroup (or use supplementary run rules).
  4. The diametral clearance between hook body and basket must be 10–30 μm, nominal 20 μm. Both parts have σ = 4 μm. What is the in-tolerance rate with full interchangeability? With ±15 μm divided into 3 groups, what is the largest possible deviation of the clearance within a group?
    Answerσc = 4√2 ≈ 5.66 μm, in-tolerance rate = 2Φ(10/5.66) − 1 ≈ 92%. With 3 groups the group width is 10 μm, so the clearance deviates by less than ±10 μm within a group; ignoring measurement error, all assemblies are in tolerance.
  5. In Virtual lab 24-1, shift the race grinding mean by +4 μm (3 groups) and watch the leftovers. Explain why the in-tolerance rate barely changes while leftovers rise sharply; give two remedies and compare their pros and cons.
    AnswerThe clearance of a within-group pair depends only on the difference of the deviations inside the group, so the in-tolerance rate barely changes; but the oversize races crowd into the large groups, the two parts no longer arrive in equal numbers, and about 27% are left over. Remedy one: bring the race grinding mean back to centre (the fundamental fix; find the cause, dress or compensate the wheel). Remedy two: move the basket-rib grinding target up by 4 μm as well (quick, but both parts then depart from the drawing nominal, which may affect spare-part interchangeability and needs review).
  6. Why can needles be inspected almost 100%, while hook-point lapping still often relies on manual work? Discuss the difficulties of automation with reference to Table 24-3 and Section 24.6.
  7. (Discussion) Following Section 24.8, write an inspection item for “hook-point position relative to the mounting datum” (a link in Chapter 23’s chain): item, characteristic name, nominal value and tolerances, which work centre measures it, and which topic it is published on.
