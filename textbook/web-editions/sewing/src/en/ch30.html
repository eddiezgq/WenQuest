Chapter 1 described how sewing equipment has passed through mechanisation, electronic control and automation. This chapter looks ahead: what will sewing machines and sewing floors look like over the next ten years? Rather than listing buzzwords, it is better to ask three questions. Which trends are already mature and spreading? Which are still stuck at basic physical and economic thresholds, progressing much more slowly than the publicity suggests? And which technologies are changing “sewing” itself, so that some garments need not be sewn at all? Fig. 30-1 divides the content of this chapter into four directions — more economical, smarter, fewer people, and no sewing; the further down each column, the newer and less mature the item. The “fewer people” column is the one most constrained by the “softness” of fabric and by payback on investment, and it is where this chapter puts most of its effort.

30.1 Direct drive and mechatronics

The energy account As Chapter 13 explained, the old clutch motor idles at rated speed as soon as it is switched on, and drives the machine head through a friction clutch only when the pedal is pressed; a servo motor turns only when needed, and direct drive also eliminates the belt. On the shop floor the most direct effect of this change is lower electricity use (Fig. 30-2). A published retrofit case documents a garment factory in Bangladesh that replaced the clutch motors on 20 lockstitch machines with servo motors: motor power fell from about 450 W to about 250 W (typical values given in the case), electricity use fell by about 45%, about 14 000 kWh was saved per year, and the investment paid back in 4 months (Clean textile partnership case study). A measurement study of a T-shirt line found that direct-drive servo motors ran at a load factor of about 20%, against 38%–51% for belt-driven AC motors; making one T-shirt, the sewing operations used about 0.098 kWh, more than 90% of all processing electricity (Mamun et al., 2024).

Apply the average powers of the case above to a factory with 500 machines, each running 3550 h a year (back-calculated from the case’s annual saving of 14 196 kWh): clutch motors use about 800 000 kWh a year and servos about 440 000 kWh, a saving of about 350 000 kWh a year; at an emission factor of 0.57 kg CO₂/kWh (worked-example value), that is a reduction of about 200 t CO₂. How much is saved depends on the share of switched-on time actually spent sewing: the less time sewing and the more time the machine waits, the more a clutch motor wastes and the more a servo saves.

The software-defined sewing machine Direct drive is only the first step. The electronic feed, electronic tension and electronic presser foot of Chapter 14 hand over motions that used to be determined by cams, links and springs to stepper motors and programs: stitch length, tension and presser-foot pressure all become numbers in a program that can be switched by sewing segment, adjusted automatically to the fabric, or downloaded over the network (Chapter 26). The mechanical structure is getting simpler and the control software more complex; more and more of a sewing machine’s functions are determined by software, and the same head with different programs becomes a different machine. This places new demands on designers: mechanism engineers must understand control, control engineers must understand stitch formation, and the control-box reliability of Chapter 18 and the mechanical accuracy of Chapter 23 matter just as much on such machines.

30.2 From single-machine automation to connected complete lines

The pattern sewers and template machines of Chapters 11 and 15 let one machine complete a pattern on its own; the automatic pocket-welting and pocket-setting machines of Chapter 12 link loading, sewing and stacking into one unit; hanger systems then link work centres into a one-piece flow (Chapter 29). The networking and digital twins of Chapter 26 let managers see the state of every machine and the position of every garment on screen, and the line-balancing calculations of Chapter 28 become live automatic work dispatching in a hanger system. String all of this together and you have what equipment makers call “connected complete lines”: what they sell is no longer individual sewing machines but whole-line solutions from cutting, hanger systems and sewing units to production-management software.

This path is already fairly mature. The difficulty lies less in individual machines than in system integration: equipment from different makers must be able to communicate (the unified data formats of Chapter 26), the factory’s process data must be accurate (the standard minutes of Chapter 28), and operators and managers must know how to use the system. Its payoff is not only fewer people, but more importantly shorter throughput times, less work in progress, and the ability to handle small orders (Chapter 29).

30.3 Case study: why soft fabric is hard for robots to sew

The technical question Car bodies are welded by robots and circuit boards are populated by machines, so why are clothes still basically sewn stitch by stitch by people? The answer starts with the “softness” of cloth.

How soft is cloth? A material’s resistance to bending is measured by its bending rigidity B (per unit width). Fabric bending rigidity is commonly measured by the cantilever method: a strip of cloth is pushed horizontally over the edge of a table, and the length at which it droops to a specified angle — the bending length c — together with the fabric’s mass per unit area gives B. The formula used by fabric-testing instruments is B (μN·m) = 9.8×10⁻⁶ × mass per unit area (g/m²) × c³ (mm) (FAST fabric testing), i.e.

B = w\,c^{3}

where w is the weight per unit area. Worked-example values for several fabrics: single jersey, 150 g/m² with a bending length of 14 mm, B about 4 μN·m; shirt poplin, 120 g/m² and 20 mm, about 9 μN·m; wool suiting about 38 μN·m; denim about 170 μN·m. For comparison, a 1 mm steel sheet has a bending rigidity of about 16.7 N·m and a bending length of about 0.6 m; 0.4 mm card (worked example: elastic modulus about 4 GPa, density 750 kg/m³) has about 0.02 N·m and a bending length of about 0.19 m. The bending rigidity of shirt fabric is less than one millionth of that of a 1 mm steel sheet.

What does this mean in practice? Let each material project 10 cm horizontally from a clamped edge: what happens under its own weight (Fig. 30-3)? The steel sheet and the card hardly move. A fabric projecting twice its bending length already has its chord — the line from the clamp to the free end — dropping by about 42° (the definition behind the cantilever bending-length test; Peirce’s approximation uses 41.5°). At 10 cm, the knit and the shirt fabric hang almost vertically, with chord angles of 82°–86°, and even the stiffest, denim, reaches 65°. When a robot picks up a piece of cloth, the cloth changes shape; pick it up at a slightly different point and the shape is different again. The “grasp–move–place” designed for steel sheet hardly works for cloth. Conversely, clamp a strip vertically and the maximum height it can stand up to under its own weight is about 2c (buckling of a heavy column, Greenhill’s formula l = (7.84B/w)^(1/3)); shirt fabric can stand only about 4 cm tall.

It cannot be pushed Worse still, cloth can hardly be pushed. Take a strip of length L in the pushing direction and width b, clamp it at one end and push it forward, with the other end free to move sideways. Neglecting its own weight, it buckles when the push reaches

F_{\rm cr} = \frac{\pi^{2} B\, b}{4L^{2}}

— the same principle as column buckling (this is the support case most prone to buckling, so the result is a lower bound; with both ends clamped the critical force is 16 times larger). For a strip of shirt fabric 5 cm long in the pushing direction and 30 cm wide, F_cr is only about 2.8 mN, less than one sixth of the strip’s own weight (about 18 mN). By this formula, for the push to exceed the strip’s weight the bending length must exceed about 0.74 L: to push a 5 cm strip you need c > 37 mm, stiffer than denim. Moreover, a horizontal strip already sags under its own weight and is not a straight column, so in practice it is even harder to push (the left of Fig. 30-4 shows the vertical, standing case). That is why sewing machines never “push” cloth: the throat plate supports it from below, the presser foot holds it from above, and the feed dog “drags” it through tooth by tooth from below (Chapter 6); in hand sewing, too, a person’s two hands pull and smooth the cloth, one in front and one behind.

What can robots do? Researchers and companies have tried many approaches (right of Fig. 30-4). One family improves grasping: needle grippers drive fine needles obliquely into the cloth to hook it, suitable for thicker, bulky fabrics; vacuum grippers suck the cloth onto a table or suction cup, but cloth is porous and needs a large air flow, and taking only the top ply from a stack is especially hard; electroadhesive grippers hold thin cloth with electrode plates; cryo-grippers spray a little water and freeze the cloth onto a cold plate. Another family changes the cloth: one company soaks the fabric in a water-soluble resin so that it temporarily behaves like sheet material, handles and sews it with ordinary industrial robot arms, and washes out the resin afterwards (public description); no large-scale use has been seen in public sources. A third family never lets the cloth leave the table: a US company’s sewing robot uses cameras and sensors to recognise the fabric pieces in real time and continually adjusts their direction as they pass the sewing head, much like a skilled operator’s hands (report on sewing-robot progress). Academic research on cloth manipulation is also very active, but a review concludes that, although grasping, unfolding and folding have progressed in the laboratory, they are still some way from stable industrial use (review of cloth-manipulation research).

A plan that never materialised In 2017 the sewing-robot company mentioned above and a Chinese apparel company announced a factory in Arkansas, USA, with 21 fully automatic T-shirt lines, “one T-shirt every 22 seconds” and “800 000 pieces a day”, at a cost of 33 cents a piece and only 3–5 people per line (report at the time). These numbers do not add up on their own: one piece every 22 seconds per line, running 24 hours a day, gives only about 3900 pieces, or about 82 000 for 21 lines — short of 800 000 by nearly a factor of ten; another description from the same period gave about 1.25 million pieces per line per year (industry report, 2017), or about 3400 a day, about 72 000 for 21 lines. When reading reports on new technology, cross-checking a few of the numbers against each other often reveals the problem. The plan was not realised; a 2025 trade-press report said that the company, while raising a new funding round, acknowledged that running at high speed 24 hours a day, 7 days a week with a yield above 98% was still very hard, and that different cotton batches and dye lots each required stopping to recalibrate (report on sewing-robot progress).

Conclusion Sewing robots are hard because of the cloth: it has almost no bending rigidity, cannot be pushed, cannot stand up, and its properties differ from batch to batch. The more workable routes at present are to keep the cloth on the table and correct its position with vision and actuators (exactly the route taken by automatic units and template machines, Chapters 12 and 15), or to automate first the products with simple shapes and large volumes (such as T-shirts and towels), rather than having robots hold and manipulate cloth in their hands as people do.

30.4 Technical thresholds for humanoid robots on the sewing floor

Progress in 2026 In 2026 a Chinese robotics company signed an agreement with a sewing-equipment company to deploy 2000 customised humanoid robots in garment factories, handling fabric transport, stacking, unloading, and alignment of collars and pockets, with a cycle time under 10 seconds per motion and an alignment accuracy of about 2 mm (report on the Zhejiang provincial government website). Another Xinhua report described a different robotics company with more specific figures: its robot separates fabric plies (picking one ply at a time) with a success rate of about 97%, about 98% of the sewing it feeds meets the quality standard, and at current efficiency the payback period is about 18 months. But the company’s head also said that “humanoid robots are still at the learning stage: they can do a single operation well, but cannot yet run a whole production flow without human involvement”, comparing handling cloth to “picking up tofu with chopsticks: grip too hard and it deforms, too lightly and you can’t hold it.” He expected single operations to be validated before 2028 and multi-operation coordination to begin in 2028–2030 (Xinhua, 2026).

The nth power of the single-step success rate “Good at single operations, unable to run the whole flow” can be explained with the simplest of formulas (Fig. 30-5). To take a panel from the stack to a finished seam, the robot must perform n motions in sequence — separating the ply, lifting, smoothing, aligning, loading and so on — each with success rate p. Assuming the steps are independent, the probability of completing the whole flow without human intervention is

P = p^{\,n}

With 97% per step and 10 steps, P = 0.97¹⁰ ≈ 74%: one piece in four needs a person to deal with it. For a 10-step flow to complete without people 90% of the time, every step must reach about 99% (the tenth root of 0.9). Suppose each failure takes a person 30 s to handle before the step is redone, and each step takes 8 s: at 97% per step a person must intervene about 12 times an hour, at 99% about 4 times, and at 99.9% fewer than 0.5 times. Only when the single-step success rate exceeds 99% can the robot work without someone standing beside it.

Doing the sums Suppose a robot costs 250 000 yuan (worked-example value), annual maintenance is 10% of the price, it replaces one operator per shift (with equivalent output), the factory works two shifts, and labour costs 0.5 yuan/min (including social insurance and overheads). The robot saves about 144 000 yuan of labour a year; after deducting the labour for handling failures and maintenance, the payback period is about 29 months at 97% per step and about 26 months at 99%. Switch to three shifts and the payback falls to around 18 months — close to the reported figure, but the inputs here are worked-example values and cannot be used to confirm each other. In this set of worked-example values, shifts and price affect payback more than success rate does: the more hours a day the robot works, the better it pays. Yet “replaces one operator” is itself an optimistic assumption: the robot in the model makes about 39 pieces an hour, not compared with a skilled operator’s speed, and robots are often slower than skilled operators at the same motions.

What other thresholds remain? The problems listed in the reports correspond one-to-one with the analysis earlier in this chapter. First, generalisation: a different fabric or style requires relearning — a direct result of the huge variation in fabric properties. Second, perception: reflections and dust interfere with vision. Third, durability and heat dissipation: the floor runs two or three shifts a day, and the robot must run continuously and reliably (the reliability issues of Chapter 18 apply here too). Fourth, there are no unified interface or safety standards: how robots communicate with sewing machines and hanger systems, and how people and robots are kept safe on the same floor, are still being worked out (Chapter 26). The advantage of the humanoid form is that it can use existing work centres and equipment directly, without rebuilding the floor for robots; the price is the complexity of two legs, two arms and two hands. For many fixed operations, a dedicated loading mechanism or a collaborative robot arm may be simpler and more reliable. Where humanoid robots will finally gain a foothold depends on whether their single-step success rate, speed and price can all pass muster at the same time.

30.5 Garments without sewing

Another route is to bypass sewing altogether (Fig. 30-6). Chapters 17 and 29 covered whole-garment knitting: the sweater is knitted complete on the flat machine, eliminating cutting, linking and seaming — but it applies only to knits, machine times are long and programming is difficult.

In sportswear, underwear and outdoor clothing, two other joining methods are increasingly common. Ultrasonic welding uses high-frequency vibration to melt synthetic fibres locally at the seam, which fuse as they cool; the seam is flat, has no needle holes and can be waterproof, but it requires a high content of thermoplastic fibre — pure cotton cannot be welded. Hot-melt tape bonding uses heat and pressure to melt an adhesive film between two layers of cloth; it is used for seamless underwear, tight-fitting sportswear and the taped seams of waterproof jackets. It is less demanding about fibre type, but temperature, pressure and time must be strictly controlled (the same kind of process as the fusing of suit interlinings described in Chapter 29).

These methods will not make the sewing machine disappear. Sewn seams are elastic, breathable and repairable, and suit the widest range of fabrics and styles; the vast majority of garments will still be sewn. But in certain categories they replace part of the sewing, and they also create new demands on equipment: welding machines, bonding machines and sewing machines often appear on the same line, and equipment designers must be familiar with a wider range of processes.

30.6 Digital printing and green manufacturing

Most of the environmental burden of apparel production lies in spinning, weaving and dyeing/finishing; sewing itself uses little energy. The study cited above measured about 0.105 kWh of electricity for processing one T-shirt (spreading, cutting, sewing, pressing), of which sewing accounted for about 0.098 kWh — about 60 g CO₂ at Bangladesh’s grid factor of about 0.57 kg CO₂/kWh (Mamun et al., 2024). Still, sewing equipment and floors can do several things: save electricity with direct-drive servos (Section 30.1); use low-oil and oil-free hooks and lubrication (Chapters 5, 8 and 23) to reduce oil-stained rejects and oily waste fabric; and reduce noise to improve operators’ working conditions (Chapters 8 and 23).

An important change in dyeing and finishing is digital printing: like an inkjet printer, it jets dye directly onto the fabric in the pattern, with no screen to make for each colour. A comparative study at a textile mill in Peru printed the same 16-colour design on 1000 m of cotton fabric by digital and by rotary-screen printing: digital printing used 36% less water, 35% less electricity, 28% fewer chemicals and 26% less gas, produced 41% less wastewater and 70% less fabric waste, and almost no chemical waste (Hoque et al., 2024, Fig. 30-7). More importantly, digital printing has almost no set-up waste when the design changes, making it well suited to small batches and on-demand production. It points in the same direction as the small-batch quick response and on-demand production of Chapter 29: take the order first, then produce, and make fewer garments that cannot be sold — which may be the apparel industry’s biggest “green” gain.

30.7 Limits of the model

The energy-saving calculation in this chapter uses the average powers from one retrofit case; actual savings depend on the share of switched-on time spent sewing, the motor model and operating habits. Fabric bending rigidity was converted with the cantilever formula from worked-example mass per unit area and bending length; real fabrics differ in stiffness along the warp, weft and bias, and their stiffness also changes with humidity. Both the cantilever model and the push-buckling model treat cloth as a uniform elastic thin plate, ignoring shear, friction and hysteresis; the push-buckling formula uses the support case most prone to buckling and neglects self-weight. The flow-success model assumes the steps are independent and that failures can be redone; in the payback model, the robot price, wages, maintenance and the “replaces one operator” assumption are all worked-example values. Figures in the reports reflect the situation at the time of reporting; new technology changes quickly, so check the latest sources when using them.

Shop-floor notes

  • On floors still using clutch motors, switching to direct-drive servos usually pays back within a few months; the longer the machines are switched on and the smaller the share of time spent sewing, the better it pays.
  • When judging an automation proposal, first ask where the cloth is and what holds it: a scheme in which the cloth stays on the table throughout is far more reliable than one that holds the cloth in the air.
  • When watching a robot demonstration, ask for the single-step and whole-flow success rates, and whether it must be readjusted for a different fabric.
  • When reading reports on new technology, first cross-check a few of the numbers in the report against each other.
  • Welding, bonding and whole-garment knitting are replacing sewing in some categories; when selecting equipment, compare them alongside sewing machines.
