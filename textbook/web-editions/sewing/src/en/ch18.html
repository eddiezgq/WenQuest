Chapter 18Designing, Building and Testing the Control Box

Chapter 13 explained how servo motors, solenoids, encoders and controllers work, and Chapters 14 to 17 showed how the various machines use them to choreograph their motions. All of those chapters quietly assumed that the control box itself is reliable: it works when powered, its parameters do not drift, and it lasts ten years. Yet a control box sits under the table top in a sewing room. In summer it faces shop temperatures of 30–40 °C, fabric lint drifts around it, high-frequency pressing and welding equipment may be running next to it, and the main shaft starts and stops more than a thousand times a day. What lets it run stably for ten years in that environment is a series of less conspicuous tasks in design, manufacturing and testing.

This chapter uses a generic servo control box for a lockstitch machine as its worked example (single-phase 220 V, 550 W, with a trimming solenoid and others; all parameters are illustrative values) and follows three threads: how the heat generated in the power devices determines junction temperature and service life; how the electromagnetic interference produced by switching spreads and how it is suppressed; and how the boxes are tested and burned in during manufacture so that defective units stay in the factory. Whether the switching devices switch fast or slow affects both heating and interference, and this trade-off is what ties the first two threads together. Content specific to individual machine types is left to Chapters 14 to 17; this chapter covers only what all control systems have in common.

18.1 Opening: the operating conditions of a control box

Open the manual of a lockstitch servo control and the specification table reads: power supply single-phase 200–240 V, permissible fluctuation ±10%; ambient temperature 5–45 °C, no direct sunlight; relative humidity 30%–95%, non-condensing; the unit must be reliably earthed through the green-yellow earth wire, a residual-current device must be installed, and the unit must be kept away from high-frequency equipment (a manufacturer’s servo control manual). Another manual allows a supply fluctuation of ±20%, a rated power of 550 W and a speed of 200–4000 r/min (a manufacturer’s control manual (in Chinese)).

Behind these numbers are several requirements that the designer must meet at once. When the voltage drops to 180 V the motor must still deliver enough torque; when it rises to 265 V no component may break down. In a 45 °C workshop the inside of the box runs another ten-odd degrees hotter, and both the power transistors and the electrolytic capacitors must cope with that. Lint gets into every gap and blocks the cooling paths, and once damp it can create leakage paths on the circuit board. When an operator in synthetic clothing touches the control panel on a dry winter day, a discharge of several thousand volts must not crash the controller. The fault-code table at the end of the manual — overvoltage, undervoltage, overcurrent, motor stall, encoder fault, solenoid overcurrent — lists the ways the controller protects itself under these conditions.

A control box like this is usually developed according to the “V-model”. The left leg runs downward, from requirements to system architecture and then to detailed design of the circuits, mechanical structure and software; the right leg runs upward, from testing circuit boards and software modules, through functional and environmental tests of the complete unit, to final validation against the requirements. The two legs correspond level by level: every level of design must have a matching verification. The following sections walk along this path.

18.2 Requirements, standards and architecture

Requirements　Requirements fall into three groups. Functional requirements state what the box must do: what motor power it drives, how many solenoids and pneumatic valves it handles, what kind of pedal, control panel and sensors it connects to, and whether it needs networking. Performance requirements state how well it must do it: maximum speed, needle-positioning accuracy, thread-trimming timing (Chapters 13 and 14). Environmental and life requirements state under what conditions: voltage range, temperature and humidity, dust protection, immunity to interference, and the design life in years and power-on hours per year. The worked example in this chapter assumes two-shift operation, about 4800 h of power-on time per year.

Standards　The safety requirements for industrial sewing machines have their own international standard, ISO 10821, which hands the electrical requirements over to IEC 60204-1 (general requirements for the electrical equipment of machines) and to IEC 60204-31, which is specific to sewing machines (scope and normative references of ISO 10821). In China, IEC 60204-31 has been adopted identically as GB/T 5226.31-2017 (Electrical safety of machinery — Electrical equipment of machines — Part 31: Particular safety and EMC requirements for sewing machines, units and systems) (National Public Service Platform for Standards Information (in Chinese)). The publicly available part of it contains several provisions that bear directly on design: control circuits shall use safety extra-low voltage (SELV or PELV, clause 9.1.1); machines started with a hold-to-run control device such as a pedal (“release to stop”), and machines such as bartackers and buttonholers that automatically complete a short cycle, may omit the emergency-stop button (clause 10.7.4); and the EMC emission and immunity tests refer to CISPR 11 and the various standards of the IEC 61000 series (Annex AA) (IEC 60204-31 sample pages). ISO 10821 further requires that the machine must not start unexpectedly if the pedal is pressed accidentally while the power has not been disconnected or while the machine is still coasting. These clauses determine how the circuits are partitioned and what checks the software must perform at power-up and at stop.

Architecture　Fig. 18-1 shows how the control box is built up. On the left is the power section: the mains input passes through a fuse and an EMI filter into the rectifier bridge and becomes a DC link of about 310 V (Chapter 13), smoothed by the DC-link capacitors; an intelligent power module (IPM — six switching devices, freewheeling diodes and gate-drive and protection circuits in one package) forms the inverter bridge that drives the motor; energy fed back by the motor during an emergency stop or deceleration is dissipated by the braking circuit. In the middle is a switch-mode power supply that draws from the DC link and produces 5 V and 3.3 V for the control electronics and roughly 24 V or 30 V DC for the solenoids. On the right is the low-voltage section: the main controller chip reads the encoder and pedal, computes the control loops and issues the PWM and solenoid commands; the control panel and communication interfaces are also on this side.

Sufficient creepage and clearance distances must be kept between the power section and the low-voltage section. PWM signals and current-sense signals cross the isolation barrier through optocouplers or isolator chips, and the transformer of the switch-mode supply forms another isolation barrier. In this way the panel, pedal connector and communication ports that the operator can touch are all at safety extra-low voltage, and they remain within the SELV range even in the event of a single fault. The power module is mounted on an aluminium heat sink, which is often the enclosure of the control box or part of it; heat-sensitive parts such as the electrolytic capacitors and the switch-mode-supply transformer should be placed well away from it.

18.3 Power stage and thermal design

Where the losses come from　Each switching device in the inverter bridge has two kinds of loss. The first is conduction loss: when current flows through a conducting device there is a voltage drop of about 1 V across it plus a resistive drop, so the loss is roughly one term proportional to the current plus one proportional to its square. The second is switching loss: every transition from off to on and from on to off passes through an interval in which voltage and current are both non-zero, and the power during that interval is very high. The average switching loss is approximately

P_{sw}\approx\frac{1}{2}\,V_{dc}\,\bar I\,t_{sw}\,f_{sw}

where V_dc is the DC-link voltage; Ī is the average of the current at which one device switches over one current cycle — for a sinusoidal phase current with RMS value I, Ī = √2 I/π; t_sw is the sum of one turn-on and one turn-off transition time; and f_sw is the switching frequency. The faster the device switches (the smaller t_sw) and the lower the frequency, the smaller the switching loss. The freewheeling diodes also have conduction losses and reverse-recovery losses. In the rectifier bridge, one pair of the four diodes conducts in each half mains cycle, and because of the DC-link capacitors they conduct only briefly near the voltage peak, carrying large current pulses; the bridge loss is roughly proportional to the average input current.

With the parameters of this chapter’s worked example (on-state voltage drop 1.0 V, on-resistance 0.1 Ω, t_sw = 300 ns, switching frequency 12 kHz, all illustrative values), the whole power stage dissipates about 20 W at a motor phase current of 3 A (RMS); during starts and stops the current rises to 9 A and the loss to about 74 W. The loss grows faster than the current because the conduction loss contains a term in the square of the current (Fig. 18-2, right).

Thermal-resistance network　Heat leaves the chip (the junction) of a switching device, passes through the package and the thermal interface layer to the heat sink, then from the heat sink to the air inside the box, and finally through the enclosure into the workshop. Each stage can be represented by a thermal resistance in K/W: the temperature difference across it when 1 W of heat flows through. Temperature behaves like voltage and heat flow like current, so the junction temperature equals the ambient temperature plus the temperature rises across all the thermal resistances (Fig. 18-2, left). The worked example takes 3.5 K/W from junction to heat sink for each device and 1.5 K/W from heat sink to the air inside the box (no fan; the aluminium enclosure doubles as the heat sink); the air inside the box is hotter than the workshop by 0.6 K/W × total heat dissipation; and the other circuits (switch-mode supply, main controller, solenoid drivers) dissipate 8 W.

Sewing is an intermittent load　If the control box ran continuously at 9 A, the calculation would put the heat sink above 200 °C — the machine simply could not be built. It works only because sewing is a typical intermittent load: a seam takes a few seconds, then the operator handles the fabric and changes pieces while the main shaft stands still; each seam begins with an acceleration and ends with a deceleration, and the current is highest during those few tenths of a second. The heat sink and the box have large thermal capacities, with time constants measured in minutes, so their temperatures are set by the average loss over a period of time. The chip is tiny, and the time constant from chip to heat sink is a few tenths of a second or less (the worked example takes 0.05 s, which is conservative), so within the 0.15 s of one acceleration the junction temperature climbs close to its steady-state value for that current. Thermal design must therefore be considered on two levels: the average loss determines the heat-sink temperature, and the peak current determines how far the junction rises above the heat sink (Fig. 18-3).

The worked example’s duty cycle is 30 starts and stops per minute with sewing half of the time; each acceleration and each deceleration takes 0.15 s at 9 A, and the rest of the sewing time runs at 3 A. The average loss is then about 18 W, of which acceleration and deceleration, though they occupy only 15% of the time, contribute about 60% of the heat. In a 40 °C workshop the air inside the box is about 56 °C, the heat sink about 83 °C and the peak junction temperature during acceleration about 110 °C; when the workshop reaches 45 °C, the peak junction temperature is about 115 °C. The maximum junction temperature of switching devices is generally 150 °C; design leaves a margin by derating, and this chapter takes 125 °C as the design limit, while a temperature sensor on the heat sink raises an alarm at 100 °C (illustrative value). So this design still has a margin of about 10 °C at 45 °C — not a large one.

The more frequent the starts and stops, the larger the share of time spent accelerating and decelerating, and the higher the average loss and temperatures; operations with many short seams (such as setting pockets or attaching labels) run hotter than those with long seams (such as joining seams). For this reason some controls automatically limit acceleration or reduce the maximum speed when the heat sink gets too hot, preferring to run a little slower rather than stop on an overheating alarm.

18.4 Electromagnetic compatibility

Where interference comes from　The control box is itself a source of interference. The switching devices of the inverter bridge switch tens of thousands of times per second, and each time the voltage jumps from 0 to 310 V within tens to hundreds of nanoseconds; when a solenoid is switched off its coil produces a very high back-EMF (the Zener diode or freewheeling diode of Chapter 13 is there to absorb it); and the switch-mode supply also switches rapidly at tens of kilohertz. On the other hand, the control box must work properly in the presence of interference from others: the mains carries voltage dips and surges caused by other equipment starting and stopping, and fast transient bursts from contactors and motors, and in winter a person’s static charge can reach several thousand volts.

Common-mode current　When a switching device switches rapidly, the rate of voltage change dv/dt is very large. There is parasitic capacitance between the motor windings and the earthed motor frame, and between the conductors and the shield of the motor cable; each time the voltage jumps, a current pulse flows through these capacitances to earth and returns through the earth wire and the mains. This is called common-mode current, and its magnitude is roughly

i_{cm}\approx C_{p}\,\frac{dv}{dt}

The worked example takes a parasitic capacitance of 300 pF and, as a teaching simplification, a voltage rise time of half of t_sw: 310 V rising in 150 ns gives a peak common-mode current of about 0.6 A. In real IGBT modules the voltage often rises faster still, at several thousand to more than ten thousand volts per microsecond. The path this current takes is long and acts like an antenna, radiating the interference; and as it flows through the earth conductors it makes the “ground” potential of the main controller board bounce, disturbing the encoder signals and communications (Fig. 18-4, left).

What the standards require　The EMC annex of IEC 60204-31 lists the tests to be performed. For emissions: the conducted and radiated emission limits of CISPR 11, and harmonics and voltage flicker per IEC 61000-3-2 and 61000-3-3. For immunity: IEC 61000-4-2 electrostatic discharge, 61000-4-3 radiated RF electromagnetic fields, 61000-4-4 electrical fast transients/bursts, 61000-4-5 surges, 61000-4-6 conducted RF disturbances, and 61000-4-11 voltage dips and short interruptions (IEC 60204-31 sample pages). The specific test levels and performance criteria must be taken from the original text of the standards.

Countermeasures　There are broadly three ways to suppress interference: reduce it at the source, intercept it along the path, and strengthen the immunity of the victim. At the source, the switching transitions can be slowed a little; dv/dt falls, and with it the peak of the common-mode current and its high-frequency content. However, the charge C·V that flows through the parasitic capacitance at each transition does not change, so conducted interference in the range from a few hundred kilohertz to a few megahertz improves little and must still be handled mainly by filters. Along the path, a filter made of a common-mode choke and X and Y capacitors is fitted at the power inlet to return the common-mode current locally instead of letting it escape into the mains; the motor cable is shielded, with the shield earthed over a large area at the control-box end; on the circuit board the loop areas of the power circuits are kept as small as possible, and power ground and signal ground are separated and joined at a single point. On the victim side, the encoder and pedal signals use differential transmission or filtering, exposed metal parts of the panel are earthed, and ESD protection devices are added at the interfaces. In software a watchdog timer is provided: if the program runs away, the watchdog resets the main controller. During and after a reset the PWM pins are pulled low by hardware and the enable input of the power module is held off, so that the motor is guaranteed to stop, rather than relying on a program that has already run away to switch the outputs off.

The switching-speed trade-off　Slower switching reduces interference, but switching loss rises in proportion to t_sw and the junction temperature goes up. Fig. 18-4 (right) plots both curves together: in a 45 °C workshop, increasing t_sw from 300 ns to 600 ns halves the peak common-mode current but raises the peak junction temperature from about 115 °C to about 133 °C, beyond the design limit; conversely, shortening it to 100 ns lowers the junction temperature to about 104 °C but raises the common-mode current to about 1.9 A, which is hard for the filter to handle. With this chapter’s illustrative criteria (junction temperature no more than 125 °C, peak common-mode current no more than 0.8 A), t_sw can only be chosen between about 235 and 470 ns. Lowering the switching frequency reduces switching loss, but the current ripple grows and the electromagnetic noise from the motor becomes more shrill. The human ear hears up to about 20 kHz, so frequencies in the low teens of kilohertz are still at the upper end of the audible range; to be completely inaudible the frequency must be raised to 16–20 kHz or more, and the switching loss rises accordingly. Sewing-machine servos therefore generally switch at ten-odd kilohertz, a compromise between noise and loss (Task 2 of Virtual lab 18-1 asks you to raise it to 20 kHz and strike the balance again). This is how EMC and thermal design constrain each other; they must be considered together.

18.5 Embedded software

Layers　Control software is usually divided into four layers (Fig. 18-5, left). The bottom layer is the driver layer, which operates the chip’s peripherals directly: PWM, analogue-to-digital conversion, encoder interface, serial ports and memory. Above it is the motion-control layer, which runs the current, velocity and position loops of Chapter 13. Above that is the process layer — the motion sequences described in Chapters 14 to 17: start backtack, thread trimming, thread wiping, colour change, pattern interpolation. At the top is the user-interface and communication layer, which handles the control panel, parameter settings and networking (Chapter 26). The lower the layer, the faster it runs and the stricter its timing: the current loop is computed about every 0.1 ms inside the PWM interrupt; the velocity and position loops run more slowly; the process layer acts on main-shaft angle or millisecond timers; and refreshing the user interface every few tens of milliseconds is enough. The benefit of layering is that changing one layer does not affect the others: the same motion control can be combined with different process layers to make the controls for a lockstitch machine, an overlock machine or a programmable pattern sewer.

Protection and fault codes　A large part of the software’s work is protection. DC-link overvoltage or undervoltage, current above its limit, motor stall, loss of encoder signal, heat-sink overtemperature and abnormal solenoid current must all be detected by software and handled according to severity: some cases first limit the speed and carry on working, others immediately switch off the outputs, stop the machine and display a fault code. At power-up the software must also check that the pedal is in its neutral position and that the machine head is not tilted back (safety switch), so that the machine cannot start turning as soon as it is powered — this is how the ISO 10821 requirement to prevent unexpected start-up is implemented in software. The fault-code table is printed in the manual and is also the service technician’s first-hand clue to the problem.

Parameter storage　User settings such as stitch counts, speeds and trimming parameters, as well as factory calibrations such as the motor zero position and the current-sense offsets, are kept in non-volatile memory. This kind of memory has a limited number of erase/write cycles, so the software cannot write every time a value changes; common practice is to write with a delay after a parameter is changed, or to rotate writes among several memory blocks. Each parameter set carries a checksum that is verified at power-up; if corruption is found, the defaults are restored and an alarm is raised, instead of running with garbled parameters.

Firmware updates　After a machine has left the factory, software problems found or functions to be added are dealt with by updating the firmware. The worst thing that can happen during an update is a power failure halfway through: a half-written program is neither the old version nor the new one, and the machine will never start again. The memory is therefore often divided into a bootloader and two program areas (Fig. 18-5, right). The new firmware is first written completely into the spare area, and only after it has been verified (checksum; some designs also check a digital signature to prevent unauthorised programs from being loaded) is the “which area to boot” flag switched over. At restart the bootloader first checks that area’s checksum and, if it is wrong, falls back to the original area. A program with a correct checksum may still hang as soon as it runs, so the first start of a new version counts only as a “trial run”, which the application confirms once it is working normally; if the watchdog resets the controller before confirmation, the bootloader rolls back to the old version. The bootloader itself is very small and is never changed after the unit leaves the factory.

Software testing　Software is also tested according to the V-model. Each module is first tested on its own — for example, the trimming-sequence module is fed a series of main-shaft angles and checked to see that it issues its commands at the specified angles. Testing then moves to a hardware-in-the-loop rig: the real controller is connected to a simulator that emulates the motor, encoder and solenoids, made to “sew” thousands upon thousands of times, and deliberately subjected to faults such as power failures, encoder disconnection and voltage dips to check how the software responds. Many faults that are hard to reproduce on a real machine can be tried again and again on the rig.

18.6 Production test and burn-in

Test flow　In control-box production, manufacturing and testing are interleaved (Fig. 18-6, top). After SMT placement and soldering, automated optical inspection (AOI) first photographs each board to check for wrong or skewed components and solder bridges. Next comes in-circuit test (ICT): the board is pressed onto a “bed of nails” covered in probes, and the values of resistors and capacitors and the polarity of diodes are measured one by one to find soldering and component problems. The program is then flashed and a functional test (FCT) is performed: on a test fixture the board is connected to a simulated motor load, encoder signals, pedal and solenoids, and made to run through all its functions according to a test program while currents, voltages and other data are recorded. Once assembled into a control box, the unit is burned in under power; after burn-in it receives another functional test and an electrical safety test — continuity of the protective earth, insulation resistance and withstand voltage (the tests specified in IEC 60204-1) — and is finally fitted to a sewing machine for running-in of the complete machine. Every circuit board carries a barcode, and the test data are archived by barcode, so that if a batch later develops a problem, its component lots and test records can be traced.

The bathtub curve　Why burn in? The failure rate of electronic products over time looks like a bathtub (Fig. 18-6, bottom). For an initial period the failure rate is relatively high: these are early (infant-mortality) failures, caused by poor solder joints, internal component defects and assembly damage, which are not necessarily revealed by factory inspection. Then follows a long period of random failures with a low, roughly constant failure rate; finally the components age and wear out, and the failure rate rises again. Burn-in means letting the control box be “used” in the factory for a while first, so that the early failures show up before shipment.

Choosing burn-in conditions　At room temperature it would take a very long time for early defects to reveal themselves, so burn-in is generally done at elevated temperature, with the control box under load and with power cycling. Higher temperature accelerates the failure mechanisms; the acceleration factor is commonly estimated with the Arrhenius relation:

AF=\exp\left[\frac{E_a}{k}\left(\frac{1}{T_{use}}-\frac{1}{T_{bi}}\right)\right]

where E_a is the activation energy (0.7 eV in the worked example), k is the Boltzmann constant, temperatures are in kelvin, and T_bi is the temperature of the components during burn-in (not the temperature of the burn-in chamber). The worked example takes the components at about 55 °C in use and at about 85 °C with the burn-in chamber at 70 °C; the acceleration factor is about 8, so 8 h of burn-in is equivalent to 64 h of use.

The worked example assumes that 1% of the control boxes in each batch carry early defects, whose times to failure follow a Weibull distribution with shape parameter 0.5 (failure rate decreasing with time), and that good units have a random failure rate of one per million per hour (all illustrative values). Without burn-in, the first-year return rate is about 1.5%, two-thirds of it from early defects; with 8 h of burn-in at 70 °C, about half of the defective boxes fail in the factory and the first-year return rate falls to about 0.9%. The longer the burn-in, the more thorough the screening, but burn-in chambers, electricity and the time tied up are all costs; the higher the burn-in temperature, the greater the acceleration, but it must not exceed the components’ rated temperatures or let the burn-in itself damage good units. 18.7 Case study: a control box that keeps reporting overtemperature in summer

The technical problem　A certain model of lockstitch control box had been on the market for several years. It rarely gave trouble in spring and autumn, but every summer several factories one after another reported machines frequently displaying the overtemperature fault code and working again after a short rest; some of the control boxes returned for repair also had bulging DC-link capacitors. Workshops in factories of this kind are often not air-conditioned, and on summer afternoons the shop temperature can reach 38 °C. This is a generic case constructed from first principles; all figures are illustrative values.

Analysis　First, use the model of Section 18.3 to check the design operating point. At 38 °C a clean control box has a heat sink at about 81 °C and a peak junction temperature of about 108 °C, which leaves margin both to the 100 °C alarm threshold and to the 125 °C design limit. The design itself is sound — so how do the returned units differ from new ones? Opening a returned unit reveals a layer of lint plastered between the heat-sink fins and over the ventilation holes of the enclosure, and some boxes had been mounted under the table top right against the machine stand or a wall, with no air circulating around them. The lint acts like a quilt: it increases the thermal resistance of the heat sink, and the air inside the box gets hotter too. Expressing this effect as a degree of blockage (0 is clean, 1 is severely blocked; in the model the heat-sink thermal resistance is scaled by 1 + 1.5 × blockage and the internal temperature rise by 1 + blockage, illustrative): at a blockage of 0.3 and 38 °C the heat sink is about 97 °C, close to the alarm threshold; at a blockage of 0.5 it is about 109 °C, already above the alarm threshold, and the peak junction temperature is about 136 °C, beyond the design limit as well (Fig. 18-7, left). The overtemperature alarm is the protection doing its job: it correctly stopped the machine.

The bulging capacitors are another consequence of the same cause. The electrolyte in an aluminium electrolytic capacitor slowly evaporates, and its life depends strongly on temperature. Engineers commonly estimate it with the “10-degree rule”: for every 10 °C drop in temperature, the life roughly doubles (a capacitor manufacturer’s technical notes).

L=L_{0}\times2^{(T_{0}-T)/10}

The DC-link capacitor in the worked example is rated 105 °C, 2000 h, and its core runs 5 °C hotter than the air inside the box. In a clean control box in a 38 °C workshop the capacitor is at about 59 °C and its life is about 50,000 h, or about 10 years at 4800 h per year; at a blockage of 0.5 the inside of the box is hotter, the capacitor is at about 66 °C and its life is about 6 years; at a blockage of 0.8 it is at about 71 °C and only about 4.4 years remain (Fig. 18-7, right). The summer heat lasts only a few months, so the actual life will be somewhat longer, but the trend is the same: every 10 °C hotter halves the life. Bulging in control boxes that have been in service for several years in an overheated environment is consistent with this. If new machines bulge quickly, look for faster causes — for example ripple current far above the design value heating the capacitor itself, a capacitor placed right against the heat sink and being baked, or DC-link overvoltage during braking. Lives extrapolated with the 10-degree rule from capacitor manufacturers’ data are generally capped (Fig. 18-7, right, is truncated at 30 years); the rule cannot be extrapolated indefinitely to low temperatures.

Improvements　Several measures can be used together. First, state in the operating manual and the maintenance procedures that lint must be cleaned regularly from the heat-sink fins and ventilation holes, and that space must be left around the control box. Second, change the structure: make the control box sealed, with the power module mounted on an external heat sink whose fins are more widely spaced and run vertically, so that lint does not easily catch on them and can be brushed off easily (in the worked example this lowers the heat-sink thermal resistance from 1.5 K/W to 1.0 K/W; at 38 °C and a blockage of 0.5 the heat sink drops to about 93 °C and no longer alarms). Third, add overtemperature derating in software: when the heat-sink temperature approaches the alarm threshold, first limit the acceleration and maximum speed. Fourth, switch to capacitors rated 105 °C, 5000 h or even longer life, or move the capacitors further from the heat sink. Coating the circuit board with conformal coating (a protective coating against moisture, dust and mould) also prevents lint from causing leakage on the board once it becomes damp.

Discussion　This case shows that thermal-design margins cannot be calculated only for a clean prototype in the laboratory; the real installation and dirt on the shop floor must be considered too. A “simulated blockage” condition can be added to the laboratory temperature-rise test — for example, covering part of the heat-sink area with a filter mesh — to see whether the overtemperature protection and derating act as designed.

18.8 Design notes and limits of the model

This chapter’s loss model treats a switching device as a fixed voltage drop plus a resistance and estimates the switching loss linearly from the transition time. It ignores the effect of temperature on the on-state voltage drop, the details of diode reverse recovery and the current ripple. It averages a sinusoidal current over one electrical cycle, whereas at start-up the motor is barely turning, the electrical cycle is very long, and a particular device may carry a current close to the peak for tens of milliseconds, so the actual junction temperature is higher than the model predicts. During braking the energy flows backwards and the loss falls mainly on the freewheeling diodes rather than on the switching devices. The thermal model is only first-order and represents the air inside the box by a single temperature, whereas temperatures vary greatly from place to place inside a real control box. The common-mode current is estimated from a single parasitic capacitance; real interference also depends on cable length, layout and filters and can only be determined by measurement. The burn-in model assumes that the proportion and distribution of early defects are known, whereas in practice they have to be estimated from years of return data; the Arrhenius relation applies only to temperature-dominated failure mechanisms, and mechanical early failures such as solder-joint fatigue and loose connectors must be screened by temperature cycling and vibration. These models are useful for seeing the direction and size of each factor; specific values must be confirmed by tests on prototypes.

Shop-floor notes

  • If a control box frequently reports overtemperature, first check whether the heat-sink fins and ventilation holes are clogged with lint and whether air can circulate around the box, then check whether short-seam operations are starting and stopping too often.
  • Bulging or leaking DC-link capacitors are usually the result of long-term overheating; when replacing them, choose capacitors of the same temperature rating and life, and find the cause of the overheating.
  • For encoder errors or occasional controller resets, first check that the earth wire is properly connected, that the motor-cable shield is earthed and that the control box is not too close to high-frequency equipment.
  • If the controller crashes when someone touches the panel in winter, check the earthing and ESD protection of the panel’s metal parts and the interfaces.
  • Do not cut the power during a firmware update; a control that can return to the old version after a failed update has a dual-bank design.
