### 1.2.1 The problem

The encoder on a joint motor puts out several thousand pulses per revolution, and the signal reaches the control board through a cable about 2 m long. The cable is bundled together with the power leads of the motor, and when the PWM switches, spikes are coupled onto the signal line; the measured interference superimposed on the signal is about ±0.3 V. The input of the control chip is an inverter: when the input is 0 V it should take it as 0, and when the input is 1.8 V it should take it as 1. Now 0 V has become 0.3 V and 1.8 V has become 1.5 V. Can the inverter still decide correctly?

To answer this question we need to know how the output changes as the input voltage varies continuously between 0 and VDD.

### 1.2.2 The voltage transfer characteristic

Raise the input voltage slowly from 0 to VDD and record the output voltage point by point; the resulting curve is called the **voltage transfer characteristic** (Figure 1.2.1). When the input is very low the NMOS is off and the PMOS is on, and the output equals VDD; when the input is very high the situation is reversed and the output is 0. In between there is a range in which both transistors conduct, and the output voltage is determined by the condition that the two currents are equal:

$$
I_{Dn}(V_{in},\,V_{out}) = I_{Dp}(V_{DD}-V_{in},\,V_{DD}-V_{out}) . \tag{1.2.1}
$$

For each Vin, solving this equation gives Vout. Program 1.2.1 solves it by bisection, and browser Lab 1.2 uses the same algorithm.

In the steepest middle part of the curve both transistors are in the saturation region. Both currents are then independent of Vout, and Eq. (1.2.1) holds at only one input voltage: with the input slightly lower, the PMOS current exceeds the NMOS current and the output rushes towards VDD; slightly higher, the NMOS gains the upper hand and the output rushes towards 0. This input voltage is the dividing line at which the inverter "decides between 0 and 1"; it is called the **switching threshold** VM and is the point on the transfer characteristic where Vout = Vin. Setting the two saturation currents equal,

$$
\frac{k'_n}{2}\frac{W_n}{L}(V_M-V_T)^2 = \frac{k'_p}{2}\frac{W_p}{L}(V_{DD}-V_M-V_T)^2 ,
$$

and taking square roots, we obtain

$$
V_M = \frac{V_T + x\,(V_{DD}-V_T)}{1+x}, \qquad x = \sqrt{\frac{k'_p W_p}{k'_n W_n}} . \tag{1.2.2}
$$

x is the square root of the ratio of the strengths of the PMOS and the NMOS. When the two transistors are equally strong (x = 1), VM = VDD/2, exactly in the middle; when the PMOS is weaker the threshold is lower, and when the PMOS is stronger the threshold is higher.

### 1.2.3 Equally strong transistors: the size ratio r

The **mobility** of holes is lower than that of electrons; in SKY130, k′p is only about 1/{{ex1_2.r:.2f}} of k′n. If the PMOS is as wide as the NMOS, the PMOS is much weaker and VM is pushed towards the low end. To make the two transistors equally strong, the PMOS must be widened to

$$
\frac{W_p}{W_n} = r = \frac{k'_n}{k'_p} . \tag{1.2.3}
$$

This book calls r the "symmetric size ratio"; for SKY130, r ≈ {{ex1_2.r:.2f}}. Note that r is determined by the process and differs from one process to another.

**Example 1.2.1** A minimum NMOS (Wn = 0.42 μm) is paired with a PMOS of Wp/Wn = 1 and of Wp/Wn = r respectively. Find the switching thresholds of the two inverters.

By Eq. (1.2.2), for Wp/Wn = 1 we have x = 1/√r and VM ≈ {{ex1_2.VMa:.3f}} V, lower than VDD/2; for Wp/Wn = r we have x = 1 and VM = {{ex1_2.VMb:.2f}} V, exactly VDD/2. The vertical parts of the two curves in Figure 1.2.1 fall at these two positions respectively.

### 1.2.4 Noise margins

Digital circuits resist interference by keeping "enough distance between 0 and 1". The two points of slope −1 on the transfer characteristic give the boundaries of this distance. When the input is below VIL, a small change of the input causes a change of the output smaller than itself, and interference is attenuated; when the input lies between VIL and VIH, interference is amplified and the output may fall into the undefined region. From this we define two **noise margins**:

$$
NM_L = V_{IL} - V_{OL}, \qquad NM_H = V_{OH} - V_{IH} . \tag{1.2.4}
$$

NML is the positive interference that a "0" can tolerate: the worst-case low level of the preceding stage is VOL, and with interference of no more than NML added, the following stage still takes it as 0. NMH is the negative interference that a "1" can tolerate. Note that VOL and VOH here refer specifically to the output voltages at the two points of slope −1 on the transfer characteristic, that is, the worst-case outputs when the input of the preceding stage itself sits at the boundary VIH or VIL; they are not the same quantity as the design specification of Section 1.1, "output low level no more than 0.2 V". Defined by such worst-case outputs, the noise margin is a slightly conservative estimate.

**Example 1.2.2** Continuing the problem of Section 1.2.1. With interference of about ±{{ex1_2.noise:.1f}} V, will either of the two inverters decide wrongly?

Program 1.2.1 solves the transfer characteristic on a fine grid from 0 to 1.8 V and finds the two points of slope −1. For Wp/Wn = 1, VIL ≈ {{ex1_2.VILa:.3f}} V and VIH ≈ {{ex1_2.VIHa:.3f}} V, NML ≈ {{ex1_2.NMLa:.2f}} V and NMH ≈ {{ex1_2.NMHa:.2f}} V; for Wp/Wn = r, NML = NMH ≈ {{ex1_2.NMLb:.3f}} V. The noise margins of both sizings exceed 0.3 V, so neither decides wrongly; but the low-level margin for Wp/Wn = 1 is about 0.1 V smaller than for Wp/Wn = r, leaving only about {{ex1_2.margin_a:.2f}} V to spare. The symmetric sizing leaves the same spare margin on both sides (about {{ex1_2.margin_b:.2f}} V), so it is equally safe whichever way the interference goes.

The noise margin is not the only figure for which larger is better. From Eq. (1.2.2), the wider the PMOS, the higher VM, the smaller NMH and the larger NML. If an input signal is subject to interference in one direction only (for example, spikes on the ground line that are always positive), the threshold can deliberately be shifted towards one side. In most cases, however, balance between the two sides is wanted, and, as we shall see, the symmetric sizing also makes the rise and the fall equally fast (Section 1.3).

The input pins of real chips have one more layer of protection: the Schmitt trigger. Its transfer characteristic has hysteresis; it switches at a higher voltage on the way up and at a lower voltage on the way down, which makes it more reliable for slowly changing and noisy signals. Signals such as those from motor encoders and push buttons usually enter a chip through Schmitt inputs.

### 1.2.5 Where the model fails

The transfer characteristic given by the square-law model has a steeper vertical part than a real device. In a SPICE simulation the current in the saturation region increases slightly with VDS (channel-length modulation), so the curve still has a finite slope where both transistors are saturated; velocity saturation in short-channel devices also makes the middle part less steep. The noise margins computed with the square law are therefore too large. In the calibration this book deliberately chooses an "effective threshold voltage" (Appendix A) so that the switching threshold and the noise margins differ from simulation by no more than 7 %; this effective value is smaller than the threshold voltage obtained by extrapolation from the linear region, and step one of the running project measures this difference first hand.

### 1.2.6 An everyday example

A sound-activated stairwell light has a trigger threshold. Set too low, a passing car or footsteps upstairs will switch the light on; set too high, a person clapping downstairs cannot switch it on. The switching threshold and noise margins of an inverter are the same: the threshold should sit between "0" and "1", with enough distance to both sides.
