### 1.3.1 The problem

The motor-control chip contains a high-speed counting clock of {{ex1_3.fclk_mhz:.0f}} MHz: both the PWM pulse widths and the intervals between encoder pulses are counted on its clock edges, and its period is only {{ex1_3.period_ns:.0f}} ns. After entering the chip, the clock passes through a string of inverters that shape and amplify it before it is distributed to the counters, and the loads of the stages along the way are not all the same. If the rising edge of a buffer is much slower than the falling edge, part of the high level of the clock is "eaten away", the duty cycle is no longer 50 %, and in the worst case a counter samples wrongly on some edge. The design requirement: the rise delay and the fall delay of the buffer must be close.

Section 1.2 required the two transistors to be equally strong from the DC point of view. This section looks again from the point of view of speed, and answers a finer question: if only the fastest average speed is wanted, how wide should the PMOS be?

### 1.3.2 The RC model of delay

In a digital circuit the load of a gate is almost entirely capacitance: the gate capacitance of the next stage, the junction capacitance of this stage's drains, and the wiring capacitance. A transition of the output is simply a transistor that is on charging or discharging these capacitances. The on-resistance changes during the transition: at first VDS is large and the transistor is in the saturation region; later VDS becomes small and it enters the linear region. In engineering this is approximated by a fixed **equivalent resistance**, taken as

$$
R_{eq} \approx \frac{3}{4}\,\frac{V_{DD}}{I_{D\text{SAT}}} . \tag{1.3.1}
$$

The origin of this expression is as follows: while the output falls from VDD to VDD/2, the transistor is assumed to carry the saturation current throughout, the voltage across it falls from VDD to VDD/2, and averaging V/IDSAT over this range of voltage gives ¾VDD/IDSAT (Exercise 1.3.4). In fact the transistor has entered the linear region in the second half and the current is smaller, so this approximation is optimistic; this book compensates for it by calibrating the parameters with SPICE (Section 1.3.5). With the equivalent resistance, the time for the output to go from one level to 50 % is the result for an RC circuit:

$$
t_{PHL} = 0.69\,R_{eqn}\,C, \qquad t_{PLH} = 0.69\,R_{eqp}\,C , \tag{1.3.2}
$$

where 0.69 = ln 2 is the number of time constants a first-order RC circuit takes to go from its starting point to 50 %. The subscript PHL denotes the output going from high to low (discharging through the NMOS), and PLH from low to high (charging through the PMOS). Together they define the **propagation delay** tp = (tPHL + tPLH)/2. C is the total capacitance at the output node: the drain capacitance of this stage, (Wn + Wp)Cd, plus the load CL.

The most commonly used load is "driving four identical inverters", that is, a **fan-out** of 4. The delay in this case is called the **FO4 delay**, the most commonly used yardstick of how fast a process is: the FO4 delay of SKY130 is about 50 ps, while more advanced processes bring it below 10 ps.

### 1.3.3 Symmetric sizing

When the two transistors are equally wide, the saturation current of the PMOS is only 1/r of that of the NMOS, its equivalent resistance is r times larger, and the rise is much slower than the fall. By Eq. (1.3.2), to make tPLH = tPHL we need only make the equivalent resistances of the two transistors equal, that is, their saturation currents equal, Wp/Wn = r: exactly the same condition as that for VM = VDD/2 in Section 1.2.

### 1.3.4 The sizing of minimum average delay

Table 1.3.1 shows one more thing: the smallest average delay is obtained not at r but at the intermediate value √r. The reason is that widening the PMOS cuts both ways. It reduces the equivalent resistance of the pull-up and so shortens tPLH; but it also increases the drain capacitance of this stage and the input capacitance of the next stage, and so lengthens tPHL (Figure 1.3.1). The average of the two is smallest at some intermediate value.

Write β for Wp/Wn. With an FO4 load the load capacitance is proportional to (1 + β), tPHL ∝ (1 + β) and tPLH ∝ (1 + β)·r/β. The average is

$$
t_p \propto (1+\beta)\left(1+\frac{r}{\beta}\right) = 1 + r + \beta + \frac{r}{\beta} ,
$$

and setting its derivative with respect to β to zero gives β = √r. Program 1.3.1 sweeps from 0.5 to 4; the minimum falls at {{ex1_3.best:.2f}}, with an average delay of {{ex1_3.tp_best:.1f}} ps, only about 5 % faster than the symmetric sizing.

The choice of sizing therefore depends on the goal: clock trees and paths that require an accurate duty cycle use the symmetric sizing r; ordinary logic paths care only about the average delay and can use √r, which also makes the PMOS more than a third smaller in area. The inverters in standard-cell libraries often take a compromise value between 1.5 and 2, and the SKY130 cell library does the same (Chapter 9).

### 1.3.5 Where the model fails

The RC model sums up the whole transition by one resistance and is very accurate for moderate loads such as FO4: in its calibration this book makes the FO4 delay at Wp/Wn = 2.25 agree with SPICE (2.25 is the point, among the sweep points chosen in advance for the calibration simulations, closest to the symmetric ratio r). For very light loads (a fan-out of 1) or size ratios far from symmetric, the error of a one-sided delay can exceed 20 % (Appendix A), because the input waveform is not an ideal step and the time during which both transistors conduct is ignored. In design, hand calculation is used to choose the direction and the order of magnitude; the final numbers must come from simulation or from the characterization data of the cell library (Chapter 9).

### 1.3.6 An everyday example

When the two people on a seesaw have different weights, the pivot has to be moved towards the heavier side to balance it. The PMOS is by nature "light" (its current is small) and has to be compensated by widening; how much is determined by the ratio of the mobilities of the two kinds of carrier.
