The motor-control chip in a robot joint, the image processor in a camera and the processor in a mobile phone are all built from hundreds of millions to tens of billions of transistors, yet their most basic unit is of a single kind: an NMOS transistor and a PMOS transistor connected in series between the supply and ground, with the gates tied together as the input and the drains tied together as the output. This is the CMOS inverter. An inverter does only one thing, turning 0 into 1 and 1 into 0, yet it brings out every core problem of digital integrated circuit design: whether the output levels are "clean" enough, how much interference they can tolerate, how long a transition takes, how to be fast when driving a large load, and how much energy each transition consumes. The NAND gates, flip-flops, adders and memories of the later chapters are all built up on top of the inverter.

A drive board for a robot joint runs through the whole chapter. We first treat the MOSFET as a switch controlled by the gate voltage and see why a CMOS inverter consumes almost no power at rest (Section 1.1). We then sweep the input voltage slowly to draw the transfer characteristic, define the switching threshold and the noise margins, and decide whether interference on an encoder signal can make the chip read wrongly (Section 1.2). Next comes sizing: the PMOS is weaker than the NMOS, so how much wider must it be for the rise and the fall to be equally fast (Section 1.3)? After that we regard the inverter as a resistor charging and discharging a capacitor, compute the propagation delay, and answer a question that is common in engineering: when a chip pin has to drive a 10 pF load on the board, how many inverter stages should be inserted in between (Section 1.4)? Finally we turn to power: how much energy each transition draws from the supply, how much can be saved by lowering the voltage, and what the price is (Section 1.5).

The whole book uses one "hand-calculation model": the long-channel square-law MOSFET, with parameters calibrated against SPICE simulations of the open-source process SKY130 (Appendix A). Every number in this chapter is computed by a program, and the browser labs use the same set of parameters; the differences between hand calculation and simulation, and where the square-law model fails, are themselves part of what this chapter teaches.

After studying this chapter the reader should be able to:

1. state the conditions and current formulas of the cut-off, linear and saturation regions of a MOSFET, and estimate output levels from the on-resistance in the linear region;
2. explain why the static power of a CMOS inverter is almost zero;
3. draw the voltage transfer characteristic of an inverter, find the switching threshold VM, VIL, VIH and the noise margins, and explain how the size ratio affects them;
4. estimate tPHL and tPLH from the equivalent resistance and the load capacitance, and choose Wp/Wn for either of two goals, equal rise and fall or minimum average delay;
5. design a tapered inverter chain to drive a large load, find the optimum number of stages, and explain how the self-loading capacitance changes the rule of thumb ln F / ln 4;
6. compute the dynamic power αCV²fN and analyse the effect of lowering the supply voltage on power and delay.
