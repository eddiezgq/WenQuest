### 1.5.1 The problem

A line-following cart runs on two lithium cells. Its control chip contains about ten thousand logic gates, clocked at {{ex1_5.f_mhz:.0f}} MHz. Battery capacity is limited: every extra milliwatt the chip consumes means the cart runs a little less; heating of the chip also makes the readings of the optical sensors near it drift. The designer needs to know: how much power does this chip consume? Where does the power go? How can it be saved?

Section 1.1 noted that a CMOS inverter sitting at 0 or 1 consumes almost no power. Power is consumed when it switches.

### 1.5.2 The energy of each transition

The output node of an inverter carries a load capacitance C. When the output goes from 0 to 1, the PMOS is on and the supply charges C through the PMOS to VDD; the charge delivered by the supply is Q = CVDD and the energy delivered is QVDD = CVDD². Half of it, ½CVDD², is stored in the capacitor; the other half is turned into heat in the on-resistance of the PMOS, and this half is the same whatever the value of the resistance (Exercise 1.5.3). When the output goes back from 1 to 0, the supply delivers no further energy; the ½CVDD² stored in the capacitor is discharged through the NMOS and is also turned into heat. Over one complete "0→1→0" cycle the supply delivers a total energy of CVDD², all of which becomes heat.

### 1.5.3 Dynamic power

The gates in a chip do not all switch in every clock cycle. Write the probability that the output of a gate goes from 0 to 1 in a clock cycle as the **activity factor** α. With N gates and a clock frequency f, there are αfN transitions from 0 to 1 per second, each drawing CVDD² from the supply. The **dynamic power** is therefore

$$
P_{dyn} = \alpha\,C\,V_{DD}^2\,f\,N . \tag{1.5.1}
$$

Some books define α as "the probability that the output switches (in either direction)"; Eq. (1.5.1) then has to be multiplied by ½, because two transitions make up one charge-and-discharge cycle. The two ways of writing it give the same result, only the meaning of α differs by a factor of two; when reading the literature, check which one is meant.

**Example 1.5.1** The control chip of the line-following cart: N = {{ex1_5.N:.0f}} gates, α = {{ex1_5.alpha:.1f}}, a clock of {{ex1_5.f_mhz:.0f}} MHz, and a load of C = {{ex1_5.C_ff:.0f}} fF per gate. Find the dynamic power. If the supply is lowered from 1.8 V to {{ex1_5.Vlow:.1f}} V, what is the power?

Each transition from 0 to 1 draws CVDD² = {{ex1_5.E_fj:.1f}} fJ. By Eq. (1.5.1), P ≈ {{ex1_5.P_mw:.2f}} mW. At {{ex1_5.Vlow:.1f}} V the power, which is proportional to VDD², becomes about {{ex1_5.P_low_mw:.2f}} mW, a saving of {{ex1_5.save_pct:.0f}} %.

Eq. (1.5.1) points out four ways to reduce dynamic power: fewer transitions (α; for example, stopping the clock of modules that are not needed, the clock gating of Chapter 5), smaller capacitance (C; smaller transistors and shorter wires), lower frequency (f), and lower voltage (VDD). Of these, the voltage enters as a square and has the most marked effect.

### 1.5.4 The price of lowering the voltage

The voltage cannot be lowered without limit. When VDD is lowered, the saturation current of the transistors falls as (VDD − VT)², while the equivalent resistance ¾VDD/IDSAT increases, and the gates become slower. In Figure 1.5.1 the FO4 delay grows rapidly as the voltage falls: about {{ex1_5.fo4_hi_ps:.0f}} ps at 1.8 V and about {{ex1_5.fo4_low_ps:.0f}} ps at {{ex1_5.Vlow:.1f}} V.

**Example 1.5.2** Continuing Example 1.5.1. To guarantee timing, the FO4 delay is required to be no more than {{ex1_5.fo4max_ps:.0f}} ps. How low can the supply go? What is the power then?

Program 1.5.1 finds by bisection the voltage at which the FO4 delay equals {{ex1_5.fo4max_ps:.0f}} ps, obtaining VDD ≈ {{ex1_5.Vlim:.2f}} V. So lowering the supply to 1.2 V will not do, as timing would fail; at {{ex1_5.Vlim:.2f}} V the power is about {{ex1_5.P_lim_mw:.2f}} mW. This is the limit of "saving power by lowering the voltage": the performance requirement sets the lowest voltage. A mobile-phone processor adjusts its voltage and frequency continually according to its load, lowering them under light load and raising them under heavy load; this is called dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (Chapter 5).

### 1.5.5 Beyond dynamic power

Two other kinds of power are consumed during switching. One is **short-circuit power**: while the input goes from 0 to 1, there is a short time during which both transistors are on, and a brief current flows from the supply to ground. The slower the input edge, the longer this time. In a well-designed circuit it is usually only about a tenth of the dynamic power; this is one of the reasons why edges must not be allowed to become too slow (another benefit of the inverter chain of Section 1.4). The other is leakage: a transistor that is off is not completely off, and with the gate voltage below the threshold there is still an exponentially decaying subthreshold current. The leakage of the 1.8 V devices of SKY130 is very small, and even summed over ten thousand gates it is far below the dynamic power; but in advanced processes the threshold voltage has been forced down, leakage rises steeply with temperature, and the static power can be comparable to the dynamic power. Chapter 5 is devoted to these questions.

### 1.5.6 An everyday example

The power-saving mode of a mobile phone lowers the refresh rate of the screen and limits the maximum frequency and voltage of the processor. The user feels that the phone has become "a little slower", and in return the battery lasts noticeably longer. This is exactly the effect of lowering f and VDD in Eq. (1.5.1), and the trade-off between the two curves of Figure 1.5.1.
