### 1.1.1 The problem

On the drive board of a robot joint, the control chip has to send an "enable" signal through an optocoupler to the isolated power stage. The input of the optocoupler is a light-emitting diode in series with a current-limiting resistor connected to 3.3 V; an output pin of the chip is connected to the other end of the diode. When the output is low it sinks the current into the chip, the diode lights up, and the power stage receives "enable". The design requirement is written very simply: the pin must sink a current of 1 mA while the output voltage stays no higher than 0.2 V; otherwise the voltage across the light-emitting diode is insufficient and the optocoupler may not turn on fully.

The part of the chip that pulls the pin low is an NMOS transistor. How large must it be? To answer this we first need to know what a transistor looks like when it is on.

### 1.1.2 The MOSFET: a switch controlled by the gate voltage

A **metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor** (MOSFET) has four terminals: the gate, the source, the drain and the body. The gate is separated from the silicon beneath it by an oxide layer a few nanometres thick. Through this insulator the gate voltage draws charge to the surface of the silicon and forms a conducting channel between the source and the drain. In an NMOS transistor the channel carries electrons, and the transistor conducts when the gate is made positive with respect to the source; in a PMOS transistor the channel carries holes, and the transistor conducts when the gate is sufficiently lower than the source. The gate is insulated from the channel and draws almost no current. This is the fundamental reason why MOSFETs can be made into digital circuits: the output of one gate can drive the inputs of many gates without having to supply them with current at rest.

The gate–source voltage at which the channel begins to form is called the **threshold voltage** VT. Depending on the gate–source and drain–source voltages, an NMOS transistor has three operating states. When VGS < VT there is no channel; the transistor is in the **cut-off region** and ID ≈ 0. When VGS > VT and VDS is small, the channel extends continuously from source to drain; the transistor is in the **linear region** and behaves like a resistor whose value is controlled by the gate voltage. When VDS rises above VGS − VT, the channel at the drain end is "pinched off" and the current no longer increases appreciably with VDS; the transistor is in the **saturation region** and behaves like a current source controlled by the gate voltage. Written as formulas, these three pieces form the most commonly used long-channel **square-law model**:

$$
I_D = \begin{cases} 0, & V_{GS} \le V_T \\[2pt] k'\dfrac{W}{L}\left[(V_{GS}-V_T)V_{DS}-\dfrac{V_{DS}^2}{2}\right], & V_{DS} < V_{GS}-V_T \\[8pt] \dfrac{k'}{2}\dfrac{W}{L}(V_{GS}-V_T)^2, & V_{DS} \ge V_{GS}-V_T \end{cases} \tag{1.1.1}
$$

Here W and L are the width and length of the channel, and k′ = μCox is called the **process transconductance parameter**; it is determined by the carrier mobility and the gate capacitance per unit area, and is a constant for a given process. The formulas for a PMOS transistor have the same form, with VGS and VDS replaced by the source–gate voltage VSG and the source–drain voltage VSD, and k′ replaced by the PMOS value.

Eq. (1.1.1) tells the designer that there is only one knob in hand: W/L. The process fixes k′, VT and the minimum channel length; what the designer can change is whether the transistor is made wider or narrower. Doubling the width doubles the current.

This book uses the 1.8 V devices of the open-source process SKY130. Real short-channel devices are more complicated than Eq. (1.1.1), but over the range that matters for digital circuits, provided the parameters are chosen suitably, the error of the square-law model can be kept between a few per cent and a little over twenty per cent (Appendix A gives the calibration procedure and a table of errors). All the calculations in this book use this set of "effective parameters": VDD = 1.8 V, VT = {{ex1_1.VT:.2f}} V, k′n = {{ex1_1.kn_ua:.1f}} μA/V², L = {{ex1_1.L:.2f}} μm.

**Example 1.1.1** A minimum-size NMOS transistor (W/L = 0.42/0.15) has its gate connected to 1.8 V. Find its saturation current and its on-resistance when VDS is very small.

The saturation current is given by the third line of Eq. (1.1.1):

$$
I_{D\text{SAT}} = \frac{k'_n}{2}\frac{W}{L}(V_{DD}-V_T)^2 ,
$$

which gives IDSAT ≈ {{ex1_1.Isat_ua:.0f}} μA, or {{ex1_1.Isat_per_um:.0f}} μA/μm per micrometre of gate width. As VDS → 0 the square term of the linear-region formula can be neglected, ID ≈ k′(W/L)(VGS − VT)VDS, and the transistor is equivalent to a resistor:

$$
R_{\text{on}} = \frac{1}{k'_n\,(W/L)\,(V_{DD}-V_T)} , \tag{1.1.2}
$$

about {{ex1_1.Ron_k:.2f}} kΩ for the minimum transistor. This **on-resistance** is inversely proportional to W: the wider the transistor, the smaller the "contact resistance" of the switch.

### 1.1.3 The CMOS inverter

Place a PMOS transistor on top with its source connected to the supply VDD and an NMOS transistor below with its source connected to ground, join the two drains as the output and the two gates as the input, and we obtain the **complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor** (CMOS) **inverter** (Figure 1.1.1, left). "Complementary" means that of the two transistors one is always on and the other off. When the input is 0 V, the NMOS has VGS = 0 and is off; the PMOS has VSG = VDD and is on, so the output is connected through the PMOS to VDD and is high. When the input is VDD the situation is exactly reversed: the output is connected through the NMOS to ground and is low (Figure 1.1.1, middle and right).

This structure has two important consequences. First, in the steady state one of the two transistors between the supply and ground is always off, so there is no DC path, and an inverter sitting at 0 or 1 consumes almost no power; its **static power** comes only from the weak leakage of the transistor that is off (Section 1.5). This is why CMOS replaced the early NMOS circuits and became the process for almost all digital chips. Second, the output high level is simply VDD and the low level is simply 0; no DC current produces a voltage drop across an on-resistance, and the output swing is "full".

Both conclusions, however, assume that the output draws no DC current. Driving the optocoupler is a different case: when the output is low, the external 1 mA has to flow through the NMOS to ground, and the voltage drop across the on-resistance is the output low level.

### 1.1.4 The output low level and the width of the transistor

**Example 1.1.2** Continuing the problem of Section 1.1.1. The output pin sinks I = {{ex1_1.I_ma:.0f}} mA and the output low level VOL is required to be no more than {{ex1_1.VOLmax:.1f}} V. How wide must the NMOS be at least?

With the input high, VGS = VDD and the drain voltage of the NMOS is VOL. VOL = 0.2 V is much smaller than VGS − VT, so the transistor works in the linear region. Setting the linear-region current equal to the load current:

$$
k'_n\frac{W}{L}\left[(V_{DD}-V_T)V_{OL}-\frac{V_{OL}^2}{2}\right] = I \quad\Rightarrow\quad W \ge \frac{I\,L}{k'_n\left[(V_{DD}-V_T)V_{OL,\max}-V_{OL,\max}^2/2\right]} . \tag{1.1.3}
$$

Substituting gives W ≥ {{ex1_1.Wn:.2f}} μm, W/L ≈ {{ex1_1.WL:.1f}}, which is {{ex1_1.ratio_min:.0f}} times the minimum transistor. The on-resistance is then VOL/I = {{ex1_1.Ron_W:.0f}} Ω, about {{ex1_1.Ron_gap:.0f}} % larger than the {{ex1_1.Ron_small_W:.0f}} Ω given by Eq. (1.1.2): Eq. (1.1.2) is the value as VDS tends to zero, and at VDS = 0.2 V the term −VDS²/2 can no longer be neglected.

Figure 1.1.2 looks at the same thing graphically. The horizontal axis is VDS; the curves are the output characteristics of NMOS transistors of different widths at VGS = 1.8 V, and the horizontal line is the 1 mA load. The intersection of a curve with the load line is the output low level. The wider the transistor, the steeper the curve and the further to the left the intersection; the curve for a width of {{ex1_1.Wn:.2f}} μm crosses exactly at 0.2 V.

When the load is increased to 2 mA, VOL does not simply double: the program gives about {{ex1_1.VOL2:.2f}} V. The reason is the term −VDS²/2 in the linear-region formula: the larger VDS, the less the transistor behaves like a fixed resistor. When the load current is much smaller than the saturation current (here about {{ex1_1.Isat_W_ma:.1f}} mA), "on-resistance times current" can be used as an estimate, but even at 1 mA it is already off by nearly ten per cent (Exercise 1.1.5); close to the saturation current, the output suddenly can no longer be held down.

This example also illustrates a practical point. Minimum-size transistors are enough for the gates inside a chip, because they only drive the gates of other gates; pins that sink or source milliampere currents directly to the outside need much wider output transistors. This is one of the reasons why the input/output cells (IO cells) around the edge of a chip are much larger than the internal logic gates.

### 1.1.5 An everyday example

The single-pole double-throw switch common in homes (one blade thrown between two contacts) is much like a CMOS inverter: the blade connects either to the upper contact or to the lower contact, never to both at once, and it does not stop in between. The output of a CMOS inverter likewise has only two states, "connected to the supply" and "connected to ground"; intermediate voltages appear only for an instant during a transition. The difference is that the two "switches" of a CMOS inverter are controlled by the same input, so one is always open while the other is closed, and the supply is never connected directly to ground.
