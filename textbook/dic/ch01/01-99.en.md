A MOSFET is a switch controlled by the gate voltage: below the threshold the gate–source voltage leaves it off, above the threshold it is on; once on, it behaves like a resistor when the drain–source voltage is small (the linear region) and like a current source when it is large (the saturation region). The long-channel square-law model describes these three regions with k′, VT and W/L, the only one the designer can change. The CMOS inverter connects a PMOS and an NMOS in series between the supply and ground; the two transistors are complementary, one of them is always off in the steady state, and there is no DC path from the supply to ground, so it consumes almost no power at rest and its output swing is full. When the output has to sink or source a DC current, the voltage drop across the on-resistance determines the output level, and the transistor must be widened according to the load current.

Sweeping the input slowly gives the voltage transfer characteristic. Where both transistors are saturated the curve is almost vertical, and the corresponding input is the switching threshold VM, determined by the ratio of the strengths of the two transistors; the two points of slope −1 fix VIL, VIH and the two noise margins. When the PMOS is widened to r = k′n/k′p times, the two transistors are equally strong, VM = VDD/2, and the noise margins on the two sides are equal.

A transition is a transistor that is on charging or discharging the load capacitance; with the equivalent resistance Req ≈ ¾VDD/IDSAT the propagation delay can be estimated as 0.69ReqC. The symmetric sizing r makes rise and fall equally fast; the sizing of minimum average delay is √r, because widening the PMOS also increases the capacitance. To drive a large load, a chain of inverters growing stage by stage is inserted, sharing the total fan-out F among N stages; the self-loading capacitance makes the optimum fan-out per stage satisfy f(ln f − 1) = γ. For SKY130, γ is about 1.6 and the optimum fan-out about 4, in agreement with the rule of thumb ln F / ln 4; the delay changes slowly near the optimum, and one or two stages fewer are often used in exchange for area and power.

Each transition from 0 to 1 draws an energy of CVDD² from the supply, half of which is turned into heat during charging and half during discharging; the dynamic power is P = αCVDD²fN. Lowering the voltage saves the most power, but the gates become slower, and the performance requirement sets the lowest voltage. During switching there is also short-circuit power, and transistors that are off still leak; in advanced processes leakage cannot be neglected.

All the calculations in this chapter use the same square-law model calibrated against SKY130. Near moderate loads and symmetric sizing it differs from simulation by a few per cent; for light loads and sizing far from symmetric the one-sided delays can differ by more than 20 %. Its noise margins are optimistic, so the calibration uses an effective threshold voltage smaller than the extrapolated value. Knowing where a model is good enough and where it fails is as important as knowing how to use it.

### Answers and hints to selected exercises

1.1.1 At VGS = 1.2 V the transistor is in the saturation region and ID ≈ {{ex1_ans.a111_i12_ua:.1f}} μA; at VGS = 1.8 V it is about {{ex1_ans.a111_i18_ua:.0f}} μA, so the former is {{ex1_ans.a111_ratio:.2f}} times the latter. The saturation current is proportional to the square of the overdrive voltage VGS − VT, and (0.65/1.25)² is much smaller than 1.2/1.8.

1.1.2 VOH ≥ 1.6 V means VSD ≤ 0.2 V. Using Eq. (1.1.3) with the PMOS parameters, Wp ≈ {{ex1_ans.a112_wp:.1f}} μm, which is {{ex1_ans.a112_ratio:.2f}} times the NMOS of Example 1.1.2, exactly r: for the same current and voltage drop, the PMOS must be r times wider.

1.1.3 Solving ID(VDD, VOL) = 1.5 mA gives VOL ≈ {{ex1_ans.a113_vol:.2f}} V, which exceeds the 0.2 V requirement. VOL increases faster than the current (it is not 0.2 × 1.5 = 0.3 V), because the term VOL²/2 in the linear-region equation grows with VOL.

1.1.4 The input is connected only to gates, and the gate is separated from the channel by the oxide layer, so the DC current is almost zero; driving 100 inputs at rest therefore needs almost no current. During switching, the 100 gate capacitances have to be charged and discharged at every transition, and the current is proportional to the switching frequency and the total capacitance (Section 1.5).

1.1.5 The two differ by 10 % at a load current of about {{ex1_ans.a115_i_ma:.1f}} mA; the larger the current, the more "on-resistance times current" underestimates.

1.2.1 From Eq. (1.2.2), x = (VM − VT)/(VDD − VT − VM), and Wp/Wn = x²r ≈ {{ex1_ans.a121_ratio:.2f}}.

1.2.2 VIL and VIH are the points of slope −1. The steeper the middle part, the closer these two points are to VM, the higher VIL and the lower VIH, and the larger the noise margins. With a slope of only −2 the two points lie far apart and the noise margins become markedly smaller; if the slope never reaches −1, VIL and VIH do not exist and the gate cannot restore logic levels.

1.2.3 VM ≈ {{ex1_ans.a123_vm:.2f}} V and NML ≈ NMH ≈ {{ex1_ans.a123_nml:.3f}} V, which is {{ex1_ans.a123_frac:.0f}} % of VDD, slightly higher even than the {{ex1_ans.a123_frac18:.0f}} % at 1.8 V. This is a result of the square-law model; at low voltage a real device approaches the subthreshold region and its gain falls, so this conclusion must be checked by simulation.

1.2.4 If interference can only pull a 1 lower, NMH must be increased, that is, VIH lowered: make the NMOS relatively stronger (reduce Wp/Wn) so that VM moves down. The price is a smaller NML and a longer rise delay.

1.2.5 The size ratio at which the two noise margins are equal is about {{ex1_ans.a125_ratio:.2f}}, in agreement with r ≈ {{ex1_3.r:.2f}}: when the two transistors are equally strong, the transfer characteristic is symmetric about its midpoint.

1.3.1 About {{ex1_ans.a131_rn_k:.2f}} kΩ for the NMOS and about {{ex1_ans.a131_rp_k:.1f}} kΩ for the PMOS; their ratio is r.

1.3.2 Differentiating tp ∝ 1 + r + β + r/β gives β = √r. The conclusion is unchanged when the drain capacitance is taken into account: the drain capacitance of this stage, (Wn + Wp)Cd, is also proportional to 1 + β and changes only the common factor. The result of the sweep in Program 1.3.1 ({{ex1_3.best:.2f}}) agrees with √r ≈ {{ex1_3.sqrt_r:.2f}}. Only if the load contains wiring capacitance independent of β does the optimum move towards r.

1.3.3 The average delay for β = 3 is about {{ex1_ans.a133_slower:.0f}} % slower than for β = √3, while the PMOS area for β = √3 is about {{ex1_ans.a133_area:.0f}} % smaller.

1.3.4 Req = (2/VDD)∫ V/IDSAT dV (integrated from VDD/2 to VDD) = (2/VDD)·(3VDD²/8)/IDSAT = ¾VDD/IDSAT. The 3/4 comes from averaging over voltages between VDD and VDD/2. In fact, once the output falls below VDD − VT the transistor enters the linear region and the current is smaller than IDSAT, so the true average resistance is larger and this approximation makes the delay too small; this book absorbs this error together with the rest when calibrating Cg and Cd with SPICE (Section 1.3.5).

1.3.5 After one stage the output stays high for T/2 − tPLH + tPHL, and the duty cycle is about {{ex1_ans.a135_duty1:.1f}} %. Write Δ = tPLH − tPHL; the output of the first stage is high for T/2 − Δ and low for T/2 + Δ; the second stage outputs high while its input is low, so its high time is (T/2 + Δ) − Δ = T/2. When two identical inverters are connected in succession the errors cancel exactly and the duty cycle returns to {{ex1_ans.a135_duty2:.0f}} %; with an odd number of identical inverters only the error of one stage remains, and it does not accumulate from stage to stage. When the load of the second stage is FO8, the errors of the two stages are no longer equal and the duty cycle is about {{ex1_ans.a135_duty2b:.1f}} %. A clock path therefore either uses the symmetric sizing or gives the two stages of each pair the same load.

1.4.1 The derivative of F^{1/N} with respect to N is −f ln f / N. Setting dt/dN = 0.69RC1[(γ + f) − f ln f] = 0 gives f(ln f − 1) = γ. For γ = 0, f = e ≈ 2.7.

1.4.2 For 2 pF the fastest is {{ex1_ans.a142_n2}} stages (ln F / ln 4 ≈ {{ex1_ans.a142_l2:.1f}}), and for 20 pF the fastest is {{ex1_ans.a142_n20}} stages (≈ {{ex1_ans.a142_l20:.1f}}). When the load becomes 10 times larger, F becomes 10 times larger, ln F increases only by ln 10, and the number of stages grows by only one or two.

1.4.3 Solving f(ln f − 1) = 0.67 gives f ≈ {{ex1_ans.a143_f:.1f}}, and the number of stages ln F / ln f ≈ {{ex1_ans.a143_n:.1f}}, about {{ex1_ans.a143_n:.0f}} stages when rounded. The smaller the self-loading ratio, the smaller the optimum fan-out and the more stages.

1.4.4 In phase requires an even number of stages. Of the even numbers of stages that meet 0.35 ns, the fastest is {{ex1_ans.a144_n}} stages, with a delay of about {{ex1_ans.a144_t_ps:.0f}} ps.

1.4.5 Each time F is multiplied by 10, the fastest number of stages grows by about one or two, and the minimum delay grows approximately linearly with ln F, because at the optimum the delay per stage stays roughly constant and the total delay is proportional to the number of stages (∝ ln F).

1.5.1 P = 0.05 × 3 fF × 1.8² × 200 MHz × 10⁶ ≈ {{ex1_ans.a151_p1_mw:.1f}} mW; at 1.2 V it is about {{ex1_ans.a151_p2_mw:.1f}} mW.

1.5.2 A complete charge-and-discharge cycle comprises two transitions, 0→1 and 1→0, and the supply delivers CVDD² only on 0→1. Counted "in either direction", the number of transitions is twice the number of 0→1 transitions, so the formula must be multiplied by ½.

1.5.3 The charging current is i = (VDD/R)e^{−t/RC}, and the energy in the resistor is ∫i²R dt (integrated from 0 to ∞) = (VDD²/R)·(RC/2) = ½CVDD², independent of R.

1.5.4 Under the conditions of the cart's chip, the lowest voltage is about {{ex1_ans.a154_v:.2f}} V (FO4 about {{ex1_ans.a154_fo4_ps:.0f}} ps), and the power is about {{ex1_ans.a154_p_mw:.2f}} mW.

1.5.5 α = 1. The clock network switches in every cycle and has to drive the clock inputs of all the flip-flops on the chip as well as long wires, so its total capacitance is very large and it often accounts for a large part of the dynamic power of the chip; clock gating (Chapter 5) exists for this reason.

### Historical notes

The idea of controlling conduction at a semiconductor surface by an electric field was written into a patent by Lilienfeld as early as 1925, but at that time no clean semiconductor surface could be made, and the device could not work. In 1959 Atalla and Kahng at Bell Laboratories passivated the silicon surface with thermally grown silicon dioxide and made the first working MOSFET. In 1963 Sah and Wanlass of Fairchild published complementary MOS circuits, pointing out that pairing N-type and P-type transistors gives almost no power consumption at rest; Wanlass filed a patent for it in the same year. Early CMOS was slower than NMOS and its process more complicated, so it was first used in products most sensitive to power, such as watches and calculators; in the 1980s, as the level of integration rose, the static power of NMOS circuits became unbearable, and CMOS became the mainstream process for digital integrated circuits, as it has remained to this day. The problem of the optimum number of stages in an inverter chain was set out systematically by Mead and Conway in *Introduction to VLSI Systems* in 1980, and Sutherland and others later generalized it into the method of "logical effort" (Chapter 2). The SKY130 process used in this book was opened by SkyWater and Google in 2020; it was the first commercial process design kit that can be used free of charge and taken to real fabrication.
