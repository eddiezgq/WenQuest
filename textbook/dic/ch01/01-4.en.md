### 1.4.1 The problem

The drive board of a joint has to sample the motor current at the midpoint of each PWM period, when the power transistors are not switching and the current reading is cleanest. The sampling instant is decided by the control chip: the chip sends a sampling clock out through a pin to the clock input of the current-sampling ADC on the board. The chip pad, the bond wire, the package pin and the PCB trace, together with the input of the ADC, add up to a total capacitance of about 10 pF. The sampling instant must be strictly aligned with the PWM count inside the chip, and the timing budget allows this output buffer a delay of no more than {{ex1_4.tmax_ns:.2f}} ns.

The gates inside a chip are all designed to drive a few fF. What happens if a minimum inverter is used to drive 10 pF directly?

### 1.4.2 A single stage: the RC is too large

**Example 1.4.1** A minimum symmetric inverter (Wn = 0.42 μm, Wp/Wn = r) drives CL = {{ex1_4.CL_pf:.0f}} pF directly. Find the delay.

The input capacitance of the minimum symmetric inverter is C1 = (Wn + Wp)Cg ≈ {{ex1_4.C1_ff:.2f}} fF. The ratio of the load to it, the total fan-out, is

$$
F = \frac{C_L}{C_1} \approx {{ex1_4.F:.0f}} . \tag{1.4.1}
$$

By Eq. (1.3.2) the delay is about {{ex1_4.t1_ns:.0f}} ns, more than a hundred times slower than required. Widening this stage would of course reduce its resistance, but its input capacitance would grow in the same proportion, and the stage before it could no longer drive it. The problem has been pushed forward, not solved.

### 1.4.3 The inverter chain

The remedy is to insert a string of inverters that grow stage by stage: the first stage is a minimum inverter, each later stage is f times wider than the one before, and the last stage drives CL. In such an **inverter chain** each stage only has to drive a load f times larger than itself, not F times. After N stages the chain has grown exactly to the load, so

$$
f^N = F, \qquad f = F^{1/N} . \tag{1.4.2}
$$

The delay of each stage has two parts: charging the input capacitance of the next stage (proportional to f), and charging its own drain capacitance (independent of f). Writing the ratio of drain capacitance to gate capacitance as γ = Cd/Cg, called the self-loading ratio, the delay of each stage is 0.69R·C1·(γ + f), where R is the equivalent resistance of the minimum symmetric inverter (the same for both transistors). Because the size and the load of each stage are scaled in the same proportion, every stage has the same delay, and the total delay is

$$
t(N) = N \cdot 0.69\,R\,C_1\,(\gamma + F^{1/N}) . \tag{1.4.3}
$$

The drain capacitance here is the **self-loading capacitance** of the stage: it scales up together with the stage and does not become smaller by dividing the task into stages.

### 1.4.4 The optimum number of stages

In Eq. (1.4.3), with too few stages N the fan-out f of each stage is too large; with too many, the self-loading delays γ of the stages add up. There is an optimum in between. Treating N as a continuous variable and setting the derivative of Eq. (1.4.3) to zero, the optimum fan-out per stage satisfies

$$
f\,(\ln f - 1) = \gamma . \tag{1.4.4}
$$

Neglecting self-loading (γ = 0) gives f = e ≈ 2.7. The rule of thumb more commonly used in engineering is "a fan-out of about 4 per stage", with the number of stages taken as N ≈ ln F / ln 4, which corresponds to a self-loading of γ ≈ 1.5 or so.

Figure 1.4.1 also shows something very useful in engineering: the curve is very flat near the optimum. One or two stages more or fewer change the delay by only a few per cent. Practical designs therefore often do not use the fastest number of stages but one or two fewer, in exchange for smaller area and power.

**Example 1.4.3** The timing budget requires a delay of less than {{ex1_4.tmax_ns:.2f}} ns. How many stages are needed at least?

From the results of Program 1.4.1, {{ex1_4.few_m1}} stages take about {{ex1_4.t_fewm1_ps:.0f}} ps, which is not enough; {{ex1_4.few}} stages take about {{ex1_4.t_few_ps:.0f}} ps, which meets the requirement. The minimum is {{ex1_4.few}} stages. With fewer stages, the fan-out per stage is again taken as the N-th root of F, and the sum of the widths of all stages (with the first stage as 1) falls from about {{ex1_4.w_best:.0f}} for {{ex1_4.best}} stages to about {{ex1_4.w_few:.0f}}; the total gate width is about {{ex1_4.w_save_pct:.0f}} % smaller, and the capacitance charged and discharged at each transition, and with it the dynamic power, falls in proportion (Section 1.5). Attention must also be paid to whether the number of stages is odd or even: the output of a chain with an odd number of inverters is inverted with respect to its input, so if the sampling clock must be in phase, either an even number of stages is used or the clock is inverted once beforehand in the preceding logic.

### 1.4.5 Where the model fails

Eq. (1.4.3) assumes that the input waveform of every stage is equally steep and that the load is only a lumped capacitance. The situation at a chip pin is more complicated: the bond wire and the package pin have inductance, the PCB trace is a transmission line, and the large current of the last stage produces noise on the supply and ground lines (simultaneous switching noise). A real output buffer therefore not only grows stage by stage but also controls its edge rate and has decoupling capacitors added. These topics belong to courses on signal integrity and packaging; Chapter 4 first treats the RC delay of long on-chip wires.

### 1.4.6 An everyday example

When moving something very heavy, one person alone is too slow; split the job into several legs of a relay, with more and stronger people on each leg, and the whole goes faster. But more people is not always better: each extra leg adds one more handover. The optimum number of stages in an inverter chain is the balance between "the burden of each leg" and "the number of handovers".
