The mechanical properties of a material are read from the tensile curve: the elastic modulus governs stiffness, the yield strength and tensile strength govern strength, and the elongation and reduction of area govern ductility. Hardness is quick to measure and is the main means of checking heat treatment in production; impact toughness governs resistance to impact and to brittle fracture at low temperature; the fatigue limit governs behavior under alternating loads. For steels σ₋₁/σb is {{ex5_1.r_steel_lo:.2f}}–{{ex5_1.r_steel_hi:.2f}}, for aluminum alloys only about 0.3, and aluminum alloys have no true fatigue limit. Stronger materials are often less ductile, and the E of steel hardly changes with grade or heat treatment.

The designation of a steel states its composition, and the main effect of the alloying elements is to raise hardenability. The properties in the tables of the standards are measured on test pieces of a prescribed size (25 mm for quenched and tempered steels); for larger parts the design values must be chosen according to section size. For quenched and tempered 40Cr at sizes up to 100 mm, the yield strength is {{ex5_2.drop40_y:.0f}}% lower than that of the test piece. Heat treatment changes properties by controlling the microstructure: quenching and tempering gives a combination of strength and toughness, while carburizing, nitriding and induction hardening make the surface hard and the core tough. The carburized case depth is roughly proportional to the square root of time; to carburize GR-203 to {{ex5_3.target:.1f}} mm at 930 °C takes at least about {{ex5_3.t930:.1f}} h. Aluminum, titanium and magnesium alloys are light, but their specific stiffness is about the same as steel's; plastics have low stiffness, creep, and are sensitive to heat and humidity; carbon-fiber composites have an extremely high specific stiffness along the fibers, but laid up quasi-isotropically keep only about a 30% advantage. The strength of a printed part depends on the print orientation, and under repeated load it must be designed for fatigue.

Materials selection derives a material index from the function, constraints, objective and free variables, ranks materials on an Ashby chart, and then makes the final decision taking process constraints, cost, joints and other factors into account. When the outside diameter of the forearm of a collaborative robot is limited, the index is E/ρ, and steel, aluminum, magnesium and titanium weigh almost the same; once the minimum wall thickness is added, a steel tube is {{ex5_7.ratio_steel_al:.1f}} times as heavy as an aluminum one. The designations of different countries correspond only approximately, and must be checked item by item before one is substituted for another.

### Historical notes

The history of the human use of metals is the history of materials changing machines. In the Shang and Zhou periods bronzes in China were already made with copper–tin ratios suited to their use; the *Kaogong Ji* (Record of Trades) describes the "six alloys" (*liu qi*), different bronzes for different objects, one of the earliest written records of "choosing the material by its use". It is generally held that the co-fusion (*guangang*) process of steelmaking existed by the Northern and Southern Dynasties (Qiwu Huaiwen); Shen Kuo's *Mengxi Bitan* (Dream Pool Essays) of the Northern Song records "false steel" (*weigang*, that is, co-fusion steel) and "hundred-times-refined steel".

The modern steel industry began in the mid-19th century: in 1856 H. Bessemer invented the converter process, and for the first time steel could be produced cheaply in quantity; the Siemens–Martin open-hearth furnace followed and spread. From the end of the 19th century to the beginning of the 20th, the scientific basis of alloy steels and heat treatment was gradually established: the metallurgical microscope made microstructure visible, and the iron–carbon phase diagram (the work of Roberts-Austen and others) explained why steel can be hardened. In 1906 A. Wilm in Germany discovered the age hardening of aluminum alloys (published in 1911), which made aluminum alloys usable in aircraft structures. After the Second World War titanium alloys, engineering plastics and carbon-fiber composites entered engineering use on a large scale one after another.

Ashby contributed more than anyone to turning materials selection from experience into method: at the end of the 1980s he proposed the method of material indices and material selection charts, published *Materials Selection in Mechanical Design* in 1992 and developed it into selection software; it is now standard content in engineering education throughout the world. Metal additive manufacturing appeared in the 1990s, and over the past decade or so has entered structural parts for aircraft and robots.
