### 5.2.1 Designations of steel

Steel is an alloy of iron and carbon, generally with less than 2.11% carbon; above that it is cast iron. The steels used for machine parts fall into several classes by composition and use, and the designation itself tells the composition:

- **Carbon structural steel** (GB/T 700): Q235, Q275, … Q stands for qufu ("yield"), the number is the lower limit of the yield strength (MPa), and the following letter A, B, C or D is the quality grade (A has no impact test; B, C and D are tested at 20, 0 and −20 °C respectively). It is cheap and easy to weld, and is used for frames, brackets and non-critical shafts. **High-strength low-alloy structural steel** (GB/T 1591), such as Q355, is used for welded frames and bases.
- **Quality carbon structural steel** (GB/T 699): the two digits give the mean carbon content in hundredths of a percent; 45 steel contains about 0.45% carbon. The low-carbon grades (10, 20) are soft, easy to weld and can be carburized; the medium-carbon grades (35, 45), after quenching and tempering, combine strength and toughness and are the most common materials for shafts and gears; the high-carbon grade 65Mn is used for springs.
- **Alloy structural steel** (GB/T 3077): the carbon content in hundredths of a percent followed by the symbols of the alloying elements, an element written without a number meaning less than 1.5%. 40Cr contains about 0.40% carbon and about 1% chromium; 20CrMnTi contains about 0.20% carbon with chromium, manganese and titanium. The most important effect of the alloying elements is to raise the **hardenability**, the depth to which the steel can be hardened by quenching (see the next subsection); chromium, molybdenum and nickel also raise strength and toughness.
- **Stainless steel** (GB/T 1220, GB/T 20878), **tool steel**, **bearing steel** (GCr15), **spring steel** (60Si2Mn) and other special steels.

### 5.2.2 Properties of GB test pieces and design values

The mechanical properties in the standards are measured on test-piece blanks of a prescribed size (25 mm for quenched and tempered steels such as 40Cr, 15 mm for case-hardening steels such as 20CrMnTi) after a prescribed heat treatment. When the part is larger than the test piece, its core cools more slowly in quenching and does not harden through, and the strength after tempering is lower. Design calculations therefore cannot use the figures of the standard directly; the values must be taken from a design handbook according to the section size of the part (this is how the digital factory's material library obtains its values, and each entry states the range of diameters to which it applies).

**Example 5.2.1** Program 5.2.1 puts the GB test-piece values beside the design values of the material library (Figure 5.2.1):

- Quenched and tempered 40Cr: test piece (25 mm) Rm ≥ {{ex5_2.Rm40}} and ReL ≥ {{ex5_2.ReL40}} MPa; the material library, for diameters ≤ 100 mm, gives σb = {{ex5_2.sb40}} and σs = {{ex5_2.sy40}} MPa, lower by {{ex5_2.drop40_b:.0f}}% and {{ex5_2.drop40_y:.0f}}% respectively. The large gear GR-302 of the WQR-105 is a Ø225 × 45 forged blank whose rim thickness lies in this range, so it is calculated with the design values.
- 20CrMnTi: the library value is the core property for diameters ≤ 15 mm (σb = {{ex5_2.sb20}} MPa). Although GR-203 is made from Ø70 bar, what matters for the tooth-root strength is the tooth itself (a module-3 tooth is about 5 mm thick), so this value can be used as an approximation; the strength of a carburized part depends mainly on its case, and the core need only be tough.
- 45 steel: the standard value is for the normalized condition (Rm ≥ {{ex5_2.Rm45}} MPa), while the library value is for the quenched and tempered condition and diameters ≤ 200 mm (σb = {{ex5_2.sb45}} MPa). The conditions differ, so compare with care. 45 steel has poor hardenability, and its core no longer hardens through above a diameter of about 30 mm; the {{ex5_2.sb45}} MPa that the handbook gives for large diameters is already a low value that allows for this. For heavily loaded parts of large section, switch to 40Cr or 42CrMo.

### 5.2.3 Cast iron

In iron–carbon alloys with more than 2.11% carbon, most of the carbon is present as graphite. The shape of the graphite determines the properties of the cast iron:

- **Gray cast iron** (GB/T 9439, such as HT200, the number being the minimum tensile strength): the graphite flakes act like many small cracks, so the tensile strength is low and there is almost no ductility; but it casts well, damps vibration, resists wear and is cheap, and its compressive strength is three to four times its tensile strength. Machine-tool beds and reducer housings (HSG-001 and HSG-002 of the WQR-105) are made of it.
- **Ductile iron** (GB/T 1348, such as QT500-7, the second number being the elongation in %): the graphite is spheroidal, the strength approaches that of medium-carbon steel and there is some ductility. It is used for crankshafts, wheel hubs and heavily loaded housings.
- Malleable iron, compacted graphite iron and alloy cast irons are used for special purposes.

Gray cast iron is a brittle material without a yield strength, and its strength is calculated from the tensile strength (Chapter 10). Its elastic modulus varies with stress; the material library uses the midpoint of the range given in the annex of the standard.

**Engineer's notes**

- In choosing a steel, look first at the section size and whether it must harden through: for small parts 45 steel is enough; heavily loaded parts of large section use 40Cr or 42CrMo; for still larger parts needing a tough core, use nickel–molybdenum steels.
- On a drawing, give the material by its full designation and condition (for example "40Cr, quenched and tempered, 241–286 HBW"), not just "steel" or "alloy steel".
- Design values change with section size: if a part of the same grade is scaled up, look up its strength again.
