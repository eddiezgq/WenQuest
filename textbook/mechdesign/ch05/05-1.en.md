### 5.1.1 The tensile test and the tensile curve

The strength and ductility of a material are measured by the **tensile test** (GB/T 228.1 / ISO 6892-1): a standard specimen is gripped in a testing machine and slowly stretched, the force and the extension are recorded and converted into engineering stress (force divided by the original cross-sectional area) and engineering strain (extension divided by the original gauge length), and the **stress–strain curve** is plotted (Figure 5.1.1a). The main quantities read from the curve are:

- The **elastic modulus** E: the slope of the initial straight portion, a measure of stiffness. For carbon and alloy steels it is about {{ex5_1.E45:.0f}} GPa (somewhat lower for stainless steel, about 193 GPa), for aluminum alloys about {{ex5_1.E_al:.0f}} GPa; steel is {{ex5_1.E_ratio:.1f}} times as stiff as aluminum. Heat treatment and alloying hardly change it.
- The **yield strength** (ReH, ReL, Rp0.2; written σs in the design calculations of this book): the stress at which marked plastic deformation begins. The low-carbon steel Q235 has a distinct yield plateau (GB/T 700 uses the upper yield strength ReH); for materials without a plateau the stress at 0.2% permanent strain, Rp0.2 (the 0.2% offset yield strength), is used.
- The **tensile strength** (Rm, written σb): the highest point of the curve. Beyond it the specimen begins to thin at one place (necking), and the engineering stress falls until fracture.
- The **elongation** A and the **reduction of area** Z: the increase in gauge length after fracture relative to the original gauge length, and the decrease of the cross-sectional area at the fracture relative to the original area; both measure ductility. A material with A greater than about 5% is called a **ductile material**, otherwise a **brittle material** (such as gray cast iron).

**Example 5.1.1** The five curves of Figure 5.1.1a are drawn by Program 5.1.1. The steels use the GB minimum values (for example normalized 45 steel: Rm ≥ {{ex5_1.Rm45}} MPa, ReL ≥ {{ex5_1.ReL45}} MPa, A ≥ {{ex5_1.A45}}%, Z ≥ {{ex5_1.Z45}}%, GB/T 699); the aluminum alloy and the stainless steel use values from the material library of the digital factory. The curves are models: after yield the material hardens according to Swift's law σ = K(ε₀ + εp)ⁿ, with n and K fixed by two conditions, "the highest point equals Rm" and "the strain at the highest point is {{ex5_1.ag_ratio:.0%}} of the elongation after fracture"; after necking the curve is drawn as a straight line to fracture. The fitted strain-hardening exponents n (model values, determined by the assumptions above, lower than measured ones and used only to compare trends) are {{ex5_1.n40:.2f}} for quenched and tempered 40Cr, {{ex5_1.n45:.2f}} for normalized 45 steel and {{ex5_1.n304:.2f}} for 304 stainless steel. Stainless steel can still harden a great deal after yield (work hardening), so its strength rises considerably after cold working; quenched and tempered steel has a high yield strength but little reserve beyond yield.

The figure illustrates a rule of thumb in design: a stronger material is often less ductile. The yield strength of quenched and tempered 40Cr is more than three times that of Q235, but its elongation is only about a third as large. For parts that take impact and must "deform before they break", strength alone is not enough.

### 5.1.2 Hardness

**Hardness** is the resistance of a material to local indentation. It is quick to measure and does not destroy the part, so it can be measured on the part itself, which is why production uses hardness to check the result of heat treatment. Three methods are common:

- **Brinell hardness** (HBW, GB/T 231.1): a cemented-carbide ball is pressed in and the hardness is computed from the diameter of the indentation. The indentation is large and the result representative; it is used for quenched and tempered steels, cast irons and non-ferrous metals. A drawing reads "quenched and tempered, 241–286 HBW".
- **Rockwell hardness** (HRC and others, GB/T 230.1): a diamond cone is pressed in and the reading is taken from the depth of penetration. It is quick and is used for hardened steels, as in "carburized and hardened, 58–62 HRC".
- **Vickers hardness** (HV, GB/T 4340.1): a square-based diamond pyramid is pressed in; the load can be very small, so it is used for thin layers, such as the hardness profile of a carburized or nitrided case.

The hardness of steel is roughly proportional to its tensile strength; σb ≈ 3.3–3.5 HBW (MPa) is a common estimate. Conversion tables exist in several standards (GB/T 1172, ASTM E140), but they are only approximate.

### 5.1.3 Impact toughness

Some parts have enough static strength yet break in a brittle manner under impact. **Impact toughness** (called absorbed impact energy in current standards) is measured by the Charpy pendulum impact test (GB/T 229 / ISO 148-1): a single swing of the pendulum breaks a notched specimen, and the energy absorbed (J) is the absorbed impact energy, for example KU₂ ≥ {{ex5_1.KU45}} J for 45 steel (the impact value in GB/T 699 is for quenched and tempered specimens; to be verified). At low temperature steel changes from tough to brittle (the ductile–brittle transition temperature), so frames and lifting gear working outdoors or in cold regions must be made of steels with guaranteed low-temperature impact properties (for example Q355D and Q355E, whose quality grades D and E require the impact test to be performed at lower temperatures).

### 5.1.4 Fatigue

A part working under alternating stress breaks after enough cycles even if the stress is far below the yield strength; this is the most common form of failure of machine parts (treated in detail in Chapter 11). The resistance of a material to fatigue is expressed by the **fatigue limit** σ₋₁ (the largest stress that a smooth specimen survives for 10⁷ cycles of fully reversed rotating bending).

**Example 5.1.2** Program 5.1.1 computes σ₋₁/σb for each material from the material library of the digital factory (Figure 5.1.1b). For the steels it lies between {{ex5_1.r_steel_lo:.2f}} and {{ex5_1.r_steel_hi:.2f}} (the library's steel data are taken from the measured tables of a design handbook), in agreement with the common estimate σ₋₁ ≈ 0.45σb. For the aluminum alloys it is only {{ex5_1.r_al_lo:.2f}}–{{ex5_1.r_al_hi:.2f}}, and aluminum alloys have no true fatigue limit: the library gives the value at 5 × 10⁸ cycles. The values {{ex5_1.r_ht:.2f}} for gray cast iron and {{ex5_1.r_pa:.2f}} for plastics (such as PA66) are proportional estimates made by the library, not measured values; always read where the data come from. So for aluminum alloy parts under alternating loads, the experience gained with steel cannot simply be carried over.

**Engineer's notes**

- The strengths in drawings and standards are minimum values, not averages. The measured values on a mill certificate are usually higher, but design is based on the minimum values.
- Stiffness is governed by E, strength by σs and σb. Changing to a higher-strength steel does nothing to make a part "deform less": all steels have much the same E.
- Hardness is the most common inspection method in production. Heat-treatment requirements on a drawing should be written as a hardness range, stating where it is to be measured.
