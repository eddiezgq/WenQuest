### 5.7.1 The problem

The forearm of a collaborative robot is a tube, joined at one end to the elbow joint and carrying at the other the three wrist joints and the payload. It must be light: the lighter the forearm, the smaller the motors and reducers of the shoulder and elbow joints, and the smaller the impact if it strikes a person. It must be stiff: if the forearm bends a little, the end point moves a little, and positioning accuracy suffers. Steel, aluminum alloy, magnesium alloy, titanium alloy or carbon fiber: which should it be? Intuition says "carbon fiber is lightest" and "steel is strongest", but neither statement is complete: lightness must be compared "while meeting the same requirements", and strength must be distinguished from stiffness.

### 5.7.2 Function, constraints, objective, free variables

M. F. Ashby organized materials selection into four steps:

1. **Function**: what does the part do? A beam carrying a bending moment (the forearm).
2. **Constraints**: what must be satisfied? Stiffness: the end deflection must not exceed the allowable value; dimensions: the outside diameter is limited by the joints; and process constraints, such as a minimum wall thickness.
3. **Objective**: what is to be minimized or maximized? The mass.
4. **Free variables**: what can the designer adjust? The wall thickness (if the outside diameter is fixed).

Substituting the constraint into the objective and eliminating the free variable leaves the mass as a product of two factors: "geometry and load" and "material". Take a thin-walled tube with fixed outside diameter D and free wall thickness t: the second moment of area is I = πD³t/8, the deflection of the cantilever is δ = FL³/(3EI); setting δ equal to the allowable value, solving for t, and substituting into the mass m = ρπDtL gives

$$m = \frac{8FL^4}{3\delta D^2}\cdot\frac{\rho}{E}$$

The first factor is set by the design requirements and does not depend on the material; the second depends only on the material. Minimizing the mass means maximizing E/ρ, and E/ρ is the **material index** of this problem. If instead "the shape of the section is fixed and its size free" (a solid bar, or a tube with fixed t/D whose diameter may be increased), the same derivation gives the index $E^{1/2}/\rho$; for a plate (thickness free) it is $E^{1/3}/\rho$. The same "light and stiff" leads to different indices under different constraints.

### 5.7.3 Ashby charts

Plotting materials on log–log axes of E and ρ gives an **Ashby chart** (Figure 5.7.1). On logarithmic axes, E/ρ = constant is a straight line of slope 1, $E^{1/2}/\rho$ = constant a line of slope 2, and $E^{1/3}/\rho$ = constant a line of slope 3. If an index line is shifted parallel to itself, all materials on it have the same index, and materials above and to the left of it are better.

The chart shows at a glance that steel, titanium, aluminum and magnesium lie almost on the same E/ρ line: their specific stiffnesses are much alike. The carbon-fiber laminate lies above and to the left of this line, the plastics below it, while spruce (along the grain) also lies close to it. But looking along the lines of slope 2 and 3, the ranking changes: the light materials (magnesium, wood, carbon fiber) are increasingly favored, while steel falls further and further behind.

The material index tells us which material is better "in itself"; process constraints (minimum wall thickness), strength (in this example the strength safety factors of all the materials are in the tens, so stiffness governs), local stability (a steel tube only a few tenths of a millimeter thick has a diameter-to-thickness ratio above a hundred and would buckle locally), joints, cost, heat conduction and collision safety decide the final choice. Real collaborative-robot forearms are mostly made of aluminum alloy: much lighter than steel, cheaper than carbon fiber, easy to machine, able to be made in one piece with the joint housing, and good at conducting heat (the heat of the motors must be carried away). The conclusion of Section 33.10 agrees: the weight saving of the tube comes from "a larger diameter and a thinner wall", and aluminum is chosen for reasons of joints and manufacturing.

### 5.7.5 Steps in materials selection

1. write down the function, constraints, objective and free variables;
2. derive the material index (as many indices as objectives; where they conflict, use weighting or plot a chart of two indices);
3. screen out unsuitable materials by the hard constraints (temperature, corrosion, electrical conductivity, minimum strength, …);
4. rank the materials by the index on the selection chart and keep a few candidates;
5. carry out detailed design for the candidates (process constraints, joints, cost, supply), then decide.

The material library of the digital factory and its finite element service use the same data, so candidate materials can go straight into a simulation check.

---

**Engineer's notes**

- "Light" must always be compared "for the same requirements": write down the constraints clearly first, then derive the index.
- For a tube whose outside diameter is fixed and wall thickness adjustable, E/ρ is what counts; for beams and plates whose section may be enlarged freely, E^(1/2)/ρ and E^(1/3)/ρ count, and light materials gain even more.
- The material ranked first by the index is not necessarily usable: go through the process constraints and the cost.
