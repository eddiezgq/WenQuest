### 5.3.1 Heat treatment changes the microstructure

The same bar of 45 steel can be cut with a hacksaw after annealing, but after quenching a file will hardly bite it. The composition has not changed; what has changed is the internal microstructure. Steel heated above a certain temperature (austenitized) and then cooled at different rates acquires different microstructures: slow cooling gives the softer ferrite and pearlite, rapid cooling (quenching) gives hard and brittle martensite. **Heat treatment** controls the microstructure, and through it the properties, by heating, holding and cooling. The common processes are:

- **Annealing.** Heating followed by slow cooling in the furnace, to lower the hardness, relieve internal stress and make cutting easier. Forged blanks and castings are often annealed first.
- **Normalizing.** Heating followed by cooling in air. The microstructure is refined and the strength is a little higher than after annealing; it is cheap, and is often used as the preliminary heat treatment of medium-carbon steels or as the final treatment of non-critical parts.
- **Quenching.** Heating followed by rapid cooling in water or oil to form martensite. The as-quenched steel is hard but brittle, with high internal stresses, and cannot be put into service in that condition.
- **Tempering.** Reheating after quenching to a temperature below the lower critical temperature (Ac1) and holding there, to reduce brittleness and relieve stress. Low-temperature tempering (150–250 °C) keeps the high hardness and is used for tools and carburized parts; high-temperature tempering (500–650 °C) gives a microstructure combining strength and toughness.
- **Quenching and tempering.** Quenching followed by high-temperature tempering. It is the most common treatment for shafts and gears, for example 40Cr quenched and tempered to 241–286 HBW (the large gear of the WQR-105).

The effect of tempering depends on the combination of temperature and time, and the **tempering parameter** (the Hollomon–Jaffe parameter) combines the two into one number: P = T(C + lg t), where T is the absolute temperature (K), t the time (h), and the constant C lies between 18 and 21 depending on the carbon content, 20 being usual for steels (proposed by Hollomon and Jaffe in 1945). **Example 5.3.1** For 40Cr tempered at 520 °C for 2 h, P = {{ex5_3.P:.0f}}; the same P is reached at 560 °C in only about {{ex5_3.t560_min:.0f}} min. A slightly higher temperature means a much shorter time, which is why the temperature of a tempering furnace must be closely controlled.

### 5.3.2 Surface strengthening

The flanks of gear teeth must be hard (to resist wear and pitting), while the tooth root and the core must be tough (to resist impact and bending fatigue). Through-hardening cannot give both, so only the surface is made hard:

- **Carburizing.** A low-carbon steel (such as 20CrMnTi) is heated to 900–950 °C in a carbon-rich furnace atmosphere; carbon diffuses in from the surface and raises the carbon content of the case to about 0.8–1.0%. After quenching and low-temperature tempering the surface is 58–62 HRC, while the core keeps a tough low-carbon microstructure. Both pinions of the WQR-105 (SH-101 and GR-203) are carburized and hardened.
- **Nitriding.** Nitrogen is diffused into the surface layer at 500–570 °C, forming hard nitrides. The temperature is low and the distortion small, but the hardened layer is thin (a few tenths of a millimeter); it is used for precision parts such as machine-tool spindles and precision lead screws.
- **Induction hardening** (surface hardening). The surface layer is heated rapidly by an induction coil and then quenched with a water spray. It can be applied to medium-carbon steels (45, 40Cr, 42CrMo), is efficient and need not be subcontracted; the shape of the hardened layer is controlled by the coil and the process.
- **Shot peening.** Steel shot strikes the surface at high speed and leaves compressive stress in the surface layer, raising the fatigue strength; it is often used on springs and gear-tooth roots.

### 5.3.3 How long does carburizing take?

Carburizing is a diffusion process: the carbon concentration at the surface is held at the carbon potential of the furnace atmosphere, and the concentration inside varies according to the diffusion equation. The diffusion coefficient of carbon in austenite increases with temperature and with carbon content; this book uses the empirical formula of Tibbetts (1980):

$$D = 0.47\,e^{-1.6C}\exp\!\left(-\frac{37000 - 6600C}{RT}\right)\ \text{cm}^2/\text{s}$$

where C is the carbon content (%) and R = {{ex5_3.R_cal:.3f}} cal/(mol·K). At 930 °C, D = {{ex5_3.D930_02_m:.2f}} × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s where the carbon content is 0.2%, and {{ex5_3.D930_09_m:.2f}} × 10⁻¹¹ m²/s where it is 0.9%.

**Example 5.3.2** The second-stage pinion GR-203 is of 20CrMnTi (about {{ex5_3.C0:.2f}}% carbon) with a module of {{ex5_3.m:.0f}} mm. The effective case depth is usually taken as 0.15m–0.25m, that is {{ex5_3.ecd_lo:.2f}}–{{ex5_3.ecd_hi:.2f}} mm, and {{ex5_3.target:.1f}} mm is required in this example. Program 5.3.1 solves the diffusion equation with a concentration-dependent diffusion coefficient by finite differences (surface carbon potential {{ex5_3.CS:.1f}}%), taking the depth at which the carbon content falls to {{ex5_3.C_ECD:.2f}}% as an approximation of the effective case depth (effective case depth is defined as the depth to 550 HV, GB/T 9450; provided the case is fully hardened, 550 HV corresponds roughly to 0.35%–0.40% carbon, so this is approximate). The results are shown in Figure 5.3.1:

- the time required is about {{ex5_3.t930:.1f}} h at 930 °C and about {{ex5_3.t900:.1f}} h at 900 °C;
- the case depth is roughly proportional to the square root of time: at 930 °C the depth after 8 h is {{ex5_3.r_sqrt:.2f}} times that after 2 h. Doubling the depth takes four times as long.

So a deep case must not be specified lightly: every bit of extra depth multiplies the furnace time, and the distortion grows too. The program assumes that the surface reaches the carbon potential of the atmosphere from the start (it ignores the transfer resistance at the surface), so it gives a lower bound on the time. In production, the boost and diffuse stages generally take 3–4 h, to which must be added the time for heating, cooling, quenching and tempering, and the case depth is checked on test coupons carburized with the load.

**Engineer's notes**

- Heat treatment must be written into the technical requirements of the part drawing: the name of the process, the surface and core hardness (as ranges), the case depth (stating the method of measurement and the limiting hardness), and the inspection locations.
- Quenching causes distortion: carburized and hardened gears generally have to be ground or honed afterward. If high accuracy is required without grinding, choose low-distortion nitriding, or cut the teeth after quenching and tempering.
- Heat treatment is often subcontracted; lead times are long, and the quality has to be controlled by incoming inspection (the digital factory has incoming inspection templates for forged blanks and castings).
