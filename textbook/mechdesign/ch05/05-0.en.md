In the bill of materials of the WQR-105, the output shaft is listed as 45 steel, the large gear as quenched and tempered 40Cr, the pinion as carburized and quenched 20CrMnTi, and the housing as HT200. Why this combination? Would another one do? The forearm of a collaborative robot is aluminum alloy: why not the "sturdier" steel, or the lighter carbon fiber? Half of the answer lies in the material itself: its strength, stiffness, toughness and density, whether it can be heat-treated, whether it can be machined. The other half lies in the requirements and constraints of the part: what loads it carries, how hard and how light it must be, what limits its size, and how much it may cost to make. **Materials selection** is matching the two.

The chapter first explains how the mechanical properties of materials are measured and read (Section 5.1), then covers the steels and cast irons most used in machines (Section 5.2), heat treatment and surface strengthening (Section 5.3), non-ferrous metals (Section 5.4), engineering plastics and composites (Section 5.5) and materials for additive manufacturing (Section 5.6). It ends with a systematic method of selection, material indices and Ashby charts (Section 5.7), and with the correspondence between the material designations of different countries (Section 5.8).

The data come from two sources: the material library of the digital factory (12 materials, each value with its source stated, also used by the finite element service), and the digital tables of this book (materials not in the library, properties of GB test pieces, cross-references of designations, marked "to be verified"). The examples are mostly from robots: lightweight materials selection for the forearm of a collaborative robot runs through Sections 5.5 and 5.7, and the 3D-printed gripper finger appears in Section 5.6; the gears and shafts of the WQR-105 run through Sections 5.2 and 5.3. The everyday example is a bicycle frame (Lab 5.7). In task sheet TS-5-1 of this chapter the reader acts as a materials engineer, compares several materials and heat-treatment routes for the second-stage gear pair of the WQR-105, works out the costs, and writes a materials selection report.

After studying this chapter the reader should be able to:

1. read a tensile curve, and state the meaning and method of measurement of yield strength, tensile strength, elongation, reduction of area, hardness, impact toughness and fatigue limit;
2. state the meaning of the designations of carbon steels, alloy steels and cast irons and their typical uses, and explain why design values must be chosen according to the section size of the part;
3. state the purposes of the common heat treatments and surface strengthening processes, and estimate the relation between case depth and time in carburizing;
4. state the characteristics of aluminum, titanium, magnesium and copper alloys and of engineering plastics, and estimate the stiffness of a composite by the rule of mixtures and lamination theory;
5. describe the anisotropy of materials for additive manufacturing, and check a part according to its print orientation;
6. derive a material index from the function, constraints, objective and free variables, read an Ashby chart, and make a choice taking process constraints into account;
7. look up the approximate correspondence between GB, ASTM/SAE, EN and JIS designations, understanding that correspondence does not mean equivalence.
