### 5.5.1 Engineering plastics

**Engineering plastics** (PA66 nylon, POM acetal, PC, PBT, PEEK and others) are light, corrosion-resistant and self-lubricating, and can be injection-molded into complex shapes in a single operation without further machining; they suit high-volume small gears, sliders, housings and cable clips. Designing with plastics calls for attention to several things that are quite different from metals:

- **Low stiffness.** E is only a few GPa, a few hundredths of that of steel, so the size of structural parts is usually set by stiffness rather than strength.
- **Creep.** Even at room temperature a plastic slowly deforms under sustained load: bolted joints loosen and snap fits relax. Design must use the creep modulus for a specified time (for example 1000 h).
- **Sensitivity to temperature and humidity.** Strength and modulus fall quickly as the temperature rises. Nylon absorbs water; the note in the material library of the digital factory reads "{{ex5_5.pa_note}}": E = {{ex5_5.pa_dry:.1f}} GPa dry and about {{ex5_5.pa_wet:.1f}} GPa wet. Parts for damp environments must be calculated with the wet values, and their dimensions also grow as they absorb water.
- **Large thermal expansion.** The coefficient of linear expansion is five to ten times that of steel, so clearance must be left where plastic parts fit with metal ones.

### 5.5.2 Fiber composites

Fibers of high strength and high modulus (carbon fiber, glass fiber) embedded in a resin form a fiber-reinforced **composite material**. The fibers carry the load; the resin bonds the fibers together, transmits shear and protects the fibers. The properties of the composite are very high along the fibers and very low across them: it exhibits **anisotropy**, and in design the fibers must be oriented to suit the load directions.

The longitudinal modulus of a unidirectional ply (all fibers in one direction) is estimated by the **rule of mixtures**, fibers and resin being loaded in parallel:

$$E_1 = V_f E_f + (1 - V_f) E_m$$

In the transverse direction fibers and resin are loaded roughly in series, and the transverse modulus is far below the longitudinal one; it is usually estimated by the semi-empirical Halpin–Tsai formula. Unidirectional plies of different orientations stacked and pressed into one piece form a **laminate**, whose in-plane stiffness is calculated by **classical lamination theory**. The stiffness matrix of each ply is transformed to common axes according to its ply angle and summed, weighted by ply thickness, giving the in-plane stiffness matrix A of the laminate (which, divided by the total thickness, gives the average stiffness), from which the modulus in each direction is found.

The lesson is a practical one: most of the specific-stiffness advantage of carbon fiber exists only when "the fibers follow the load". Laid up quasi-isotropically so as to carry load in every direction, it is only about 25%–30% better in specific stiffness than aluminum alloy or steel. A composite part therefore needs the load directions to be clear before the lay-up is decided, and this is what makes composite design harder than metal design. Beyond that, joints (holes, inserts, bonding), damage after impact, inspection and cost are all more troublesome than with metals.

**Engineer's notes**

- For plastic parts, consult the supplier's data sheet first and note the test conditions (temperature, humidity, time); the "typical values" in handbooks are often for dry material at room temperature under short-term loading.
- Where a composite part joins a metal part is where trouble is most likely; inserts or metal end fittings must be specially designed.
- Glass fiber is cheap and insulating, with low specific stiffness; carbon fiber is expensive and conductive, with high specific stiffness. They serve different purposes.
