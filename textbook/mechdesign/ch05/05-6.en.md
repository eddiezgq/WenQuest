### 5.6.1 Common materials and processes

**Additive manufacturing** (3D printing) "grows" a part layer by layer without tooling, which suits complex shapes, small batches and prototypes. The common combinations (classified according to ISO/ASTM 52900) are:

- **Material extrusion** (FDM/FFF): molten plastic filament is extruded to build the part. Materials include PLA, ABS, PETG, nylon and chopped-carbon-fiber-reinforced nylon. The machines are cheap, and the strength of the parts is markedly anisotropic.
- **Powder bed fusion**: laser sintering of plastic powder (SLS, usually PA12), nearly isotropic and needing no supports; laser melting of metal powder (SLM/LPBF, usually AlSi10Mg, TC4, 316L or maraging steel), giving parts close to forgings in strength, but with residual stress and porosity, so the parts often need heat treatment, hot isostatic pressing and machining.
- **Vat photopolymerization** (SLA/DLP): high accuracy and good surface finish, but rather brittle materials; used mostly for appearance parts and models.

In the robot industry the most common uses are printed gripper fingers, sensor brackets and cable guides, and lightweight metal structural parts for robot arms and legged robots (shapes produced by topology optimization, Chapters 46 and 47).

### 5.6.2 Anisotropy: the print orientation determines the strength

In an extruded part the filament is continuous within a layer, but the layers are only fused to one another, so the strength is much lower under tension normal to the layers. The note on ABS in the material library of the digital factory says that the interlayer strength of printed parts must be multiplied by a further knockdown factor of about {{ex5_6.k_lay_lo:.1f}}–{{ex5_6.k_lay_hi:.1f}}.

**Example 5.6.1** The fingers of the electric gripper of a collaborative robot are 3D-printed. Treated as a cantilever, a finger is {{ex5_6.L:.0f}} mm long, {{ex5_6.B:.0f}} mm wide and {{ex5_6.h:.0f}} mm thick; the gripping force at the fingertip is {{ex5_6.F:.0f}} N (assumed in this book), and the bending stress at the root is σ = 6FL/(bh²) = {{ex5_6.sigma:.2f}} MPa. Program 5.6.1 compares three options (Figure 5.6.1):

- ABS printed flat (bending stress along the print path, strength taken as {{ex5_6.k_in:.1f}} times the solid value): static safety factor {{ex5_6.S_flat:.2f}};
- ABS printed upright (bending stress normal to the layers, using the lower end, {{ex5_6.k_lay_lo:.1f}}, of the interlayer knockdown factor): only {{ex5_6.S_up:.2f}};
- PA12, laser sintered (nearly isotropic; the {{ex5_6.pa12_sb:.0f}} MPa in the table of this book is already the tensile strength of sintered parts, which have no distinct yield point): {{ex5_6.S_pa:.2f}}.

A gripper closes thousands of times a day, so the finger carries a pulsating load from 0 to maximum: the stress amplitude and the mean stress are both σ/2. The fatigue strength of ABS in the material library, {{ex5_6.abs_s1}} MPa, is estimated as 30% of the yield strength (not a measured value), and that of PA12 is estimated with the same ratio. Allowing for the mean stress by the Goodman line (Chapter 11), 1/S = σa/σ₋₁ + σm/σb, the fatigue safety factors of the three options are {{ex5_6.Sf_flat:.2f}}, {{ex5_6.Sf_up:.2f}} and {{ex5_6.Sf_pa:.2f}}. None reaches the required {{ex5_6.S_req:.1f}}, and the upright print does not even reach 1. To reach {{ex5_6.S_req:.1f}}, flat-printed ABS must be thickened to {{ex5_6.h_flat:.1f}} mm, upright-printed ABS to {{ex5_6.h_up:.1f}} mm and PA12 to {{ex5_6.h_pa:.1f}} mm. Plastics also creep under a sustained mean stress, which must be considered for grippers that keep a part clamped for long periods.

The example illustrates three rules of design for printed parts: make the principal stress run along the print path, not normal to the layers; design parts under repeated load for fatigue, remembering that the fatigue strength of plastics is very low; and when the data are uncertain, test prototype parts. The print parameters (layer thickness, infill, temperature) have a large effect on strength, and handbook data are only a guide.

**Engineer's notes**

- The drawing or print instructions of a printed part must state the print orientation, layer thickness, infill and material; otherwise a different printer or a different operator produces a different part.
- Metal printed parts generally need stress-relief annealing, and mating faces and threaded holes must be machined.
- Printing suits small batches and complex shapes; once the same part is needed in thousands, injection molding or machining is usually cheaper (Chapter 6).
