### 33.3.1 The problem

When we laid out the structure in Section 33.2 we simply used the diameters Ø30, Ø35 and Ø40. When a new shaft is really being designed, where do these numbers come from? The layout needs a starting point: we must know at least roughly how thick the thinnest segment is before we can add steps one after another. But an accurate strength calculation needs the bending moment first, and the bending moment depends on the span and on where the parts sit, which are not known until the structure is fixed.

This is the familiar "chicken and egg" problem of design. The engineer's way out is to estimate the minimum diameter with a rough formula that needs only power and speed, lay out the structure on that basis, and then come back for an accurate check, changing the design if it fails. This section discusses the rough formula and why it errs on the conservative side.

### 33.3.2 Diameter from torque

For a solid round shaft carrying a torque $T$, the maximum torsional shear stress at the surface is

$$
\tau = \frac{T}{W_\mathrm T} = \frac{16\,T}{\pi d^3} . \tag{33.3.1}
$$

The torque follows from power and speed. The power $P$ (kW), the speed $n$ (r/min) and the torque $T$ (N·m) are related by $P = T\omega$ with $\omega = 2\pi n/60$, which gives

$$
T = 9550\,\frac{P}{n} . \tag{33.3.2}
$$

Require $\tau$ not to exceed the allowable value $[\tau]$, substitute Eq. (33.3.2) into Eq. (33.3.1), convert $T$ to N·mm (multiply by 1000), and solve:

$$
d \ge \sqrt[3]{\frac{16 \times 9550 \times 10^3}{\pi\,[\tau]}}\;\sqrt[3]{\frac{P}{n}} = A_0\,\sqrt[3]{\frac{P}{n}} \quad (\text{mm}) . \tag{33.3.3}
$$

The coefficient $A_0$ depends only on the allowable shear stress. Many handbooks tabulate it by material; deriving it from $[\tau]$ shows what it means.

### 33.3.3 Why the allowable shear stress is taken so low

Eq. (33.3.3) accounts only for the torque. It ignores the bending moment, stress concentration and fatigue. To make the estimate "good enough", the allowable shear stress $[\tau]$ is taken much lower than the strength of the material: for 45 steel it is generally {{ex33_3.tau_lo:.0f}}–{{ex33_3.tau_hi:.0f}} MPa, whereas quenched and tempered 45 steel has a torsional fatigue limit of 155 MPa and a torsional yield strength of about 200 MPa. This low value in effect "packs in" the bending moment, the stress concentration and the fluctuation of the load. Correspondingly, $A_0$ = {{ex33_3.A0_lo:.0f}}–{{ex33_3.A0_hi:.0f}}: when $[\tau]$ is taken at the high end, $A_0$ is taken at the low end, for shaft segments with a small bending moment or carrying torque only; otherwise take the high end of $A_0$.

When one keyway is cut in the shaft, increase the diameter by 5%; for two keyways, by 10%. The estimated diameter must also be rounded up and coordinated with the bores of the mating parts: bearing bore diameters are multiples of 5, and couplings and motor shaft extensions come in standard series.

### 33.3.4 Worked example

**Example 33.3.1** Estimate the minimum diameter of the WQR-105 output shaft.

The rated output torque is $T$ = {{ex33_3.T:.0f}} N·m and the output speed is $n$ = 1450/10.5 = {{ex33_3.n:.1f}} r/min. From Eq. (33.3.2) the corresponding power is $P = Tn/9550$ = {{ex33_3.P:.2f}} kW.

From Eq. (33.3.3):

$$
d \ge ({{ex33_3.A0_lo:.0f}} \sim {{ex33_3.A0_hi:.0f}}) \times \sqrt[3]{\frac{ {{ex33_3.P:.2f}} }{ {{ex33_3.n:.1f}} }} = {{ex33_3.d_lo:.1f}} \sim {{ex33_3.d_hi:.1f}}\ \mathrm{mm} .
$$

The extension has one keyway, so after the 5% increase the result is {{ex33_3.d_lo_k:.1f}}–{{ex33_3.d_hi_k:.1f}} mm.

This result is larger than the Ø30 of the extension on both version A and version B. Is Ø30 not enough? We cannot conclude yet. The extension carries torque only and no bending moment, so the upper limit of the allowable stress may be used, and the estimate is {{ex33_3.d_lo_k:.1f}} mm, nearly 20% above 30 mm. The nominal torsional shear stress of the Ø30 extension is {{ex33_3.tau_30:.0f}} MPa (Figure 33.3.1), which exceeds the upper limit of the $[\tau]$ used in the estimate. The estimate gives a warning: the extension is a place that needs a careful check. The fatigue check in Section 33.5 and the life calculation in Section 33.8 will confirm this warning.

### 33.3.5 Two other starting points

Besides Eq. (33.3.3), engineers commonly use two other ways to fix the first diameter:

1. **Fix it from the mating part.** The motor shaft extension determines the bore of the coupling, and hence the extension of the reducer input shaft; the bearing bore series determines the diameter at the bearing seats. The extension of the WQR-105 output shaft is fixed by the bore of the coupling that goes with it.
2. **Fix it from empirical proportions.** In a general reducer, the extension of the high-speed input shaft is about 0.8–1.2 times the motor shaft diameter; the diameters of the low-speed shafts at each stage can be estimated as 0.3–0.4 times the center distance. The center distance of the WQR-105 low-speed stage is 135 mm, and the Ø40 at the gear seat of the output shaft is about 0.3 times it.

If the numbers obtained by the three methods differ greatly, some assumption is unreasonable, and it must be thought through before going on.

**Everyday example.** A car handbook gives the maximum engine power and the speed at which it occurs, for example 110 kW at 5500 r/min. By Eq. (33.3.2), the torque at the crankshaft is then 191 N·m. In first gear the transmission ratio is about 3.5, and after the final drive of about 4, the total torque shared by the two half-shafts is more than ten times the engine torque. The more the speed is reduced, the more the torque is multiplied. That is why the half-shafts of a car are sized for the torque when pulling away in first gear (engine torque × first-gear ratio × final drive ratio), and are much thicker than the high-speed shafts in the transmission.

**Engineer's notes**

- Eq. (33.3.3) is for laying out the structure, not for checking. A diameter that passes it is not thereby safe; one that fails it is not necessarily unsafe.
- Use the power that actually reaches this shaft, after deducting the efficiency of the preceding stages, and use the calculated torque enlarged by the application factor (the rated 350 N·m of the WQR-105 already includes the application factor $K_\mathrm A$). $K_\mathrm A$ accounts for long-term average overload (for example the uneven load of a conveyor); it does not include the shock at starting. The starting peak recorded on the run-in test bench is more than 60% above the rated value (Sections 33.4 and 33.8), and such short-time peaks must be checked separately for static strength and for fatigue life.
- The lower the speed, the larger the torque: the low-speed shaft of a reducer is always the thickest shaft.
