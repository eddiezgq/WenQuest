### 33.8.1 The problem

The calculations in the earlier sections all rest on nominal stresses from beam theory, corrected for stress concentration by factors. This method has been used for more than a hundred years and is reliable and transparent, but two questions deserve to be asked. First, do the nominal stresses and the factors really describe this shaft? For example, the gear force is not concentrated at a point, and the torque enters along the whole hub. Second, Section 33.5 designed for "infinite life", assuming that every start and stop is a full-rated pulsating cycle. In a real load spectrum, how many large cycles are there and how many small ones? The factor of safety at Section IV is below 1.5: how long can it actually last?

This section answers with two tools: a finite element model of the whole shaft, and a fatigue life calculation based on a measured load spectrum. Both are tools in "Simulation & Analysis" of the digital factory (factory Lab 8), and the program in this section calls the same code.

### 33.8.2 A finite element model of the whole shaft

Program 33.8.1 uses Gmsh to build a three-dimensional solid model of version B: seven cylindrical segments, with fillets at the five steps as in Section 33.2. The keyway is not modeled (its effect is added separately later). The mesh is of second-order tetrahedra, refined at the fillets, with {{ex33_8.n_el:.0f}} elements in all. The boundary conditions and loads are treated as follows:

- **Bearings.** At the middle of each bearing seat a ring of nodes 2 mm wide is taken and only the radial displacement is constrained (the radial degree of freedom in a cylindrical coordinate system). The tangential and axial displacements are free, so the rotation of the section is not constrained, which agrees with the "simple support" assumption of Section 33.4. Two constraint equations are added to the ring at the left bearing: the sum of the tangential displacements of all its nodes is zero, and the sum of the axial displacements is zero. This removes only the two rigid-body motions of the whole shaft, rotation about the axis and translation along the axis. It does not clamp the section, as fixing the axial displacement of every node in the ring would, which would produce a spurious constraint moment.
- **Gear.** The gear force (the resultant of the tangential and radial forces) is applied as a uniform surface load on the cylindrical surface 45 mm wide (the face width) at the middle of the gear seat. The torque is applied as a tangential surface load around the circumference on the same surface, with a resultant moment of 350 N·m and a resultant force of zero.
- **Coupling.** Equal and opposite tangential surface loads are applied on the cylindrical surface of the last 20 mm at the right end of the overhang, balancing the torque at the gear. The external loads on the whole model balance each other, and the constraint forces at the two rings of bearing nodes are the support reactions.

All these treatments are approximations. A real hub transmits force to the shaft through the interference fit and the key, and the contact pressure is not uniform. The coupling torque also enters along the length of the key. Chapter 13 discussed that stresses near constraints and loads cannot be trusted, so the results must be read at some distance from them (in this example the last 25 mm at the right end is discarded).

### 33.8.3 Finite element results and beam theory

**Example 33.8.1** Comparing the surface stresses.

First the support reactions. The sums of the constraint forces on the two rings of bearing nodes are $R_A$ = {{ex33_8.RA_fe:.0f}} N and $R_B$ = {{ex33_8.RB_fe:.0f}} N. The hand calculation of Section 33.4 gave {{ex33_8.RA_hand:.0f}} N and {{ex33_8.RB_hand:.0f}} N, a difference of {{ex33_8.err_RA:+.1f}}% and {{ex33_8.err_RB:+.1f}}%. The difference comes from the way the gear force acts: in the finite element model it is spread over a face 45 mm wide, whereas the hand calculation concentrates it at the mid-point of the gear. That the reactions agree shows that the constraints do not "clamp" the shaft and that the bending moment is transmitted correctly. This is the first piece of evidence that the finite element model can be trusted.

Figure 33.8.1 plots the von Mises stress at the surface nodes against the axial coordinate; the black line is the nominal stress from the beam theory of Section 33.4 (as in Section 33.4, the torque is assumed to enter in one step at the mid-point of the gear). Some observations:

1. In smooth segments away from the steps the two almost coincide. At the middle of the overhang the finite element value is {{ex33_8.vm_ext:.1f}} MPa and the nominal value $\sqrt3\,T/W_\mathrm T$ is {{ex33_8.vm_ext_nom:.1f}} MPa, a difference of {{ex33_8.err_ext:.2f}}%. This is the second piece of evidence.
2. At the gear seat, to the left of the mid-point the finite element stress is lower than the nominal value and increases from left to right. In the model the torque enters uniformly along the whole face width, so only half of the torque has passed the mid-point of the gear, while the black line assumes that the whole torque has already passed everything to the right of the mid-point. A real hub also transmits torque through the key and the interference fit, and the entry of torque is likewise distributed, so the assumption of Section 33.4 is conservative here.
3. On the small-diameter side of each step there is a stress peak: {{ex33_8.hot_II:.0f}} MPa at Section II, {{ex33_8.hot_III:.0f}} MPa at Section III, and {{ex33_8.hot_IV:.0f}} MPa at the shoulder at the root of the overhang. The ratios to the nominal values are {{ex33_8.Kfe_II:.2f}}, {{ex33_8.Kfe_III:.2f}} and {{ex33_8.Kfe_IV:.2f}} respectively. Sections II and III carry both bending and torsion, and their ratios fall between the $\alpha_\sigma$ and $\alpha_\tau$ found from the local models of Section 33.5. The shoulder at the root of the overhang (Ø35 → Ø30, r = 1 mm) carries torsion only, and its ratio shows that the stress concentration at this shoulder is smaller than that of the keyway near the same section (Section 33.5 took $\alpha_\tau$ = 3.0), which is why Section 33.5 placed Section IV at the keyway.
4. On the large-diameter side, close to the outer corner, the stress is in fact very low: the stress "flow" goes around the corner and the material there is hardly loaded. This is exactly where material can be removed in a lightweight design (Chapter 39).

### 33.8.4 The measured load spectrum

In the simulation workshop of the digital factory, every reducer is put through a "duty simulation" on the run-in test bench (test-01) before it leaves the factory. It simulates the start, run and stop of a belt conveyor, repeated three times, and records the output shaft torque (10 Hz, 75 s), which is sent over the unified data bus (message type `test.torque`). Program 33.8.1 calls the very function with which the simulation workshop generates this record and obtains the curve on the left of Figure 33.8.2. Each start has a shock of about 1.5–1.7 times the rated value, with a maximum of {{ex33_8.T_max:.0f}} N·m. During running the torque fluctuates around 0.75 times the rated value, and after the stop it returns to zero. The mean torque over the whole record is {{ex33_8.T_mean:.0f}} N·m.

Rainflow counting (Chapter 11) breaks this record into individual cycles. The record is treated as a "block" that repeats again and again, and each block contains {{ex33_8.n_cycles}} cycles (Figure 33.8.2, right). The great majority are small fluctuations during running, with ranges of only a few tens of N·m. The cycles that really do damage are the three large start–stop cycles, the largest of which has an amplitude of {{ex33_8.big_amp:.0f}} N·m and a mean of {{ex33_8.big_mean:.0f}} N·m.

### 33.8.5 Fatigue life

**Example 33.8.2** Calculating the life of SH-301 from the load spectrum.

The method is the same as the fatigue calculation in "Simulation & Analysis" of the digital factory (factory Lab 8):

1. Linear elasticity: the stress at any moment = finite element stress × torque at that moment / 350 N·m (the gear force is proportional to the torque).
2. The stress amplitude of each rainflow cycle is corrected by the Goodman line to an equivalent fully reversed stress amplitude.
3. The S-N curve of the part has its knee at the part's endurance limit $S_\mathrm D = \sigma_{-1}\beta\varepsilon/K_\mathrm f$, a slope $m$ = 9 and a reference cycle count of 10⁷. Here $K_\mathrm f$ is the fatigue stress-concentration factor, $\beta$ = {{ex33_8.beta_fac:.2f}} is the surface factor for a ground surface, and $\varepsilon$ is the size factor. The three are multiplied together, as written in [SHI], which is slightly more optimistic than the "addition" of Eq. (33.5.4). Cycles below the knee are not entirely harmless: small cycles below the knee can still make the microcracks created by large cycles grow. So, following Haibach's suggestion, damage below the knee continues to be counted with a shallower slope of 2m − 1.
4. Miner's rule of linear accumulation: the damage per block is $D = \sum n_i/N_i$, and the life is $1/D$ blocks.

The stress concentration at the step fillets is already in the finite element result, so $K_\mathrm f$ is taken as 1. This is equivalent to taking the notch sensitivity $q$ = 1, that is, treating the whole theoretical stress concentration as effective, which is conservative (the size factor is taken uniformly for Ø35). The keyway on the overhang is not modeled; it is accounted for by multiplying the von Mises stress of the overhang by the fatigue stress-concentration factor $k_\tau$ = {{ex33_8.kt_IV:.2f}} of Section 33.5 ($\varepsilon$ = {{ex33_8.eps30:.3f}}, ground). The results (a life is meaningful only in order of magnitude, and is given to three significant figures) are:

| Location | 45 steel, quenched and tempered | 40Cr, quenched and tempered |
|---|---|---|
| Step fillet (most critical point) | {{ex33_8.L45_fil_r:.0f}} h | more than 10⁶ h |
| Keyway on the overhang | {{ex33_8.L45_key_r:.0f}} h | {{ex33_8.L40_key_r:.0f}} h |

The required life is {{ex33_8.life_req:.0f}} h (set by the customer, and the same as the default bearing life in the parameter configurator of the digital factory). The keyway on the overhang in 45 steel lasts only about {{ex33_8.L45_key_r:.0f}} h and does not meet the requirement. With quenched and tempered 40Cr it lasts about {{ex33_8.L40_key_r:.0f}} h, which does, and the life at the step fillet is far more than enough.

This load spectrum is "severe": a start and stop every 25 s is equivalent to {{ex33_8.starts_test:.0f}} per hour. In the damage per block at the overhang keyway, the three large start–stop cycles account for {{ex33_8.big_share:.0f}}%, and the life is roughly inversely proportional to the start–stop frequency. If a real conveyor starts and stops 10 times an hour, the keyway life in 45 steel is about {{ex33_8.L45_key_10ph_w:.0f}} × 10⁴ h, which also meets the requirement. The conclusion therefore depends on the duty: where starts and stops are frequent, SH-301 must change to 40Cr; where they are rare, 45 steel is sufficient.

### 33.8.6 The design decision

Putting together the results of Sections 33.3 to 33.8:

| Method | Conclusion for Section IV |
|---|---|
| Rough estimate from torsional strength (33.3) | Ø30 is smaller than the estimated minimum diameter: warning |
| Equivalent bending moment method (33.4) | Exceeds the allowable value: not enough |
| Factor of safety method (33.5) | $S_\mathrm{ca}$ < 1.5: not enough |
| Modified Goodman method (33.5) | Even smaller: not enough |
| Life from load spectrum (33.8, severe duty) | Does not meet 10 000 h: not enough; 40Cr meets it |

When several methods point to the same weak spot, one can make a decision with confidence. The engineering decision is: change the material of SH-301 version B to quenched and tempered 40Cr. The reasons are that WQR-105 is a general-purpose product, the customer's start–stop frequency cannot be controlled, so the design is based on the severe duty; and changing the material changes no mating part, so the cost increase is small. If the conclusions of the methods contradict one another, one must go back and check which assumption is wrong, and test if necessary. In that case the simulation workshop of the digital factory can arrange a "severe run-in" and record the real torque.

### 33.8.7 Engineering practice: one task sheet through the whole chain

The last steps of Task Sheet TS-33-1 are the content of this section, and they carry the design into manufacturing:

1. **Finite element analysis and fatigue.** In "Simulation & Analysis" of the digital factory, calculate the version B model with the boundary conditions of this section and download the Word calculation report. Calculate the life from the torque record of the run-in test bench, compare it with the results of this section, and explain the difference.
2. **Drawings.** Dimension the part drawing to the national standard: dimensions, tolerance zones, geometric tolerances (circular run-out of the bearing seats, symmetry of the keyway), surface roughness, fillets, and technical requirements (40Cr quenched and tempered, 241–286 HB).
3. **Submit for review.** Submit the model, drawings, calculation report and analysis report together to "Design Release & Approval". The AI reviewer first checks them item by item against the review points of the task sheet, and after the teacher approves, the version of SH-301 in ERPNext goes up by one, the material changes to 40Cr, and the inspection template is updated accordingly.
4. **Manufacture and inspection.** The work order goes to the simulation workshop, and the keyway is machined by a CAM program (Chapter 43). A coordinate measuring machine inspects the bearing seats, the gear seat and the keyway width, and the inspection data return to the task page. If an inspection fails, the nonconforming product is handled by the disposition procedure (Chapter 44).

With this step the whole process of a shaft, from calculation to manufacture, has been completed.

**Everyday example** In a car's maintenance manual, "stop-and-go city traffic" and "frequent short trips" are listed as "severe duty", and a shorter service interval is required. The reasoning is the same as in this section: the life of a part depends not only on how strong it is but also on how it is used. The number of large cycles experienced by the drive shaft of a car that starts and stops dozens of times a day differs many times over from that of a car that runs several hundred kilometers a day at constant speed on a motorway.

**Engineer's notes**

- Do a "sanity check" on finite element results first: compare with the hand calculation away from stress concentrations (here {{ex33_8.err_ext:.2f}}%), compare the support reactions with the hand calculation (here about 1%), and check that the direction of deformation is right. Only when these agree should the peak values be examined.
- Fatigue life is very sensitive to stress (a 10% difference in stress makes a factor of 2–3 in life), so the calculated "hours" can be used only as an order of magnitude, to compare designs and find weak spots. Absolute values must be verified by tests.
- The load spectrum is the most uncertain input to fatigue design. Collecting real torque records from the equipment after a product is in service (as the run-in test bench of the digital factory does) is the most valuable data for improving the next generation of the design.
