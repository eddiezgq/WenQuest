### 33.1.1 The problem

In the digital factory the output shaft SH-301 of the WQR-105 is turned from 45 steel bar, quenched and tempered, and ground (BOM: RM-45-D50). A customer would like the rated torque of the reducer raised a little, and the process planner suggests changing the material to quenched and tempered 40Cr: it is stronger, and the shaft does not need new dimensions. This does help strength, but does it help stiffness? How much does the cost rise? To answer these questions we must first be clear what loads a shaft carries and which properties of the material really decide its load capacity.

### 33.1.2 Types of shafts

According to the loads they carry, shafts fall into three kinds (Figure 33.1.1):

- An **axle** carries bending moment only and transmits no torque. An axle may rotate (such as the axle of a railway vehicle) or stand still (such as the axle of a pulley).
- A **transmission shaft** mainly transmits torque and carries practically no bending moment, such as the propeller shaft between a car gearbox and the rear axle.
- A **rotating shaft** carries bending moment and transmits torque at the same time; the great majority of the shafts in reducers, machine-tool headstocks and robot joints are rotating shafts.

SH-301 is a rotating shaft: the force on the gear bends it, and the torque coming from the gear passes through it to the coupling. Note that bending moment and torque are distributed differently. The bending moment exists only between the two bearings and peaks at the gear; the torque starts at the gear and extends to the coupling, and the overhanging part carries torque only. This difference decides where the critical sections lie in the sections that follow.

By the form of the axis, shafts are also straight shafts, crankshafts (engines, compressors) and flexible wire shafts; by cross-section, solid shafts and hollow shafts. For the same section modulus in bending a hollow shaft is much lighter, so hollow shafts are common in aircraft and in lightweight robot joints, where cables are also run through the middle. This chapter deals only with the commonest case, the solid stepped straight shaft.

### 33.1.3 Shaft materials

The commonest shaft materials are quality carbon steels and alloy steels. Four things must be considered together in choosing: strength (especially fatigue strength), stiffness, sensitivity to stress concentration, and manufacturability and price.

**Example 33.1.1** Compare the advantages and disadvantages of quenched and tempered 45 steel and quenched and tempered 40Cr for SH-301.

From the material library of the digital-factory finite-element service (from the same source as the `mdstd` module of this book; for the origin of the data see Chapter 5): for quenched and tempered 45 steel the tensile strength $\sigma_\mathrm b$ = {{ex33_1.sb_45:.0f}} MPa, the yield strength $\sigma_\mathrm s$ = {{ex33_1.ss_45:.0f}} MPa, the fatigue limit in fully reversed bending $\sigma_{-1}$ = {{ex33_1.s1_45:.0f}} MPa and the torsional fatigue limit $\tau_{-1}$ = {{ex33_1.tau1_45:.0f}} MPa; for quenched and tempered 40Cr they are {{ex33_1.sb_40:.0f}}, {{ex33_1.ss_40:.0f}}, {{ex33_1.s1_40:.0f}} and {{ex33_1.tau1_40:.0f}} MPa respectively. The modulus of elasticity of both is {{ex33_1.E_45:.0f}} GPa (Figure 33.1.2).

With 40Cr the fatigue limit rises to {{ex33_1.gain_strength:.2f}} times its former value, while the ratio of moduli is {{ex33_1.gain_stiff:.2f}}: the stiffness is not improved at all. As for the price of raw material, by the unit prices of the digital factory's bill of materials, 45 steel bar costs {{ex33_1.price_45:.2f}} USD/kg; 40Cr is not listed separately, and is estimated at the {{ex33_1.price_40:.2f}} USD/kg of the similar alloy steel 20CrMnTi, which is {{ex33_1.price_ratio:.1f}} times that of 45 steel.

There are two cases. If the dimensions of the shaft are set by strength (as in most reducer shafts; Section 33.5 will show that the overhanging part of SH-301 is one), changing to alloy steel is an effective measure, and the increase in material cost is a small part of the cost of the whole reducer. If the dimensions are set by stiffness (slender machine-tool lead screws, the long link shafts of robots), changing to alloy steel is useless, and the only remedies are a larger diameter or a shorter span.

Where the materials are used:

1. **45 steel.** After quenching and tempering (quenching followed by high-temperature tempering) its mechanical properties are well balanced and its price is low, so it is the first choice for general reducer shafts and ordinary machine-tool shafts. The normalized state can also be used for lightly loaded shafts.
2. **40Cr, 35SiMn, 40MnB and other quenched and tempered alloy steels.** Used for heavily loaded shafts and where compact size or low weight is required, for example in cars, construction machinery and robot joints. Alloy steels are more sensitive to stress concentration, so the requirements on fillets and surface roughness are stricter (the notch sensitivity factor of Section 33.5 will explain why).
3. **20CrMnTi, 20Cr and other case-hardening steels.** After carburizing and quenching the surface is hard and wear-resistant while the core stays tough. They are used for gear shafts (gear and shaft made in one piece, like the input gear shaft SH-101 of the WQR-105) and for splined shafts that must resist wear. The fatigue limit of {{ex33_1.s1_cr:.0f}} MPa given by the material library is the core value of a small specimen under 15 mm in diameter; a large part does not harden through, and its core strength is much lower, so the value for the actual part size must be looked up in design.
4. **Spheroidal graphite cast iron.** For example QT500-7, with fatigue limit {{ex33_1.s1_qt:.0f}} MPa and modulus of elasticity {{ex33_1.E_qt:.0f}} GPa. It casts well, is insensitive to notches and damps vibration, and can be used for crankshafts and camshafts of complicated shape.
5. **Stainless steel, titanium alloys, aluminium alloys, carbon-fibre composites.** Used where corrosion resistance (food and medical machinery) or light weight (robots, aircraft) is needed; Chapter 46 discusses them further.

### 33.1.4 Heat treatment and surface strengthening

Fatigue cracks in shafts almost always start at the surface, so raising the strength and the compressive stress of the surface layer is especially effective for fatigue strength. The usual methods are surface hardening (induction hardening, hardened layer 1–3 mm), carburizing and quenching, nitriding, shot peening and rolling. Rolling and shot peening leave residual compressive stress in the surface layer and can raise the fatigue strength by 20%–50%, with a particularly marked effect at stress concentrations such as fillets and keyways. In the calculations of Section 33.5 such strengthening is taken into account by a strengthening factor greater than 1.

### 33.1.5 Engineering practice

The first step of task sheet TS-33-1 is "confirmation of material and process": open the item master data and the routing of SH-301 in the digital factory's ERPNext (cutting-off, rough turning, quenching and tempering, finish turning, milling the keyway, grinding the journals, part inspection), check the material grade and the hardness after tempering (inspection template: 217–255 HB), and write the comparison of this section into the "Material" entry of the calculation report. Whether to change to 40Cr is left until the results of Sections 33.5 and 33.8 are available.

**Everyday example** The axles of bicycles are mostly of medium-carbon or chromium–molybdenum steel, while the hub axles of racing bicycles are often of aluminium alloy or even titanium alloy. The aluminium axle is thicker, because the modulus of elasticity of aluminium is only a third of that of steel and the stiffness has to be kept by a larger diameter; the titanium axle strikes a balance between strength, weight and corrosion resistance, and costs a great deal more.

---

**Engineer's notes**

- The first question in choosing a material is "what decides the size?": if strength, consider alloy steel and surface strengthening; if stiffness, only a change of structure will help.
- A quenched and tempered alloy steel must always go with a better surface finish (grinding) and larger fillets, or most of its strength advantage will be eaten up by stress concentration.
- Writing "quenched and tempered, 217–255 HB" on a drawing is better than writing only "quenched and tempered": hardness is a quantity the workshop can inspect, and the records of the coordinate-measuring machine and the hardness tester go automatically into the quality records of the digital factory.
