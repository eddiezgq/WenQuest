### 33.10.1 The problem

SH-301 is a shaft in a speed reducer: it turns slowly and in one direction, its loads come from gears, and its size is decided by fatigue strength. The shaft in a robot joint is quite a different case. Take a collaborative robot with a reach of {{ex33_10.reach:.0f}} mm and a payload of {{ex33_10.payload:.0f}} kg (parts library [[part:B-ARM-UR5E]]). The shoulder joint J2 lifts the whole arm: the servo motor transmits torque through a harmonic drive to an output shaft, the output shaft is carried by two bearings in the joint housing, and its other end is joined to the upper arm by a flange. This shaft turns forward and backward tens of thousands of times a day, and the torque comes from the weight of the arm and from acceleration and deceleration; cables pass through the middle, so the shaft is hollow; and since the reach is close to 1 m, a small twist of the shaft moves the end point a long way.

In this section we design the output shaft of the shoulder joint of this robot, numbered RJ-201 (a design of this book, unrelated to the actual product of any robot maker), and answer four questions: where do the loads come from? Is the reducer chosen correctly? What decides the diameter of the shaft? Steel shaft or aluminium shaft?

### 33.10.2 Loads: the torque curve from motion planning

The loads on a joint shaft are not looked up in a handbook; they are decided by what the robot does. Program 33.10.1 uses the same model and solver as "Multibody Dynamics" of the digital factory (MuJoCo, Chapter 12; factory Lab 9) to compute a handling cycle: the end point carries {{ex33_10.payload:.0f}} kg, goes from station A to station B in {{ex33_10.move_s:.0f}} s, dwells for {{ex33_10.dwell_s:.1f}} s, and returns to A, so one cycle takes {{ex33_10.cycle_s:.0f}} s. The two postures are: A is {{ex33_10.pose_a}}; B is {{ex33_10.pose_b}}. The result is the driving torque of J2 and the force the upper arm transmits to the output shaft (Figure 33.10.1):

- the largest absolute value of the torque is $T_\mathrm{max}$ = {{ex33_10.T_peak:.1f}} N·m, at the moment when the arm is stretched farthest and is accelerating; during the dwell the torque is not zero, because it has to hold up the weight of the arm;
- the torque always has the same direction: the weight of the arm must be held up all the time, and the minimum is still {{ex33_10.T_min:.0f}} N·m (absolute value). Although the arm turns back and forth, the torque does not alternate between positive and negative but fluctuates about a large mean value. This is an irregular, asymmetric cyclic load with a large mean stress and a small stress amplitude, different from the zero-to-peak load of SH-301 at starting and stopping;
- the largest force the upper arm transmits to the shaft is {{ex33_10.F_peak:.0f}} N; it is carried by the two bearings and produces a bending moment at the outer bearing;
- the largest angular velocity of J2 is {{ex33_10.w_max_deg:.0f}} °/s, much slower than the reducer shaft.

This curve is the "load spectrum" of RJ-201. In the digital factory, one run of "Multibody Dynamics" can do only one motion segment for each joint, so we first compute the segment A → B (motion {{ex33_10.move_s:.0f}} s, dwell {{ex33_10.dwell_s:.1f}} s, 1.5 s in all); in the fatigue calculation of "Simulation & Analysis", choose the "Joint-torque record" as the load spectrum, select this run and J2, and the platform repeats this segment as a block again and again to compute the life (operation (9) of Section 33.9.5). The torque curve of the return B → A is different, and the program in the book computes both segments; with either approach the life far exceeds the requirement (33.10.5).

### 33.10.3 The harmonic drive: checking against the parts-library data

J2 uses the harmonic drive CSF-25-100 (parts library [[part:D-RDC-HD-CSF/25-100]], reduction ratio {{ex33_10.hd_ratio:.0f}}). The parts library gives several allowable torques for it, and the checking method laid down in the catalogue is:

| Item | This example | Allowable value (parts library) |
|---|---|---|
| Peak torque at starting and stopping | {{ex33_10.T_peak:.1f}} N·m | Allowable peak at starting and stopping {{ex33_10.hd_repeat:.0f}} N·m |
| Average load torque (speed-weighted cubic mean) | {{ex33_10.T_av:.1f}} N·m | Allowable average load {{ex33_10.hd_avg:.0f}} N·m |
| Maximum input speed | {{ex33_10.n_in_max:.0f}} r/min | {{ex33_10.hd_nmax:.0f}} r/min (grease lubrication) |
| Average input speed (one cycle, including dwell) | {{ex33_10.n_in_avg:.0f}} r/min | Allowable average input speed {{ex33_10.hd_navg:.0f}} r/min |
| Shock at emergency stop or collision | — | Instantaneous allowable maximum {{ex33_10.hd_mom:.0f}} N·m |

The first four items are all satisfied. Note that the rated torque {{ex33_10.hd_rated:.0f}} N·m is smaller than the peak: the rated torque is a value fixed by life for continuous running at an input of 2 000 r/min; it is used together with the average load torque to compute the life of the reducer, and it is not an upper limit that must "not be exceeded". Comparing the peak directly with the rated value and concluding that "the reducer is too small" is a common misreading by beginners. The life is computed by the catalogue formula

$$
L_{10} = L_\mathrm n \left( \frac{T_\mathrm r}{T_\mathrm{av}} \right)^3 \frac{n_\mathrm r}{n_\mathrm{av}} \tag{33.10.1}
$$

where $L_\mathrm n$ = {{ex33_10.hd_l10:.0f}} h is the rated life, $T_\mathrm r$ the rated torque, $n_\mathrm r$ = 2 000 r/min, and $n_\mathrm{av}$ the average input speed. The result is $L_{10}$ = {{ex33_10.hd_life:.0f}} h, which just meets the design life of {{ex33_10.life_req:.0f}} h. The average load torque is higher than the rated value, and the life relies on a low average speed (many dwells); if the cycle were any faster, the life of the reducer would fall short first.

The last row is important for the shaft: when the robot stops in an emergency or hits something, the reducer may bear a shock close to the instantaneous allowable maximum. The instantaneous allowable maximum torque is the largest torque this joint can transmit to the output shaft, so it is the static-strength design load of RJ-201: under this torque the shaft must not yield or deform permanently, and it should not become the weakest link of the whole drive train. Note that a harmonic drive is not a slipping clutch; when it is broken the arm falls just the same, and what prevents the fall is the brake on the motor side and the torque limit of the control system. The static strength of RJ-201 is checked for {{ex33_10.hd_mom:.0f}} N·m (33.10.5).

### 33.10.4 The diameter is decided by stiffness

The structure of RJ-201 is shown in Figure 33.10.2: at one end is the flange that connects to the flexspline of the reducer, made in one piece with the shaft; the two bearings sit on seats of the same diameter with a spacer between them; at the other end is an involute spline (module {{ex33_10.spl_m:g}}, {{ex33_10.spl_z}} teeth, pitch diameter Ø{{ex33_10.spl_d:.0f}}), and the arm flange RJ-202 is a separate part that fits on it with an internal spline and takes the torque; at the very end an M{{ex33_10.thread}} × 1.5 round nut (GB/T 812, with a tab washer) clamps the hub of RJ-202, the inner ring of the outer bearing, the spacer and the inner ring of the inner bearing against the end face of the reducer flange. Both bearings go on from the arm end. The bore runs right through, which both saves weight and carries the cables.

This structure has been revised. The first scheme had both flanges in one piece with the shaft and a shoulder between the two bearings — the strength and stiffness worked out, but the bearings could not be fitted from either end. It was found when the assembly drawing was made (Section 3.5.4). In the structural design of a shaft, the assembly order comes first (Section 33.2): which end each part goes on from, what locates it and what clamps it must be settled before the strength is calculated.

**Setting the stiffness target.** When the output shaft twists through an angle $\theta$, the end point is displaced by $\delta \approx \theta R$, where $R$ is the reach. The repeatability of a robot is usually ±0.03–0.1 mm, but that refers to the same posture and the same payload; the elastic displacement caused by a change of payload is another specification. Here we assume that system design allocates the item "twist of the joint output shaft" under peak torque to $\delta$ ≤ {{ex33_10.budget:.2f}} mm, the rest being given to the reducer (the torsional stiffness of the harmonic drive itself is the softest link in the whole joint), the upper arm and the forearm. Then

$$
\theta = \sum_i \frac{T L_i}{G J_i} \le \frac{\delta}{R}, \qquad J_i = \frac{\pi (D_i^4 - d^4)}{32} . \tag{33.10.2}
$$

**Choosing the size.** The diameter of the bearing seat decides the other segments (the reducer flange is 20 mm larger; the outer diameter of the spline must be smaller than the bearing bore so that the bearings can go on from that end), so we need only choose among a few bearing-seat diameters and bores. The twist is computed as "half the reducer flange + the bearing-seat section + half the spline section", with the spline section taken as a cylinder of its minor diameter (not all of the teeth take part in torsion; on the safe side). Program 33.10.1 lists five schemes for the deep-groove ball bearings available in the parts library ([[part:A-BRG-DG/6009]], [[part:A-BRG-DG/6010]]), in 40Cr quenched and tempered steel:

| Bearing seat / bore | Bearing | End displacement δ / mm | δ ≤ {{ex33_10.limit_hand:.4f}} mm? | Mass / kg |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ø45 / Ø25 | 6009 | {{ex33_10.d_4525:.4f}} | {{ex33_10.ok_4525}} | {{ex33_10.m_4525:.2f}} |
| Ø45 / Ø30 | 6009 | {{ex33_10.d_4530:.4f}} | {{ex33_10.ok_4530}} | {{ex33_10.m_4530:.2f}} |
| **Ø50 / Ø30** | **6010** | **{{ex33_10.d_pick:.4f}}** | **{{ex33_10.ok_5030}}** | **{{ex33_10.m_pick:.2f}}** |
| Ø50 / Ø34 | 6010 | {{ex33_10.d_5034:.4f}} | {{ex33_10.ok_5034}} | {{ex33_10.m_5034:.2f}} |
| Ø50 / Ø38 | 6010 | {{ex33_10.d_5038:.4f}} | {{ex33_10.ok_5038}} | {{ex33_10.m_5038:.2f}} |

The hand calculation counts only the torsion of the segments and not the local deformation at the steps and flanges, so we leave a margin of {{ex33_10.margin:.1f}} times in selection: we require {{ex33_10.margin:.1f}}δ ≤ {{ex33_10.budget:.2f}} mm, that is, δ ≤ {{ex33_10.limit_hand:.4f}} mm. Among the schemes that satisfy this, the lightest is Ø50 / Ø30, and we choose it. Ø50 / Ø34 is over the hand-sizing line by only {{ex33_10.d5034_over:.1f}} μm; it is more than a tenth lighter and is worth noting as an alternative: the finite-element calculation of 33.10.5 shows that the local deformation actually adds about {{ex33_10.err_theta:.0f}}%, by which its end displacement is about {{ex33_10.d5034_fe:.3f}} mm, also acceptable, but with less than a tenth of margin. The bearing of the chosen scheme is {{ex33_10.brg}} ([[part:A-BRG-DG/6010]], {{ex33_10.seat:.0f}} × {{ex33_10.brg_D:.0f}} × {{ex33_10.brg_B:.0f}}). Figure 33.10.3 plots the end displacement as a function of the bearing-seat diameter: as the diameter increases, the displacement falls roughly as $1/(D^4 - d^4)$.

Here we see the basic difference between a robot shaft and a reducer shaft: once RJ-201 has been chosen by stiffness, its strength is far more than sufficient (the next subsection finds a static safety factor of several tens). For the shafts, arms and housings of a robot joint, stiffness design is the rule in the great majority of cases; strength matters only at an emergency stop or a collision.

### 33.10.5 Strength and fatigue life

**Normal operation.** The distance from the centre of the outer bearing to the arm connection face (the flange face of RJ-202) is {{ex33_10.arm_offset:.0f}} mm, and the force of the arm produces a bending moment $M$ = {{ex33_10.M_b:.1f}} N·m at the outer bearing. The thinnest section through which the whole torque must pass is the spline section (taken at its minor diameter, Ø{{ex33_10.hub:.2f}} / Ø30); on the safe side we put this bending moment on it as well: the bending stress is {{ex33_10.sig_hub:.1f}} MPa, the torsional shear stress {{ex33_10.tau_hub:.1f}} MPa, and the static safety factor {{ex33_10.S_hub:.0f}}. (At the bearing seat they are {{ex33_10.sig:.1f}} MPa and {{ex33_10.tau:.1f}} MPa, with safety factor {{ex33_10.S_static:.0f}}.)

**Emergency stop.** Under the instantaneous allowable maximum torque of the reducer, {{ex33_10.hd_mom:.0f}} N·m, the shear stress in the spline section is {{ex33_10.tau_stop:.0f}} MPa and the safety factor {{ex33_10.S_stop:.0f}}; the shaft does not yield, which meets the requirement of 33.10.3.

**Spline.** The torque passes to RJ-202 through the spline flanks. A static joint is checked for flank bearing stress: $\sigma_\mathrm p = 2T/(\psi z h l d)$, with load-sharing factor $\psi$ = 0.75, working depth $h$ taken as the module, engaged length $l$ = {{ex33_10.spl_L:.0f}} mm and pitch diameter $d$ (Chapter 25). At peak torque it is {{ex33_10.sigma_p:.1f}} MPa, at an emergency stop {{ex33_10.sigma_p_stop:.1f}} MPa; with an allowable bearing stress of {{ex33_10.sp_allow:.0f}} MPa (static joint, flanks not heat-treated, average conditions of use and manufacture: middle of 60–100 MPa), the safety factor at an emergency stop is {{ex33_10.S_sp:.1f}}.

**Finite-element check.** Program 33.10.1 builds RJ-201 with build123d (1 mm fillets at the steps, through bore) and hands it to the same programs as "Simulation & Analysis" of the digital factory: the end face of the reducer flange is fixed, the peak torque is applied on the outer surface of the spline section (the internal spline of RJ-202 drives it along its whole length; torque only, without the force of the arm), the spline and thread sections are simplified to cylinders, and there are {{ex33_10.n_el}} second-order tetrahedral elements. The results:

- the twist angle is {{ex33_10.theta_fe_urad:.0f}} μrad, {{ex33_10.err_theta:.0f}}% larger than Eq. (33.10.2), and the end displacement is {{ex33_10.d_fe:.3f}} mm, still within {{ex33_10.budget:.2f}} mm. The excess comes from local deformation at the flanges and steps (where a flange meets the shaft body the diameter does not change "in one cut" from one value to another); this is the reason for leaving a margin of {{ex33_10.margin:.1f}} times in selection, and the finite-element result shows that the margin was well chosen;
- away from the fixed face and the loaded face, the largest equivalent stress is {{ex33_10.vm_hot:.1f}} MPa, at the step fillet between the bearing seat and the spline section (z ≈ {{ex33_10.z_hot:.0f}} mm); by the nominal-stress hand calculation, with torque only the spline section has $\sqrt3\tau$ = {{ex33_10.vm_hub_hand:.1f}} MPa, and the ratio of the two is of the order of the stress concentration factor at the fillet.

**Fatigue life.** Taking the torque curve of Figure 33.10.1 as the load spectrum and repeating the same cycle again and again, we compute by the method of Section 33.8 (rainflow counting, Goodman correction, S-N curve with the Haibach correction, Miner accumulation; turned surface). The life comes out of the order of $10^{ {{ex33_10.life_exp}} }$ h, far beyond the design life of {{ex33_10.life_req:.0f}} h: the largest stress amplitude is much lower than the corrected fatigue limit of {{ex33_10.S_D:.0f}} MPa, and the Haibach line merely gives a finite number mathematically, which in engineering means infinite life. Unlike SH-301, fatigue is not a problem for RJ-201.

### 33.10.6 Steel shaft or aluminium shaft

Robot designers always want to reduce the weight of the arm. What happens if RJ-201 is made of 7075-T6 aluminium alloy?

- **Same size.** The shear modulus of the aluminium alloy is only one third of that of steel ({{ex33_10.G_al:.1f}} GPa against {{ex33_10.G_st:.0f}} GPa), and the end displacement becomes {{ex33_10.d_al:.3f}} mm, which exceeds {{ex33_10.budget:.2f}} mm. The mass falls from {{ex33_10.m_st:.2f}} kg to {{ex33_10.m_al:.2f}} kg, but the stiffness is not acceptable.
- **Same stiffness.** Enlarge the diameter of every segment to {{ex33_10.k_al:.3f}} times (bearing seat about Ø{{ex33_10.seat_al:.0f}}); the mass is {{ex33_10.m_al_same:.2f}} kg, lighter than the steel shaft. But the bearing must be changed to about 6013 (the 60 series of the parts library goes only up to 6010, so another source must be found), and the connection dimensions of the reducer and the joint housing must all grow with it.
- **A thin-walled steel tube of the same outer diameter.** The outside is the same as in the previous case, the bore is enlarged to Ø{{ex33_10.bore_big:.0f}}, the torsional stiffness is the same as the original scheme, and the mass is {{ex33_10.m_st_big:.2f}} kg.

Why is the third scheme heavier than the aluminium shaft? Look at the shaft in two parts. The "tube body" between the two bearings decides the torsional stiffness: {{ex33_10.m_al_body:.2f}} kg in aluminium alloy, {{ex33_10.m_big_body:.2f}} kg in the thin-walled steel tube, the steel tube being, if anything, slightly lighter. The reason is that the "efficiency" of torsional stiffness per unit mass depends on $G/\rho$: {{ex33_10.Gr_st:.1f}} for steel and {{ex33_10.Gr_al:.1f}} for aluminium alloy (in GPa/(g/cm³)), almost the same. **On the tube body, the weight saving comes from a larger diameter and a thinner wall, not from aluminium.** The difference is at the two ends: the reducer flange has to carry screws and fit against the flexspline, and the spline section has teeth to be cut, so their sizes are decided by the connection, not by stiffness, and the lighter this material the better: {{ex33_10.m_al_fl:.2f}} kg in aluminium alloy, {{ex33_10.m_big_fl:.2f}} kg in steel.

Now consider manufacture: the wall of the thin-walled steel tube is only about {{ex33_10.wall_big:.1f}} mm at the bearing seat and {{ex33_10.wall_hub_big:.1f}} mm at the spline section (minor diameter), where the spline could not even be cut; it is prone to chatter in turning and the fitting surface for the bearing deforms easily; for the same stiffness the aluminium part has a much thicker wall and can also be machined in one piece with the joint housing and the upper arm. This is why the upper arm, housing and flanges of collaborative robots are mostly made of aluminium alloy, while the output shaft, which carries the bearings directly and bears concentrated loads, is mostly made of steel in a case like this one where the size is limited by the bearings and the reducer.

This conclusion is not learned by heart; it comes from comparing stiffness, mass and manufacture.

### 33.10.7 Engineering practice

Task sheet TS-33-2 asks the reader to complete the design of RJ-201: in "Multibody Dynamics" of the digital factory, change the handling cycle (or change the robot) and export the torque curve of J2; look up the harmonic drive and the bearings in the parts library and check the selection; fix the size by stiffness and model it with build123d or the web design desk (adding fillets at the steps); in "Simulation & Analysis" carry out the torsional stiffness, strength and fatigue-life calculations (choose "Joint-torque record" as the load spectrum); and finally submit the design for review.

**Engineer's notes**

- Settle the assembly order before the strength: which end each part goes on from, what locates it and what clamps it. A shaft with large flanges at both ends and a shoulder in between cannot take its bearings (the first scheme of this section made exactly this mistake).
- The stiffness chain of a robot joint is a series chain: motor shaft, reducer, output shaft, bearings, arm. The total compliance is the sum of the compliances of the links, and the softest link (usually the harmonic drive) decides the whole. Making the output shaft excessively stiff helps the end accuracy very little and only adds weight.
- A crossed-roller bearing alone can carry radial, axial and overturning-moment loads, and the joint can be made very short, so most collaborative-robot joints use it; in this example, in order to use the data already in the parts library, a pair of deep-groove ball bearings was used, and the axial length is therefore somewhat longer.
- The bore of a hollow shaft must leave room for the wiring: the cables are twisted as the joint turns, and a bore that is too small wears them out. The bore diameter is often fixed first by the wiring and the outer diameter afterwards.
- The torque curve depends on motion planning. For the same robot, the faster the handling cycle and the farther the payload, the larger the peak. In design one takes a "design condition", and the maximum payload and maximum acceleration stated in the product specification are the limits on these conditions.
