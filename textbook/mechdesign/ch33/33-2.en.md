### 33.2.1 The problem

Open SH-301 on the "Design & Process" page of the digital factory and you see a stepped shaft of five segments: Ø30–Ø35–Ø40–Ø35–Ø30, with a 12 × 5 keyway on the Ø40 segment. This is version A, the teaching model of the FreeCAD introductory lab. As the geometry of "a shaft" there is nothing wrong with it; but if bearings, gear, sleeve and coupling are really to be put on it, we find that they will not go on or will not stay in place. For example, the 6207 bearing is {{ex33_2.B_brg}} mm wide, whereas the left-hand Ø35 segment of version A is only {{ex33_2.A_seat_short:.0f}} mm long.

Shaft design starts here: not by calculating strength, but by "putting on" every part of the shaft in turn and seeing what each needs from it, namely a mating surface, a shoulder it can rely on, and a path along which it can slide into place. This step is called the **shaft layout design**. It determines all the dimensions of the shaft, and the strength calculation then only checks the diameters on this basis and modifies them.

### 33.2.2 The parts on the shaft and their requirements

The output shaft assembly SA-300 of the WQR-105 consists of the output shaft SH-301, the large gear GR-302, two 6207 deep-groove ball bearings (parts library [[part:A-BRG-DG/6207]]), two parallel keys at the gear and the coupling ([[part:A-KEY-FLAT]]), and also the bearing covers, the oil seal ([[part:A-SEL-LIP]]) and the sleeve. The layout of the shaft must meet four requirements:

1. **It can be assembled and taken apart.** Every part must be able to slide from one end of the shaft to its position, and every segment it passes on the way must have a diameter smaller than its bore.
2. **Axial location and fixing.** Every part must have a definite axial position and stay there under axial force. The usual means of location are shoulders, collars, sleeves, round nuts, end washers and circlips.
3. **Circumferential fixing.** A part that transmits torque must not slip on the shaft; it is fixed by a key, spline, interference fit or pin.
4. **Good manufacturability.** The shaft should be easy to turn, grind, key-mill and inspect; the steps should be few and justified, and the fillets, chamfers and undercuts should follow the standards.

### 33.2.3 The assembly order decides the diameters

Think of the assembly in reverse (Animation 33.2.1). The bore of the gear is Ø40, so it can only be slid on from an end whose diameter is not more than 40, and pushed until it stops against a step larger than 40, the collar. The collar must therefore be on the other side of the gear, and on this side of the gear a sleeve presses it in place. Outside the sleeve is the left bearing, which is fitted last. The right bearing is fitted from the other end, passing over the overhanging part and the seal segment, and rests against the shoulder on the right; the coupling is fitted last of all.

Laid out this way, the diameters of the shaft are bound to be "thick in the middle, thin at both ends": from the collar they decrease step by step towards both ends. Every step has its purpose: some are for location (shoulders, collars), some only to let a part slide over easily. Two segments of the same diameter but different tolerance classes (for example the bearing seat k6 and the seal segment f9, both Ø35) need no step; the tolerances are simply marked segment by segment on the drawing. A step without a purpose should not be made, because it only adds stress concentration and machining cost.

### 33.2.5 Rules for shoulders, collars and fillets

**Height of a locating shoulder.** A shoulder or collar must carry the axial force from the part without being crushed, and must give the end face of the part enough contact area; as a rule of thumb the height is $h \ge (0.07 \sim 0.1)d$. The height of the collar of version B is {{ex33_2.h_collar:.1f}} mm, which is greater than $0.07 \times 40$ = {{ex33_2.h_min:.1f}} mm.

**Shoulder at a bearing.** There are two opposite requirements: the shoulder must be high enough to locate reliably, but must not stand much higher than the inner ring of the bearing, otherwise the claws of the puller cannot grip the ring when the bearing is removed. For this the bearing catalogue gives upper and lower limits of the "mounting dimension" $d_\mathrm a$; for the 6207, $d_{\mathrm a,\min}$ = {{ex33_2.da_min}} mm, and the shoulder of version B is taken as Ø42.

**Fillets.** This is the detail of shaft design that is easiest to get wrong and has the most serious consequences. If the fillet is too small the stress concentration is large and the fatigue crack starts there (Section 33.5); if it is too large it runs into the chamfer of the part, which then cannot seat against the shoulder. Therefore

$$
r < r_\mathrm s \quad \text{or} \quad r < C , \tag{33.2.1}
$$

must be satisfied, where $r_\mathrm s$ is the chamfer of the bearing inner ring and $C$ is the chamfer at the mouth of the bore of the gear or sleeve. For the 6207, $r_{\mathrm s,\min}$ = {{ex33_2.r_smin}} mm, and the fillet at the right bearing of version B is taken as 1.0 mm (Figure 33.2.3(a)). Where a larger fillet is needed and the part must still seat firmly, a spacer ring with a large chamfer can be put between the shoulder and the part, or the fillet can be made an undercut fillet recessed into the end face of the shoulder.

**Segment length and hub.** The gear is clamped by the sleeve, and the sleeve must press on the hub of the gear and not butt against a step of the shaft, so the segment carrying the gear must be 2–3 mm shorter than the hub (Figure 33.2.3(b)). It is the same with the coupling: the overhanging segment is 2 mm shorter than the coupling hub, so that the end washer can press the hub against the shoulder. Likewise, the length of a key must be 5–10 mm less than the hub and be a standard length: the key at the gear is {{ex33_2.key_gear}} (type A, round ends, centred on the hub). The coupling hub is only 2 mm longer than the overhanging segment; a closed keyseat must stay a few millimetres away from both the shaft end and the shoulder, which leaves no room for a long enough type A key. So the keyseat on the extension runs out at the shaft end (an open keyseat) and takes a type C (single round end) key, {{ex33_2.key_ext}} (GB/T 1096), whose working length is $L - b/2$, longer than that of a type A key of the same length.

### 33.2.6 Fits and tolerances

The tolerance class of each segment of the shaft is decided by the requirements of the fit (Chapter 4):

- Rolling-bearing inner ring on the shaft: the inner ring turns with the shaft and the direction of the load is fixed, so the inner ring carries a rotating load and needs a transition fit or a light interference fit, taken as k6: Ø35 k6 = {{ex33_2.k6_35}} mm. This agrees with the limits of "bearing seat Ø35 k6" in the digital factory's inspection template.
- Gear on the shaft: the torque is transmitted mainly by the key, and the fit ensures centring; version B uses the interference fit H7/r6 (Ø40 r6 = {{ex33_2.r6_40}} mm, bore Ø40 H7 = {{ex33_2.H7_40}} mm), so that the interference and the key transmit the torque together, which is less likely to work loose under shock than the k6 of version A. The change must be written into an engineering change order, and the inspection template changed accordingly.
- Seal segment: it passes only through the bearing cover and oil seal, and is taken as f9 ({{ex33_2.f9_35}} mm), a little smaller than the bearing seat, so that the right bearing can slide over it without trouble.
- Keyway width: N9 for the shaft keyway and JS9 for the hub keyway (normal connection).

**Example 33.2.1** Check version B against the layout rules one by one.

Program 33.2.1 writes the rules of Section 33.2.5 as a checklist of {{ex33_2.n_checks}} items (Lab 33.2 uses seven of them), and version B passes all of them ({{ex33_2.n_ok_B}} items). Two of the items concern torque. By the usual method the contact height of the key is taken as $k \approx h/2$, the working length as $l = L - b$, and the bearing stress is $\sigma_\mathrm p = 2T/(dkl)$ with an allowable value of {{ex33_2.sigma_p:.0f}} MPa: the gear key {{ex33_2.key_gear}} can transmit {{ex33_2.T_key_gear:.0f}} N·m and the coupling key {{ex33_2.key_ext}} can transmit {{ex33_2.T_key_ext:.0f}} N·m, both not less than the rated torque of 350 N·m.

One point has to be made here. $k \approx h/2$ is an approximation: the real contact height on the hub side is the part of the key that stands out of the shaft surface, $h - t$, which is {{ex33_2.k_gear_exact:.1f}} mm for the gear key and {{ex33_2.k_ext_exact:.1f}} mm for the coupling key. Calculated with these values, the gear key can transmit only {{ex33_2.T_key_gear_exact:.0f}} N·m, not enough for 350 N·m; the type C key at the coupling can transmit {{ex33_2.T_key_ext_exact:.0f}} N·m, just enough, with little margin. The margin at the gear was small to begin with (the digital factory also sets the rated torque at this figure); version B uses the H7/r6 interference fit at the gear, so that interference and key transmit the torque together; if the coupling has to carry more torque, the problem is left to Chapter 25 (keys, splines and interference connections) and to the calculation report of the task sheet, where a double key, a spline or a shrink disc may be chosen. Wherever in a design an "approximate formula just suffices", check again by a different method.

**Example 33.2.2** Design review of version A.

Checking version A and its accompanying BOM against the same checklist reveals four problems (① to ④ in Figure 33.2.2):

1. The left Ø35 segment is {{ex33_2.A_seat_short:.0f}} mm long, less than the width of the 6207 bearing, {{ex33_2.B_brg}} mm, so the bearing cannot be fitted;
2. The forging blank of the gear GR-302 is a Ø225 × {{ex33_2.blank_w}} disc, the hub is only {{ex33_2.blank_w}} mm wide, and the key is 45 mm long; a key cannot be as long as the hub. If the key is taken at the standard length of 40, "5 mm shorter than the hub", its capacity falls to {{ex33_2.T_key_gear_hub45:.0f}} N·m, not enough for 350 N·m. Version B changes the gear to a forging with a hub, hub width 55;
3. The key of the overhanging segment in the BOM is 8 × 7 × 36, which by the same allowable bearing stress can transmit only {{ex33_2.T_key_ext_A:.0f}} N·m, again not enough;
4. The steps have sharp corners and no fillets. In Lab 8 of the "Simulation & Analysis" page of the digital factory it was already seen that the finite-element stress at a sharp corner keeps rising as the mesh is refined, so no definite value can be obtained (Chapter 13).

Version A is suitable as an introductory exercise in geometric modelling, but as a product drawing it has to be revised. This is a common situation in engineering: a prototype drawing that can be "drawn" is not the same as one that can be made, assembled and used, and the purpose of design review is to find such problems before production.

### 33.2.7 Manufacturability

The layout must also take machining and inspection into account:

1. On segments to be ground (bearing seats, gear seat), leave a wheel runout groove at the step, or make a fillet transition and write "ground to the fillet" on the drawing; on segments to be threaded leave a thread relief groove.
2. On one shaft keep the fillet radii as nearly equal as possible, and likewise the chamfers, to cut down tool changes.
3. If several keyways are cut along the same line of the surface, they can be milled in one setting (the keyway program `wq_cam_keyway.py` for SH-301 in the digital factory does it in one setting).
4. Centre holes at both ends serve as the datum for turning, grinding and inspection and ensure that the segments are coaxial.
5. Chamfer the ends of the shaft, which makes assembly easier and prevents knocks and cuts to the hands.

### 33.2.8 Engineering practice

Step two of task sheet TS-33-1: in FreeCAD or build123d (Chapter 3), change SH-301 to the layout of version B according to the table, put fillets on all steps, and cut the two keyways along the same line (the digital factory's online design bench can change diameters, lengths, keyways and step fillets; modelling in desktop CAD or with a build123d script is also possible, and the STEP file is exported and uploaded in "Design Release & Approval", with the steps given in Section 33.9). Write a layout statement that explains, segment by segment, why each has its diameter and length, and attach the four problems found in the review of version A and how they were corrected. This statement is the first part of the calculation report.

**Everyday example** On the rear axle of a bicycle are fitted in turn the lock nut, washer, cone, steel balls and hub; each part is screwed or slid on from one end or the other, and the thread on the axle extends only as far as is needed. Once you have taken a rear wheel apart, you understand the saying "the assembly order decides the layout of the parts".

**Engineer's notes**

- Settle the assembly order first, then the diameters; settle the diameters first, then the lengths; the lengths are finally "meshed" out of the bearing spacing, the hub widths and the housing dimensions.
- If a step cannot be given a purpose, remove it.
- Do not be content with "unspecified fillets R1" on a drawing. The fillets at bearings and gears should be dimensioned individually and checked against the chamfers of the parts.
- Take a checklist along to the design review (the rules of this section are one); it is far more reliable than "having a look" from experience.
