### 33.4.1 The problem

The structure is fixed, and the span and the positions of the parts are known, so the forces can now be calculated accurately. Gear GR-302 receives the thrust of the pinion at the mesh point. This force lies neither on the axis nor in a "convenient" plane, and it bends and twists the shaft at the same time. Which section is the most dangerous? How large is the stress there? Will the shaft yield under the starting shock? This section answers these three questions.

### 33.4.2 Forces of the gear on the shaft

The mesh force of a spur gear acts along the line of action, at the pressure angle $\alpha$ = 20° to the tangent of the pitch circle. Resolve it into the **tangential force** $F_\mathrm t$ along the tangent and the **radial force** $F_\mathrm r$ directed towards the center of the wheel (discussed in detail in Chapter 29):

$$
F_\mathrm t = \frac{2T}{d}, \qquad F_\mathrm r = F_\mathrm t \tan\alpha , \tag{33.4.1}
$$

where $d$ is the reference-circle diameter. GR-302 has module 3 and 70 teeth, so $d$ = {{ex33_4.d_gear:.0f}} mm; $T$ = {{ex33_4.T:.0f}} N·m. Then $F_\mathrm t$ = {{ex33_4.Ft:.0f}} N and $F_\mathrm r$ = {{ex33_4.Fr:.0f}} N.

The two forces are perpendicular to each other and bend the shaft in two planes: the radial force lies in the vertical plane containing the centers of the two wheels (the convention of this chapter), and the tangential force lies in the horizontal plane perpendicular to it. The torque is transmitted from the gear through the key to the shaft, and on to the coupling.

### 33.4.3 Support reactions and bending moments in the two planes

Treat the shaft as a simply supported beam resting on the two bearing centers (a deep-groove ball bearing allows a small angular misalignment and can be taken as a pin support), and solve in each plane separately. Taking moments about the left bearing A, the reaction at the right bearing B is $R_B = F\,a/L$ and $R_A = F - R_B$, where $a$ = {{ex33_2.a_gear:.0f}} mm is the distance from the gear to A and $L$ = {{ex33_2.span:.0f}} mm is the span.

**Example 33.4.1** Find the support reactions and the bending moment diagrams of SH-301.

Horizontal plane: $R_{AH}$ = {{ex33_4.RAx:.0f}} N, $R_{BH}$ = {{ex33_4.RBx:.0f}} N, and the bending moment at the gear is $M_H = R_{AH}\,a$ = {{ex33_4.M_gear_x:.1f}} N·m.

Vertical plane: $R_{AV}$ = {{ex33_4.RAy:.0f}} N, $R_{BV}$ = {{ex33_4.RBy:.0f}} N, and $M_V$ = {{ex33_4.M_gear_y:.1f}} N·m.

At the same section, the bending moments in the two planes are mutually perpendicular vectors and are combined as vectors:

$$
M = \sqrt{M_H^2 + M_V^2} . \tag{33.4.2}
$$

The resultant bending moment at the gear is $M$ = {{ex33_4.M_gear:.1f}} N·m (Figure 33.4.1). The resultant support reactions at the two bearings are $R_A$ = {{ex33_4.RA:.0f}} N and $R_B$ = {{ex33_4.RB:.0f}} N, which are exactly the radial loads needed for the bearing life calculation in Chapter 34.

Note that the resultant bending moment diagram cannot be obtained by "lining up and adding" the diagrams of the two planes; they must be combined section by section with Eq. (33.4.2). In this example the loads in both planes act at the same point, the two moment diagrams have the same shape, and the resultant is still a triangle. In general (for example with two gears on an intermediate shaft) the resultant diagram has a shape different from either plane's.

### 33.4.4 Critical sections

The critical section is not necessarily where the bending moment is largest. Three things must be considered together: the magnitudes of the bending moment and the torque, the diameter of the section, and the stress concentration. From Figure 33.4.1, four sections of SH-301 are selected for checking:

- Section I: the middle of the gear, where the bending moment is largest, with a keyway and an interference fit;
- Section II: the shoulder on the left of the collar (Ø40 → Ø48), where the bending moment is still large, with a fillet;
- Section III: the shoulder at the right bearing (Ø42 → Ø35), where the diameter decreases and all of the torque passes;
- Section IV: the root of the extension (Ø35 → Ø30), where the diameter is smallest, with torque only, a shoulder and a keyway.

### 33.4.5 Nominal stress and static strength

The maximum bending normal stress and torsional shear stress on a section are

$$
\sigma = \frac{M}{W}, \quad W = \frac{\pi d^3}{32}; \qquad \tau = \frac{T}{W_\mathrm T}, \quad W_\mathrm T = \frac{\pi d^3}{16} . \tag{33.4.3}
$$

Both occur at the same point on the surface at the same time. According to the distortion-energy theory (Chapter 10), they are combined into the von Mises stress

$$
\sigma' = \sqrt{\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2} . \tag{33.4.4}
$$

**Example 33.4.2** Static strength check under the starting shock.

In the torque record of the run-in test bench (Section 33.8), the maximum torque at starting is {{ex33_4.T_peak:.0f}} N·m, which is {{ex33_4.K_peak:.2f}} times the rated value. The gear forces are proportional to the torque, so the bending moments are enlarged by the same factor. The nominal stresses of each section at rated torque and the static safety factor under the starting shock, $S = \sigma_\mathrm s/\sigma'$, are as follows ($\sigma_\mathrm s$ = {{ex33_4.sigma_s:.0f}} MPa):

| Section | M / N·m | T / N·m | σ / MPa | τ / MPa | σ′ under shock / MPa | Static S |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | {{ex33_4.M_I:.1f}} | {{ex33_4.T_I:.0f}} | {{ex33_4.sigma_I:.1f}} | {{ex33_4.tau_I:.1f}} | {{ex33_4.s_vm_I:.0f}} | {{ex33_4.S_static_I:.2f}} |
| II | {{ex33_4.M_II:.1f}} | {{ex33_4.T_II:.0f}} | {{ex33_4.sigma_II:.1f}} | {{ex33_4.tau_II:.1f}} | {{ex33_4.s_vm_II:.0f}} | {{ex33_4.S_static_II:.2f}} |
| III | {{ex33_4.M_III:.1f}} | {{ex33_4.T_III:.0f}} | {{ex33_4.sigma_III:.1f}} | {{ex33_4.tau_III:.1f}} | {{ex33_4.s_vm_III:.0f}} | {{ex33_4.S_static_III:.2f}} |
| IV | {{ex33_4.M_IV:.1f}} | {{ex33_4.T_IV:.0f}} | {{ex33_4.sigma_IV:.1f}} | {{ex33_4.tau_IV:.1f}} | {{ex33_4.s_vm_IV:.0f}} | {{ex33_4.S_static_IV:.2f}} |

The smallest static safety factor is at Section {{ex33_4.S_static_min_at}} and equals {{ex33_4.S_static_min:.2f}}, which is greater than the usual requirement of 1.5, so the starting shock will not make the shaft yield.

Two points deserve attention. First, in all four sections the shear stress due to torque is larger than the bending normal stress, and Section IV has shear stress only: for the output shaft of a low-speed, heavily loaded reducer, torque is the main actor. Second, static strength does not need to include stress concentration: after local yielding the stress in a ductile material is redistributed, and a notch weakens the static strength very little (Chapter 10). For fatigue strength, however, stress concentration is decisive, as Section 33.5 shows.

### 33.4.6 The "equivalent bending moment" method of domestic textbooks

Domestic machine design textbooks commonly use a simplified method. According to the third strength theory, the torque is converted into a bending moment:

$$
M_\mathrm{ca} = \sqrt{M^2 + (\alpha T)^2}, \qquad \sigma_\mathrm{ca} = \frac{M_\mathrm{ca}}{W} \approx \frac{M_\mathrm{ca}}{0.1\,d^3} \le [\sigma_{-1}]_\mathrm b . \tag{33.4.5}
$$

$\alpha$ is a reduction coefficient that allows for the different stress-cycle characteristics of bending and torsional stress (the usual notation of domestic textbooks, using the same letter as the pressure angle and the stress concentration factor but with a different meaning): the bending stress is fully reversed, and for a torque treated as a repeated cycle $\alpha \approx 0.6$. The allowable bending stress $[\sigma_{-1}]_\mathrm b$ is an empirical value for a fully reversed cycle, with the effects of stress concentration and so on already folded in; for quenched and tempered 45 steel it is about {{ex33_4.sigma_1b_allow:.0f}} MPa.

**Example 33.4.3** Check the four sections by the equivalent bending moment method.

Section I: $M_\mathrm{ca}$ = {{ex33_4.Mca_I:.0f}} N·m and $\sigma_\mathrm{ca}$ = {{ex33_4.sigma_ca_I:.1f}} MPa. Section III: {{ex33_4.sigma_ca_III:.1f}} MPa. Section IV: $M_\mathrm{ca} = \alpha T$ = {{ex33_4.Mca_IV:.0f}} N·m and $\sigma_\mathrm{ca}$ = {{ex33_4.sigma_ca_IV:.1f}} MPa, which exceeds {{ex33_4.sigma_1b_allow:.0f}} MPa.

Here, following the habit of domestic textbooks, $W \approx 0.1d^3$ is used. For a section with a keyway, the keyway should strictly be deducted, using $W = \pi d^3/32 - bt(d-t)^2/(2d)$ as in the program of Section 33.2, and the resulting stress is still somewhat larger. Section 33.5 follows the convention of [SHI] and uses the nominal stress of the solid section, putting the whole effect of the keyway into the stress concentration factor. The two practices must not be mixed.

The simplified method also points to Section IV. Its advantage is speed, which suits comparing several schemes in the early stage of design. Its drawback is that stress concentration, size, surface and other factors are all hidden in one empirical allowable value, so one cannot see "why it is not enough and what should be changed". The fatigue strength check in the next section lays these factors out one by one.

### 33.4.7 Engineering practice

Step 3 of task sheet TS-33-1: in the calculation sheet, write the force analysis (free-body sketch, support reactions in the two planes, bending moment diagram, torque diagram) and the static strength check of the four sections. The calculation sheet template is generated by the program, with the formulas and sources already filled in, and the student fills in the substitutions and results. Record the support reactions $R_A$ and $R_B$; they are needed again when choosing the bearings in Chapter 34.

**Everyday example.** When you tighten a very tight bolt with a wrench, the force of your hand on the wrench both turns the bolt (torque) and bends it (bending moment), and the longer the bolt sticks out, the more it bends. If you push with both hands symmetrically on the two sides of the wrench, the bending moments cancel. This is one reason why a car wheel wrench is made in the shape of a cross.

**Engineer's notes**

- Draw the directions of the gear forces clearly. The tangential forces on the driving gear and the driven gear are opposite in direction, and the radial forces both point to the centers of their own wheels. If one direction is drawn wrongly, the support reactions and the bearing loads are all wrong.
- A helical gear also has an axial force, which loads the shaft in tension or compression and produces an additional bending moment on the shaft ($F_\mathrm a d/2$); the choice and the locating of the bearings change accordingly.
- A coupling does not carry torque only: when the two shafts are misaligned, a flexible coupling exerts an additional radial force on the shaft (in practice taken as 0.2–0.5 times the tangential force). The worked examples of this chapter neglect it; Exercise 33.4.4 includes it so that you can see the effect.
