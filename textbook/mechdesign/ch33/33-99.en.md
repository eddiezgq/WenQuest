The shaft strings gears, bearings and couplings together, bearing both bending moment and torque, and almost every shaft failure is a fatigue failure. The order for designing a shaft is: choose the material, lay out the structure, estimate the diameter, compute forces and bending moments, check fatigue strength, then check stiffness and critical speed, and finally verify with finite elements and a measured load record.

The choice of material depends on what determines the size. Quenched-and-tempered 45 steel is the first choice for ordinary rotating shafts; when strength is insufficient one changes to a quenched-and-tempered alloy steel such as 40Cr, which raises the fatigue limit by about 30%, but the elastic modulus stays the same, so stiffness does not improve, and alloy steels are more sensitive to notches and surface condition.

The structural design fixes all the dimensions of the shaft. Fit the parts onto the shaft one by one in assembly order, and the diameters naturally arrange themselves "thick in the middle, thin at both ends"; every shoulder has a purpose: locating, letting a part slide past, or separating different tolerance zones. The shoulder height is not less than 0.07d, the fillet at a bearing must be smaller than the bearing chamfer, the shaft segment carrying a part is 2–3 mm shorter than the hub, and the key is shorter than the hub. Checking the current revision A of SH-301 in the digital factory against these rules turned up four problems, and this chapter gave revision B: the gear is changed to a forging with a hub, the gear seat uses an H7/r6 interference fit, the coupling is fixed with a shaft-end plate, and every shoulder gets a fillet.

The torsional strength estimate uses only power and speed, and with a very low allowable shear stress it "packages" the bending moment and the stress concentration; it suits laying out the structure but not checking. The accurate calculation starts from the forces: the gear force is resolved into tangential and radial components, the reactions and bending moments are found in the two planes separately, and at each section they are combined vectorially. Static strength uses the nominal stress and the von Mises combination, without stress concentration.

The fatigue check is the core of shaft design. The bending stress is fully reversed, and the torque is taken as pulsating or static according to the operating condition. The fatigue limit of the part is corrected for four factors: stress concentration, size, surface and strengthening; the theoretical stress concentration factor can be read from charts or computed by finite elements for one's own geometry; multiplied by the notch sensitivity factor it gives the effective stress concentration factor, which is then combined with the size factor and the surface factor into the overall influence factor. The safety-factor method and the modified Goodman method rest on the same principle, the latter being more conservative. Among the four sections of SH-301 revision B, the keyway on the overhanging end (Section IV) is the weakest: smallest diameter, all the torque passing through it, and a keyway as well, with a safety factor of less than 1.5 for 45 steel. An infinite-life calculation for the starting peak is too conservative, and a finite-life calculation from the load record is then needed.

Stiffness and critical speed are usually ample for the low-speed shafts of reducers, but for slender shafts and high-speed shafts they decide the size. A rigid shaft runs at no more than 0.7 times the first critical speed; a flexible shaft runs above 1.3 times and must pass quickly through the critical speed.

The finite element constraints must remove only rigid-body displacement and not clamp the section; a model built this way gives reactions that differ from the hand calculation by about 1%, stresses in smooth segments that differ from beam theory by less than one part in a thousand, and local stress peaks at the shoulders; after rainflow counting, the load record gives the life through the Goodman correction and Miner accumulation. The several methods agree in pointing to a problem at Section IV; under frequent starts and stops, 45 steel does not meet 10 000 h, and quenched-and-tempered 40Cr does. The design decision is that revision B uses 40Cr, and it enters production in the digital factory through an engineering change.

The design of one shaft is a complete chain in the digital factory: the parts library supplies standard parts, CAD models and draws, CAE computes strength, stiffness, natural frequencies and fatigue life, review and release follow, and the shop manufactures and inspects. The result of CAE depends first of all on the boundary conditions: for a strength calculation, constraining the whole ring of the bearing seat radially does little harm, but for modal analysis it amounts to clamping the shaft, and the natural frequency can come out much too high (about twice for the upper shaft of the lockstitch machine); only constraining the middle ring of the bearing seat (pinned) agrees with beam theory.

The two cases show that the size of a shaft is not necessarily decided by strength. The load on the hollow output shaft of the collaborative-robot shoulder joint comes from motion planning: the arm swings back and forth, but because the shaft has to hold the arm up the torque always has the same sign, fluctuating about a fairly large mean; the end-point offset equals the twist angle times the working radius, and once the diameter is fixed by torsional stiffness the strength has a margin of dozens of times and the shaft will not yield even in an emergency stop. In the tube that determines the torsional stiffness, weight saving comes from a large diameter and a thin wall, since steel and aluminum have almost the same $G/\rho$; the benefit of aluminum alloy lies in the flange, whose size is set by the connection, and in manufacturing. The upper shaft of the lockstitch machine runs at high speed under light load, and the inertial force is proportional to the square of the speed; forces that turn with the shaft produce mean stress, forces of fixed direction produce alternating stress, so one must follow a point on the shaft for one revolution, and the balance weight just cancels the "static" half of the first-order inertial force, leaving mainly alternating stress. The most dangerous place is the crank-seat shoulder, whose safety factor is still above 1.5 after the speed-up; the real risk of the speed-up is that the second-order excitation comes close to the natural frequency of the machine head.

### Historical notes

The fatigue problem of shafts appeared together with the railways. In the 1840s the axles of railway vehicles broke one after another in service, with flat fracture surfaces and no plastic deformation, and this was mistakenly attributed to the iron "crystallizing". In 1843 W. J. M. Rankine analyzed broken axles and pointed out that the cracks started at the sharp corners of the shoulders, advocating large fillets. The German railway engineer A. Wöhler, from the 1850s, tested axle materials systematically on rotating-bending and other testing machines, and published his results around 1870: he tabulated stress against cycles to fracture and proposed that there is a stress limit below which the specimen does not break, the fatigue limit; later L. Spangenberg plotted his data as a curve, which is today's S-N curve, also called the Wöhler curve. For the effect of mean stress, Gerber in 1874, J. Goodman in 1899 and C. R. Soderberg in 1930 successively gave different correction lines. A. Föppl in 1895 and H. H. Jeffcott in 1919 gave the analysis of the single-disc rotor, explaining the "passing through the critical speed" of steam-turbine rotors. In the mid-20th century H. Neuber and R. E. Peterson established the theory of notch stress and notch sensitivity, and Peterson compiled and published the stress concentration charts. From the 1980s finite element and fatigue analysis software let engineers compute the actual geometry directly. The German standard DIN 743, first published in 2000 and revised in 2012, standardized the load-capacity calculation of shafts and became the method in common use in European industry.
