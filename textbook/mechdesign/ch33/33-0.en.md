title: Chapter Overview
---

A gear hands its torque to a shaft, and the shaft hands it on to a coupling, a sprocket or the next gear; bearings hold the shaft in the housing and let it do nothing but turn. In a speed reducer the shaft is the "backbone" that strings all the other parts together. Its design looks simple, since it is no more than a few cylinders, but the diameter and length of every segment are fixed by other parts, every step has its purpose, and when a shaft breaks the whole machine stops. Almost all shaft failures in practice are fatigue failures: with every revolution the bending stress at the surface goes from tension to compression and back, and the crack often starts at the bottom of a keyway or at a shoulder fillet and grows through some millions of cycles before the shaft suddenly breaks.

The chapter follows the output shaft SH-301 of the digital-factory reducer WQR-105, in the order in which an engineer actually designs a shaft. First the material is chosen (Section 33.1). Then the diameter and length of each segment are fixed from the assembly and location of the parts on the shaft, which is the layout design (Section 33.2). The minimum diameter is then estimated from the torque (Section 33.3), and the gear forces, support reactions and bending-moment diagrams are found for a strength calculation under combined bending and torsion (Section 33.4). Stress concentration and the effects of size and surface are then taken into account in the fatigue strength check, which is the core of shaft design (Section 33.5). Stiffness (Section 33.6) and critical speed (Section 33.7) follow, and last the whole shaft is checked by finite elements and the fatigue life is calculated from the real torque recorded on the run-in test bench (Section 33.8). Section 33.9 maps these steps onto the pages of the digital factory: choosing standard parts from the parts library, modelling and drawing in CAD, calculating strength, stiffness, natural frequency and fatigue life in CAE software, review and release, and then manufacture and inspection in the workshop. Sections 33.10 and 33.11 are two typical case studies: the hollow output shaft of a collaborative-robot shoulder joint (slow, turning back and forth, with dimensions set by torsional stiffness) and the upper shaft of an industrial lockstitch sewing machine (fast, with inertia forces, infinite life and critical speed). Together with SH-301 they make three quite different kinds of "shaft". The results of hand calculation, standard look-up and finite elements are set side by side to show what each rests on and where each is limited.

The chapter has three engineering task sheets, from which the teacher may choose one or let groups do one each. TS-33-1 asks the reader to carry SH-301 through a complete revision from version A to version B: version A, currently in the digital factory, is a simplified teaching model, and the review will find several places where it breaks the layout rules. The reader redesigns, calculates, models, runs the finite-element and fatigue analyses and produces the drawings by the methods of this chapter, submits them for design review, and after approval the simulation workshop machines the keyways and carries out the coordinate-measuring inspection. TS-33-2 designs the hollow shaft RJ-201 of a collaborative-robot joint. TS-33-3 checks the sewing-machine upper shaft LS-101 and evaluates a proposal to raise its speed.

The chapter uses the limits and fits of Chapter 4, the materials of Chapter 5, the stress analysis and fatigue theory of Chapters 8–11, and the finite element method of Chapter 13. Keys, bearings and couplings on the shaft are discussed in detail in Chapters 25, 34 and 37; the reducer course design of Chapter 50 brings them all together. Chapter 32 (reducers) and Chapter 45 (joint modules) of the *Robotics* textbook also use the methods of this chapter.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. distinguish axles, transmission shafts and rotating shafts, and choose the material and heat treatment of a shaft according to strength, stiffness, manufacture and cost;
2. determine the layout of a shaft from the assembly order and location of the parts on it, state the purpose of every step, fillet and undercut, and check them one by one against the rules;
3. estimate the minimum shaft diameter from power and speed, and explain why this estimate is conservative;
4. find the forces of the gear on the shaft and the support reactions in the two planes, draw the bending-moment and torque diagrams, locate the critical section, and carry out a static strength check;
5. find the stress concentration factor, notch sensitivity factor, size factor and surface factor at the critical section, carry out the fatigue strength check by the safety factor method and the modified Goodman method, and compare the two methods;
6. calculate the deflection, slope and critical speed of a shaft with a beam model, and tell a rigid shaft from a flexible one;
7. check the stresses in the shaft by finite elements, calculate the fatigue life from a measured load spectrum, rainflow counting and linear damage accumulation, and make a design decision on that basis;
8. in the digital factory, choose parts from the parts library, model in CAD, calculate strength, stiffness, natural frequency and fatigue life in CAE software, and check the finite-element results by hand calculation (including how the bearing supports are applied);
9. size the shafts of a robot joint and of a high-speed machine by torsional stiffness, and by infinite life and critical speed respectively, and explain how a load that rotates with the shaft produces a mean stress;
10. complete the design calculation, three-dimensional model, analysis report and drawings of a shaft according to an engineering task sheet, and submit them for review.
