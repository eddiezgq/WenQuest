### 33.5.1 The problem

The static safety factor of Section 33.4 is at least {{ex33_4.S_static_min:.2f}}, greater than 1.5, which looks comfortable. But a shaft almost never fails by being crushed at one stroke; it fails by fatigue. A reducer runs a dozen or so hours a day, and with each turn of the output shaft the surface goes through one tension–compression reversal. With each start and stop the torque rises from zero to the rated value and falls back to zero. After a few years the number of cycles is of the order of tens of millions. A crack quietly starts at the place where the stress is most concentrated, the bottom of a keyway or a shoulder fillet, and grows until the remaining section can no longer carry the load. This section calculates the fatigue factor of safety of each section and answers: where does trouble start first? Is it enough? If not, what can be done?

### 33.5.2 Stress cycles in the shaft

When the shaft rotates, the direction of the bending moment is set by the gear force and does not turn with the shaft, whereas a point on the shaft surface turns with the shaft to the tension side and then to the compression side (Animation 33.5.1). The bending stress is therefore a **fully reversed cycle**:

$$
\sigma_\mathrm a = \frac{M}{W}, \qquad \sigma_\mathrm m = 0 . \tag{33.5.1}
$$

There is one cycle per revolution. The output shaft runs at 138 r/min, and at 4000 h a year this gives 3 × 10⁷ cycles, far beyond the cycle base of 10⁷ for 45 steel, so the shaft must be designed for infinite life.

The torque does not change with the rotation of the shaft, but it changes with the operating conditions. For a machine that starts and stops often, or whose torque fluctuates, treat it as a **repeated cycle**, that is,

$$
\tau_\mathrm a = \tau_\mathrm m = \frac{\tau}{2} = \frac{T}{2W_\mathrm T} ; \tag{33.5.2}
$$

When the torque is very steady it can be treated as a static stress, and when the direction changes often it is treated as fully reversed. The run-in test bench record of the WQR-105 shows a large torque cycle at every start (Section 33.8), so treating it as a repeated cycle is reasonable.

### 33.5.3 Fatigue limit of the part: four corrections

The fatigue limits $\sigma_{-1}$ and $\tau_{-1}$ given in materials handbooks are measured on smooth, polished specimens of about 7–10 mm diameter. A real shaft is thicker, its surface is not as fine, and it has steps and keyways, so the fatigue limit must be reduced (Chapter 11). Four corrections matter most in shaft calculation:

**1. Stress concentration.** The ratio of the local maximum stress at an abrupt change of section to the nominal stress is called the theoretical stress-concentration factor, $\alpha_\sigma$ (bending) and $\alpha_\tau$ (torsion); it depends only on the geometry. In fatigue the material does not "accept the notch in full": when the notch is very sharp or the material is soft, the actual loss of fatigue strength is less than $\alpha$ predicts. The notch sensitivity $q$ (0–1) is used to correct this into the fatigue stress-concentration factor:

$$
k_\sigma = 1 + q_\sigma(\alpha_\sigma - 1), \qquad k_\tau = 1 + q_\tau(\alpha_\tau - 1) . \tag{33.5.3}
$$

$q$ grows as the fillet radius grows and as the strength of the material rises (the Neuber–Kuhn fit in the fatigue chapter of [SHI]), so high-strength steels are more sensitive to notches.

**2. Size.** The larger the diameter, the more material in the surface layer is under high stress and the greater the chance of containing a defect, so the lower the fatigue limit. The size factor is calculated as $\varepsilon = 1.24\,d^{-0.107}$ (2.79 mm ≤ d ≤ 51 mm, fatigue chapter of [SHI]); this expression serves for both rotating bending and torsion, so in this chapter $\varepsilon_\sigma = \varepsilon_\tau = \varepsilon$. Domestic handbooks give separate curves for bending and torsion, with similar values.

**3. Surface.** Tool marks are themselves tiny notches. The surface factor is $\beta = a\,\sigma_\mathrm b^{\,b}$, where $a$ and $b$ differ for ground, machined, hot-rolled and forged surfaces; the higher the strength of the steel, the smaller $\beta$ is, which shows once more that alloy steels need a better surface.

**4. Surface strengthening.** Shot peening, rolling and surface hardening make $\beta_\mathrm q > 1$; SH-301 in this chapter is not strengthened, so take 1.

These are combined into the combined fatigue factor (the notation of domestic textbooks; here $\beta$ is the surface factor, not to be confused with the $\beta_\sigma$ of DIN 743 in Section 33.5.7, which is the fatigue stress-concentration factor):

$$
K_\sigma = \left(\frac{k_\sigma}{\varepsilon_\sigma} + \frac{1}{\beta_\sigma} - 1\right)\frac{1}{\beta_\mathrm q}, \qquad
K_\tau = \left(\frac{k_\tau}{\varepsilon_\tau} + \frac{1}{\beta_\tau} - 1\right)\frac{1}{\beta_\mathrm q} . \tag{33.5.4}
$$

The fully reversed fatigue limits of the part are then $\sigma_{-1}/K_\sigma$ and $\tau_{-1}/K_\tau$. The notation of [SHI] multiplies the correction factors onto the fatigue limit, $S_e = k_a k_b \cdots \sigma'_e$, and then enlarges the stress with $K_f$; the two notations have the same physical meaning, but Eq. (33.5.4) adds the effects of surface and notch instead of multiplying them, so the results differ slightly. This book calculates both, see Example 33.5.2. In English-language textbooks and in the programs of the digital factory the fatigue stress-concentration factor is written $K_\mathrm f$ (bending) and $K_{\mathrm fs}$ (torsion), which are the $k_\sigma$ and $k_\tau$ of this book.

### 33.5.4 Theoretical stress-concentration factor of a shoulder: by finite elements

The traditional source of $\alpha$ is Peterson's charts of stress-concentration factors ([PET]), which are themselves the results of photoelastic experiments and numerical calculation. Early in design, when the fillet size is not yet known, [SHI] gives a set of "first-round estimates": for a shoulder with a sharp fillet (r/d = 0.02) take $\alpha_\sigma$ = {{ex33_5k.kt_sharp}} and $\alpha_\tau$ = {{ex33_5k.kts_sharp}}, and for a generous fillet (r/d = 0.1) take {{ex33_5k.kt_round}} and {{ex33_5k.kts_round}}; for an end-milled keyway take 2.14 and 3.0.

With the finite element method we can compute $\alpha$ directly for our own geometry. Program 33.5.1 builds a stepped round shaft with D/d = 1.2, a small-diameter segment of length 2.5d and a large-diameter segment of length 1.5D, with the end face of the large end fixed and pure bending or pure torsion applied to the end face of the small end. The mesh is refined locally at the fillet (Gmsh second-order tetrahedra, CalculiX solver; mesh writing and result reading use the same programs as "Simulation & Analysis" of the digital factory), and the ratio of the maximum stress to the nominal stress is found (Figure 33.5.1):

Three points need attention when finding $\alpha$ by finite elements. The mesh must be fine enough at the fillet, and a refinement comparison must be made (this example uses elements of size r/4 at the fillet; at r/d = 0.09 the elements at the fillet are refined to r/6, and $\alpha_\sigma$ and $\alpha_\tau$ change by only {{ex33_5k.conv_s:.2f}}% and {{ex33_5k.conv_t:.2f}}%). The load application and the constraint must be far enough from the fillet (here the loaded end is more than 2.5d away and the fixed end more than 1.5D away). And the result is valid only for this geometry; if D/d changes, it must be recomputed.

### 33.5.5 Factor of safety

With the combined fatigue factors, the safety factors for bending and for torsion are

$$
S_\sigma = \frac{\sigma_{-1}}{K_\sigma \sigma_\mathrm a + \psi_\sigma \sigma_\mathrm m}, \qquad
S_\tau = \frac{\tau_{-1}}{K_\tau \tau_\mathrm a + \psi_\tau \tau_\mathrm m} , \tag{33.5.5}
$$

where $\psi$ is the mean-stress sensitivity factor, which expresses how much the mean stress weakens the fatigue strength. From the Goodman line (Chapter 11), $\psi_\sigma = \sigma_{-1}/\sigma_\mathrm b$ and, for torsion, $\psi_\tau = \tau_{-1}/(\sigma_\mathrm b/\sqrt3)$; for quenched and tempered 45 steel these are {{ex33_5.psi_s_I:.2f}} and {{ex33_5.psi_t_I:.2f}} respectively. Domestic textbooks often take smaller empirical values (for carbon steel $\psi_\sigma$ = 0.1–0.2), which corresponds to a limit curve more "optimistic" than the Goodman line; this book uses the Goodman line throughout, which errs on the safe side.

When bending and torsion act together, they are combined by an elliptical relation. This relation originated in the combined bending–torsion fatigue tests of Gough and Pollard; when $\tau_{-1}/\sigma_{-1} \approx 1/\sqrt3$ it agrees with the distortion-energy theory:

$$
S_\mathrm{ca} = \frac{S_\sigma S_\tau}{\sqrt{S_\sigma^2 + S_\tau^2}} \ge [S] . \tag{33.5.6}
$$

The required factor of safety $[S]$ is 1.3–1.5 when the material is homogeneous and the loads and stresses are calculated fairly accurately, and 1.5–1.8 when the material is less homogeneous and the calculation cruder; this chapter takes {{ex33_5.S_req}}.

### 33.5.6 Worked examples

**Example 33.5.1** Calculate the coefficients for Sections I–IV.

The material is quenched and tempered 45 steel, with $\sigma_\mathrm b$ = {{ex33_5.sigma_b:.0f}} MPa, $\sigma_{-1}$ = {{ex33_5.sigma_1:.0f}} MPa and $\tau_{-1}$ = {{ex33_5.tau_1:.0f}} MPa; all mating surfaces are ground. The $\alpha$ values of Sections II and III are computed by finite elements for their own D/d and fillets. For Sections I and IV the keyway is taken (for Section IV, the position on the extension where the keyway begins, a few millimeters to the right of the shoulder; the stress concentration of the shoulder itself is smaller than that of the keyway), using the first-round estimates for an end-milled keyway, with the fillet at the groove bottom taken as 0.02d.

Two points need explanation. First, the groove-bottom fillet specified in GB/T 1095 is about 0.16–0.25 mm for an 8 × 7 key and about 0.25–0.40 mm for a 12 × 8 key, which is even smaller than 0.02d, so the true $\alpha$ is larger; the first-round estimate is therefore not conservative here, and the drawing should specify a larger groove-bottom fillet, or $\alpha$ should be read from the charts for the actual fillet or found by finite elements. Second, Section I also has an interference fit: at the edge of the hub the fit pressure changes abruptly and there is fretting wear, so its fatigue stress concentration is of the same order as that of the keyway or even larger, and the right end of the hub rests against the collar, so the edge of the interference fit also coincides with the fillet of Section II. This section calculates only for the keyway. Once the coefficient for the interference fit is given in Chapter 25, the task sheet requires that Sections I and II be rechecked in the calculation sheet using the larger of the two.

The [SHI] modified Goodman method works as follows ($S_e$ follows the notation of [SHI] and multiplies the correction factors onto the fatigue limit, but starts from the $\sigma_{-1}$ of this book's materials table rather than the estimate $0.5\sigma_\mathrm b$ often used in [SHI]): the stress amplitude and the mean stress are each combined by the von Mises rule (the stress-concentration factor multiplies both), $\sigma'_\mathrm a = \sqrt{(k_\sigma\sigma_\mathrm a)^2 + 3(k_\tau\tau_\mathrm a)^2}$ and $\sigma'_\mathrm m = \sqrt3\,k_\tau\tau_\mathrm m$, and then $1/n = \sigma'_\mathrm a/S_e + \sigma'_\mathrm m/\sigma_\mathrm b$. Because it multiplies the mean stress by the stress-concentration factor as well, it is more conservative than the safety-factor method.

The conclusion is clear: Sections I, II and III all have adequate margin, while Section IV (the extension) does not: $S_\mathrm{ca}$ = {{ex33_5.S_ca_IV:.2f}} < {{ex33_5.S_req}}, and the modified Goodman method gives only {{ex33_5.n_sg_IV:.2f}} (Figure 33.5.3). **Example 33.5.3** How should Section IV be changed?

There are three ways:

1. Change to quenched and tempered 40Cr and keep everything else: $K_\tau$ = {{ex33_5.K_t_40:.2f}} (the strength is higher, and the notch sensitivity also rises to {{ex33_5.q_t_40:.2f}}), $S_\mathrm{ca}$ = {{ex33_5.S40_IV:.2f}}, which meets the requirement.
2. Increase the extension to Ø32 (key changed to 10 × 8), keeping 45 steel: $\tau$ falls to {{ex33_5.tau_32:.1f}} MPa and $S_\mathrm{ca}$ = {{ex33_5.S32_IV:.2f}}, which also meets the requirement. But the step from Ø35 to Ø32 is only 1.5 mm high, so the axial location of the coupling has to be changed to an end washer, and the bore of the coupling has to change with it.
3. Use a sled-runner keyway cut with a disc cutter: the end of the groove is a gentle transition, and for bending [SHI] gives an $\alpha_\sigma$ of only {{ex33_5.kt_sled}}. Section IV carries torsion only, and [SHI] gives no value for the effect in torsion, so [PET] or a finite element calculation is needed before a conclusion can be drawn. A disc-cut keyway needs a type B (flat-ended) key, and the end of the groove must also leave room for the cutter to run out.

Option 1 changes no dimension or mating part and only the material and heat treatment, at the cost of about 50% more material cost per shaft.

One more question cannot be avoided. This section calculated with the rated torque of 350 N·m as a repeated cycle, whereas the starting peak recorded on the run-in test bench is {{ex33_5.T_peak:.0f}} N·m, which is {{ex33_5.K_peak:.2f}} times the rated value. If every start were a repeated cycle at this peak, the safety factor of Section IV would be only {{ex33_5.S_peak_IV:.2f}}, below 1, and the shaft would simply not hold up under an "infinite life" design. But the starting peaks occur a limited number of times, dozens or hundreds a day, far fewer than the bending cycle once per revolution. For a load of this kind, "a few large cycles plus very many small ones", the safety-factor method either underestimates the amplitude (if calculated with the rated value) or treats a finite number of peaks as infinite (if calculated with the peak), and cannot give a clear answer in either case. The right approach is a finite-life calculation from the actual load spectrum, see Section 33.8.

### 33.5.7 The DIN 743 procedure

The German standard DIN 743, "Shafts and axles, calculation of load capacity" (2012 edition), is the shaft checking method in general use in European industry, and many commercial programs (such as KISSsoft and MDESIGN) can calculate by it. Its approach is the same as in this section, but every factor is written out in more detail. The symbols below follow DIN 743, in which $\beta_\sigma$ and $\beta_\tau$ are the fatigue stress-concentration factors (equivalent to $k_\sigma$ and $k_\tau$ of this section) and not surface factors. The procedure is as follows:

1. **Size correction of the material strength.** The standard gives the tensile strength and yield strength at a test diameter, which are multiplied by a "technological size factor" $K_1(d_\mathrm{eff})$ according to the blank diameter and the heat treatment: the thicker the blank of a quenched and tempered steel, the less completely the core is hardened and the lower the strength. This is a point that the $\varepsilon$ of this section does not distinguish.
2. **Fatigue limits.** The fatigue limits in bending and torsion are found in proportion from the corrected tensile strength.
3. **Notch effect.** The fatigue stress-concentration factors $\beta_\sigma$ and $\beta_\tau$ are given by the charts and formulas of the standard, covering shoulders, keyways, interference fits, undercuts, cross holes and so on, and are corrected by a "geometrical size factor" $K_2(d)$.
4. **Surface and strengthening.** The surface roughness factor $K_{F\sigma}$ (calculated from Rz) and the strengthening factor $K_V$.
5. **Fatigue limit of the part.** $\sigma_\mathrm{WK} = \sigma_\mathrm W(d)/K_\sigma$ with $K_\sigma = (\beta_\sigma/K_2 + 1/K_{F\sigma} - 1)/K_V$, which has the same form as Eq. (33.5.4).
6. **Mean stress.** The fatigue strength amplitude of the part is found from the mean-stress sensitivity factor of the part and the loading case (constant mean stress or constant stress ratio).
7. **Factor of safety.** The utilizations in tension–compression and in bending are added linearly and then combined with torsion by the root of the sum of squares, with fatigue and static strength checked together (including the support effect of local yielding); finite-life calculation is in another part of DIN 743.

The DIN 743 procedure has quite a few more coefficients than the hand calculation of this section, but each has a basis in the standard, which makes it suitable as a company design specification and as the algorithm of software. The course design project in Chapter 50 will compare the method of this section with the DIN 743 results once.

### 33.5.8 Engineering practice

Step 4 of task sheet TS-33-1: in the calculation sheet, write the fatigue check of the four sections (with tables like Examples 33.5.1 and 33.5.2), give the source of every coefficient looked up (standard number, table or figure number, formula), and attach the mesh refinement comparison for each $\alpha$ obtained by finite elements. If Section IV does not satisfy the requirement, write a comparison of the improvement options but do not decide yet; leave the decision to step 5 (Section 33.8), after the life has been calculated from the load spectrum.

**Everyday example.** Bend a paper clip back and forth with your hand and it breaks after a few dozen bends; that is "low-cycle fatigue", where each bend goes to yield, and if the surface is scratched first it breaks even sooner. The shaft in a machine is a different case: the stress is far below yield, and it takes several million cycles to break. The fracture surface shows rings of "beach marks" centered on the crack origin, left by the crack pausing and growing again. Search the web for photographs of "shaft fatigue fracture" and take a look; engineers read these marks to tell where the crack began.

---

**Engineer's notes**

- More than 90% of fatigue cracks in shafts start at a stress concentration: the end of a keyway, a shoulder fillet, the edge of an interference fit, an undercut. When reading a drawing, look at these places first.
- The edge of an interference fit has a strong stress concentration (the fit pressure changes abruptly there) and fretting wear, and handbooks at home and abroad give large fatigue stress-concentration factors for it. Cutting a relief groove in the shaft near the edge of the fit, or thinning the end of the hub, can reduce it.
- Do not use a "higher factor of safety" to cover up loads that are not well understood. Once the loads are accurate, [S] can be taken smaller; if the loads are estimated crudely, even a large [S] is not necessarily safe.
- The problem of Section IV exists equally in version A. For version A shafts already produced in the digital factory, the remaining life can be estimated by the method of Section 33.8 to decide whether a recall or early replacement is needed. This is the work of the quality department and part of the engineering change (Chapter 44).
