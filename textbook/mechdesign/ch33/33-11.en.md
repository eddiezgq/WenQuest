### 33.11.1 The problem

The industrial flat-bed lockstitch machine is the most numerous machine in a garment factory. Its upper shaft (also called the main shaft) runs across the machine head and is driven directly by a servo motor at up to {{ex33_11.n_max:.0f}} r/min: each revolution, the crank at the front end moves the needle bar up and down once, making one stitch. A machine sews several hundred thousand stitches a day; running continuously at top speed, the upper shaft makes {{ex33_11.cyc_h:.0f}} revolutions per hour and passes $10^7$ cycles in about {{ex33_11.h_1e7:.0f}} hours, so within a few months the machine has gone past the knee of the fatigue curve. Such a shaft can only be designed for **infinite life**.

Compared with the previous two shafts, the upper shaft of a lockstitch machine is characterized by high speed and light load: the motor is rated at only 400 W, and the average power in normal sewing is about {{ex33_11.P_sew:.0f}} W. The load on the shaft is mainly not the transmitted torque but the inertial forces of the reciprocating needle bar and connecting rod. Inertial force is proportional to the square of the speed, so when the sewing machine maker wants to raise the top speed from {{ex33_11.n_max:.0f}} to {{ex33_11.n_up:.0f}} r/min, the first question is: will the upper shaft still hold?

This section takes the lockstitch machine WQ-LS from the book WenQuest, *Sewing Machine Design and Manufacturing*, as its subject (the data are schematic data from that book), checks the upper shaft LS-101, and evaluates the speed-up proposal.

### 33.11.2 Structure and loads

LS-101 is a stepped shaft of quenched-and-tempered 45 steel with ground journals and an overall length of {{ex33_11.length:.0f}} mm (Figure 33.11.3, top). The front end, Ø10, carries the needle-bar crank (with a balance weight); the two Ø12 journals at the ends carry 6201 deep-groove ball bearings (parts library [[part:A-BRG-DG/6201]]); the Ø16 shaft body in the middle carries the take-up cam and the feed eccentric; the rear end, Ø10, carries the timing pulley, the handwheel and the motor rotor. At the shoulders from journal to shaft body, the fillet is only r = {{ex33_11.fillet:.1f}} mm, limited by the chamfer on the bearing inner ring (Section 33.2.5).

**Inertial force.** The needle-bar mechanism is a slider-crank mechanism: crank radius $r$ = {{ex33_11.r:.1f}} mm, connecting-rod length $l$ = {{ex33_11.l:.0f}} mm, $\lambda = r/l$ = {{ex33_11.lam:.3f}}, and reciprocating mass (needle bar, needle, clamp and part of the connecting rod) $m$ = {{ex33_11.m_r:.2f}} kg. The reciprocating inertial force acts along the needle bar (vertically); taken to second order,

$$
F_y = m r \omega^2 \left[ (1 - k_\mathrm b)\cos\theta + \lambda\cos 2\theta \right], \qquad F_x = -k_\mathrm b\, m r \omega^2 \sin\theta , \tag{33.11.1}
$$

where $\theta$ is the crank angle (0 when the needle bar is at top dead center) and $k_\mathrm b$ = {{ex33_11.bal:.2f}} is the balance ratio: the balance weight on the crank produces a centrifugal force opposite to the crank and rotating with the shaft, which cancels {{ex33_11.bal_pct:.0f}}% of the first-order reciprocating inertial force, at the price of introducing a horizontal component $F_x$ (balancing of machinery, Chapter 22). At {{ex33_11.n_max:.0f}} r/min the amplitude of the first-order inertial force $m r \omega^2$ is {{ex33_11.amp1:.0f}} N and the maximum resultant is {{ex33_11.F_max:.0f}} N (Figure 33.11.1, top). A Ø16 shaft and a 400 W motor must bear alternating forces of several hundred newtons; in high-speed machinery the inertial force is often far larger than the working load.

**Torque.** The reciprocating inertial force also makes the torque fluctuate back and forth within one revolution (Figure 33.11.1, bottom). Taken to third order,

$$
T_\mathrm i = m r^2 \omega^2 \left( \frac{\lambda}{4}\sin\theta - \frac12 \sin 2\theta - \frac{3\lambda}{4}\sin 3\theta \right) , \tag{33.11.2}
$$

with a peak-to-peak value of {{ex33_11.T_pp:.1f}} N·m, while the mean torque corresponding to the sewing power is only {{ex33_11.T_mean:.2f}} N·m. Within one revolution the motor alternately drives and is back-driven, which is why a servo motor must have ample torque margin.

### 33.11.3 A rotating shaft and rotating forces: how the stress cycles

Section 33.5 said that "the bending stress is fully reversed", on the premise that **the force direction is fixed, the shaft turns**: a point on the shaft surface passes the tension side and then the compression side once per revolution. In the upper shaft of a lockstitch machine, however, part of the force is fixed and part turns with the shaft:

- The centrifugal force of the balance weight rotates with the shaft and keeps a constant direction relative to a point on the shaft; acting alone, it produces a static bending stress, that is, a **mean stress**.
- The first-order reciprocating inertial force $F_y \propto \cos\theta$ has a fixed direction and a varying magnitude. It can be resolved into two rotating forces of equal size (each half the amplitude), one turning forward with the shaft and one backward. The forward half is stationary relative to the shaft and also contributes mean stress; the backward half turns at twice the shaft speed relative to the shaft and produces alternating stress.
- The second-order inertial force and the timing-belt tension produce alternating stress.

The first two items are the interesting ones: the centrifugal force of the balance weight ($k_\mathrm b$ times the amplitude) and the forward half of the first-order inertial force (0.5 times the amplitude) point in exactly opposite directions, and at a balance ratio of {{ex33_11.bal:.2f}} they almost cancel, leaving only {{ex33_11.bal_cancel:.2f}} times the amplitude. So **the mean stress of the upper shaft is small, and the stress amplitude comes mainly from the backward half of the first-order inertial force and the second-order inertial force**. What the balance weight balances is the "static" part; the alternating part is beyond its reach (Exercise 33.11.1).

"The bending stress is fully reversed" holds here only approximately. Program 33.11.1 does not make this decomposition but takes points directly on the shaft: 72 points around the circumference of the section, each turning once with the shaft; from the bending moments in the two planes it computes the bending stress $\sigma(\theta)$ of each point at each angle, finds the stress amplitude and mean stress, then computes the safety factor by Eq. (33.5.5), taking the smallest over the points. This way of "following a point on the shaft for one revolution" is how finite element fatigue software treats rotating parts.

**Which section is the most dangerous.** The crank overhangs at the front end, and the bending moment grows from zero at the crank to its maximum at the front bearing. On either side of the front bearing there is a shoulder, both with a fillet of only r = {{ex33_11.fillet:.1f}} mm:

- Section I: crank seat Ø10 → front journal Ø12 (11 mm from the crank center). This has the smallest diameter, all the torque passes through it, and it also carries the fitting edge of the crank hub and the small flat for the set screw.
- Section II: front journal Ø12 → shaft body Ø16 (on the other side of the front bearing). The bending moment is somewhat larger, but the diameter is much larger.

Both sections must be calculated, and the more dangerous one taken. At {{ex33_11.n_max:.0f}} r/min, the most dangerous point of Section I has $\sigma_\mathrm a$ = {{ex33_11.sa5:.1f}} MPa and $\sigma_\mathrm m$ = {{ex33_11.sm5:.1f}} MPa (Figure 33.11.2); Section II has {{ex33_11.saB5:.1f}} MPa and {{ex33_11.smB5:.1f}} MPa.

### 33.11.4 Infinite-life check

**Stress concentration.** For the shoulder of Section I, D/d = 12/10 and r/d = {{ex33_11.fillet:.1f}}/10; the same finite element program as in Section 33.5.4 gives the theoretical stress concentration factors $\alpha_\sigma$ = {{ex33_11.alpha_s:.2f}} and $\alpha_\tau$ = {{ex33_11.alpha_t:.2f}} (Section II, 16/12: {{ex33_11.alpha_sB:.2f}} and {{ex33_11.alpha_tB:.2f}}). The fillet is small, so the notch sensitivity factors are $q_\sigma$ = {{ex33_11.q_s:.2f}} and $q_\tau$ = {{ex33_11.q_t:.2f}}, giving effective stress concentration factors $k_\sigma$ = {{ex33_11.k_s:.2f}} and $k_\tau$ = {{ex33_11.k_t:.2f}}; the size factor is {{ex33_11.eps:.2f}} and the ground-surface factor {{ex33_11.beta:.2f}}, so the overall influence factors are $K_\sigma$ = {{ex33_11.K_s:.2f}} and $K_\tau$ = {{ex33_11.K_t:.2f}}.

**Safety factor.** For quenched-and-tempered 45 steel, $\sigma_{-1}$ = {{ex33_11.sigma_1:.0f}} MPa and $\tau_{-1}$ = {{ex33_11.tau_1:.0f}} MPa. For Section I at {{ex33_11.n_max:.0f}} r/min, $S_\sigma$ = {{ex33_11.Ss5:.2f}}, $S_\tau$ = {{ex33_11.St5:.2f}}, and the combined $S$ = {{ex33_11.S5:.2f}}; raising the speed to {{ex33_11.n_up:.0f}} r/min multiplies the inertial force by {{ex33_11.k_up:.2f}}, the bending stress amplitude rises to {{ex33_11.sa55:.1f}} MPa, and $S$ = {{ex33_11.S55:.2f}}. For Section II the values are {{ex33_11.SB5:.2f}} and {{ex33_11.SB55:.2f}} respectively. Note that the torque is far from negligible here: the inertia torque reverses sign every revolution, and Section I is only Ø10, so $S_\tau$ is comparable to $S_\sigma$.

Both speeds give values above 1.5, so the strength of the upper shaft is no obstacle to the speed-up, but after the speed-up the margin is no longer generous. The calculation for Section I has not yet included the fretting wear at the fitting edge of the crank hub or the stress concentration at the set-screw flat, and these two places are exactly where upper shafts of lockstitch machines most often break in practice. The speed-up proposal must also check how the crank is fixed (for example, switching to a clamped crank and eliminating the flat).

The diameter of this shaft is determined mainly not by strength but by the bearing (the 12 mm bore of the 6201 is already among the smaller sizes that can carry this speed and load), by stiffness (the crank overhangs, and the slope at the journal affects the motion accuracy of the needle bar), and by the critical speed.

### 33.11.5 Critical speed and natural frequency

A high-speed shaft must be checked for critical speed (Section 33.7). Program 33.11.1 uses the beam-element method of Section 33.7 and applies the crank with its balance weight, the take-up cam, the feed eccentric, the timing pulley, the handwheel and the motor rotor, {{ex33_11.m_total:.2f}} kg in all, as lumped masses on the shaft (Figure 33.11.3), simply supported at the two bearings. The first critical speed comes out as $n_\mathrm{c1}$ = {{ex33_11.nc1:.0f}} r/min, {{ex33_11.ratio:.1f}} times the top speed and far outside the 0.7 limit: LS-101 is a rigid shaft.

**Checking with finite elements.** The digital factory's “Simulation & Analysis” can compute the natural frequencies of a part (modal analysis). It does not include the parts mounted on the shaft, so the comparison must be with the beam-element result without lumped masses: the first critical speed of the bare shaft is {{ex33_11.nc0:.0f}} r/min, that is, {{ex33_11.hz0:.0f}} Hz.

Here is a trap that almost every beginner falls into: how to apply the bearing supports.

- Constraining the radial displacement over the whole outer surface of the bearing seat (as in the strength calculation of factory Lab 8) gives a first natural frequency of {{ex33_11.f_full:.0f}} Hz, twice what beam theory predicts. The reason is that the 10 mm wide bearing seat is clamped all around, so the shaft cannot rotate in the bearing; this amounts to fixed ends, whereas a deep-groove ball bearing allows a small angular rotation of the shaft and is closer to a pinned support. For strength calculations such a constraint does little harm (the bending moment is determined mainly by the forces and the span), but for modal analysis the difference is large.
- Constraining only a ring about 1 mm wide at the middle of the bearing seat (tick “Middle ring only (pinned)” under bearing support in “Simulation & Analysis”) gives {{ex33_11.f_fe:.0f}} Hz, which differs from beam theory by {{ex33_11.err_fe:.1f}}%.

The second mode, {{ex33_11.f_fe2:.0f}} Hz, is the "twin" of the same bending mode in the vertical plane: the shaft is a body of revolution, so in theory the bending frequencies in the two directions are the same; in the finite element model the "middle ring only" constraint has just a dozen or so nodes, not quite symmetrically distributed, so the constraint stiffnesses in the two directions differ slightly, which accounts for the difference of about 3%. The third mode is {{ex33_11.f_fe3:.0f}} Hz (second-order tetrahedra, {{ex33_11.n_el_modal}} elements, mesh size 3 mm). The first frequency is about 4% higher than beam theory, of which about 1% comes from the elastic modulus (the finite element material library takes 210 GPa for 45 steel, the beam-element program 206 GPa) and the rest from the mesh and the width of the constraint ring. This example shows that a finite element result depends first of all on the boundary conditions; "calibrating" the constraint method once on a case that can be done by hand, and only then computing cases that cannot, is a basic skill for engineers using CAE.

### 33.11.6 Speeding up to 5 500 r/min: the problem lies beyond the shaft

The upper shaft itself is fine. So where does the risk of a speed-up lie? {{ex33_11.n_max:.0f}} r/min corresponds to a rotation frequency of {{ex33_11.hz5:.0f}} Hz and {{ex33_11.n_up:.0f}} r/min to {{ex33_11.hz55:.0f}} Hz; the frequency of the second-order reciprocating inertial force is twice the rotation frequency. It rises from {{ex33_11.hz2_5:.0f}} Hz to {{ex33_11.hz2_55:.0f}} Hz. *Sewing Machine Design and Manufacturing* gives the first natural frequency of the head arm (the cast-iron casing) as about 190 Hz. After the speed-up the second-order excitation comes close to it, the machine head will vibrate noticeably, the noise will increase, and the stitches will also be affected. The balance weight can balance only the first-order inertial force, not the second-order; so what the speed-up proposal really has to change is the machine head: ribbing the head arm to raise its natural frequency clear of twice the rotation frequency (the scheme in Chapter 23 of that book raises it to about 217 Hz), reducing the reciprocating mass as far as possible, and readjusting the balance ratio according to vibration tests of the whole machine, rather than changing the upper shaft. This is a common engineering conclusion: checking a shaft ends by returning to the whole machine.

**Engineer's notes**

- The journals of high-speed shafts are often surface-hardened or carburized and quenched, then ground: this raises the fatigue strength (surface strengthening factor, Section 33.5) and also the wear resistance. The upper shaft of a sewing machine also requires journal roundness and cylindricity within a few micrometers, otherwise the bearing is noisy.
- The balance ratio $k_\mathrm b$ is a compromise: the larger the balance weight, the smaller the vibration in the vertical direction and the larger in the horizontal. Sewing machine makers choose by test the balance ratio that gives the least head vibration, generally between 0.4 and 0.6.
- Cranks and eccentrics are often fixed to the shaft with set screws, with a small flat milled on the shaft at the corresponding place. The flat is a source of stress concentration: at the take-up cam and the eccentric the bending moment is small and the effect limited, but the flat on the crank seat is close to Section I, where all the torque passes and the bending moment is also considerable, making it a weak spot of the upper shaft; high-speed machines often switch to a clamped crank.
