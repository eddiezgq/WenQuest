### 33.7.1 The problem

In the first few seconds of the spin cycle, a washing machine often shakes violently with a "thump, thump", and then runs smoothly once the speed has risen further. In rotating machinery this is called "passing through the critical speed". The rotor on any shaft (gear, impeller, drum, grinding wheel) has its center of mass a little off the axis. When it rotates, the centrifugal force bends the shaft, and the bending increases the centrifugal force. When the rotational speed approaches the transverse natural frequency of the shaft, the bending grows rapidly, and this rotational speed is the **critical speed**. If the operating speed lies near a critical speed, the shaft vibrates violently and may even break. This section answers two questions: does SH-301 need to worry about this, and which shafts must worry about it?

### 33.7.2 The single-disc rotor

The simplest model is a massless shaft simply supported at both ends with a disc of mass $m$ in the middle, whose center of mass is offset from the axis by $e$. The transverse stiffness of the shaft at the disc is $k$. At angular velocity $\omega$, the deflection $y$ of the disc center satisfies the balance of centrifugal and elastic forces (including the damping ratio $\zeta$):

$$
\frac{y}{e} = \frac{r^2}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}}, \qquad r = \frac{\omega}{\omega_\mathrm c}, \qquad \omega_\mathrm c = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} . \tag{33.7.1}
$$

Here $\omega_\mathrm c$ is the transverse natural angular frequency of the shaft–disc system, and the corresponding rotational speed

$$
n_\mathrm c = \frac{60}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \quad (\mathrm{r/min}) \tag{33.7.2}
$$

is called the first critical speed. Eq. (33.7.1) is plotted in Figure 33.7.2. When the speed approaches $n_\mathrm c$ the deflection grows rapidly. Beyond it the deflection decreases and approaches $e$: the center of mass of the disc then turns to a position between the disc center and the line joining the bearings, almost on that line, and the shaft "centers itself" (Animation 33.7.1). The German engineer A. Föppl first used this model in 1895 to show that stable running above the critical speed is possible. The Englishman H. H. Jeffcott gave the complete solution including damping in 1919, which is why the English literature calls this the Jeffcott rotor (the German literature calls it the Laval rotor, after de Laval, who first ran a steam turbine above its critical speed).

In engineering, shafts are divided into two classes on this basis:

- **Rigid shaft**: operating speed $n \le 0.7\,n_\mathrm{c1}$;
- **Flexible shaft**: operating speed $1.3\,n_\mathrm{c1} \le n \le 0.7\,n_\mathrm{c2}$ ($n_\mathrm{c2}$ is the second critical speed), and $n_\mathrm{c1}$ must be passed through quickly at start-up and shutdown.

These two sets of limits are empirical, and different books take different values. Some domestic textbooks take 0.85 and 1.15 (for example the shaft design chapter of Pu Lianggui et al., Machine Design). [SHI] recommends that the first critical speed be at least twice the operating speed, that is, $n \le 0.5\,n_\mathrm{c1}$. Industry standards for pumps, compressors and the like (such as API 610 and API 617) prescribe a "separation margin" between operating speed and critical speed according to the amplification factor of the resonance peak. The figures and the lab in this book draw the boundaries at 0.7 and 1.3. When designing a particular product, follow the standard of the industry concerned.

### 33.7.3 Methods of calculation

A real shaft has distributed mass, several discs and stepped sections, and the rigorous solution is the eigenvalue problem of beam vibration. Three methods are in common use:

1. **Beam element method**: a numerical method, used by the program in this section. The beam elements of Section 33.6 are given a consistent mass matrix, gears and couplings are added to the nodes as lumped masses, and the generalized eigenvalue problem $K\boldsymbol x = \omega^2 M\boldsymbol x$ is solved (Chapter 40 of *Linear Algebra*).
2. **Dunkerley's formula**: the critical angular velocity of each disc and of the shaft itself is calculated separately, and the result is estimated from

$$
\frac{1}{\omega_\mathrm c^2} \approx \frac{1}{\omega_0^2} + \sum_i \frac{1}{\omega_i^2} \tag{33.7.3}
$$

   The estimate is always low (on the safe side).

3. **Rayleigh's method**: the static deflection curve is used as an approximation to the mode shape and the frequency is found by equating energies. The result is high.

### 33.7.4 Worked examples

**Example 33.7.1** The critical speed of SH-301.

The mass of gear GR-302 (version B, forged with hub) is estimated from its dimensions as {{ex33_7.m_gear:.1f}} kg (the lightening of the web is ignored). Program 33.7.1 uses the beam element method and finds the first and second critical speeds to be {{ex33_7.n_c1:.0f}} r/min and {{ex33_7.n_c2:.0f}} r/min, while the operating speed is only {{ex33_7.n_out:.0f}} r/min, so the first critical speed is {{ex33_7.ratio_B:.0f}} times the operating speed. SH-301 is a typical rigid shaft and need not worry about critical speed.

Note the first mode shape (Figure 33.7.1, left). The largest amplitude is at the end of the overhang, not at the gear. The reason is lever amplification: the gear bends the shaft within the span, and the section at the right bearing B turns through an angle with it. The overhang projects from B like a lever, and the displacement of its end equals this slope multiplied by the overhang length, which makes it larger than the displacement within the span. Without the mass of the coupling the overhang end hardly takes part in the inertia. Once the coupling is fitted the overhang end has mass and the critical speed falls, but it remains far above the operating speed.

With Dunkerley's formula: the critical speed of the gear alone is {{ex33_7.n_g:.0f}} r/min and that of the shaft alone is {{ex33_7.n_s:.0f}} r/min, which combine to {{ex33_7.n_dunk:.0f}} r/min, {{ex33_7.dunk_err:.0f}}% lower than the beam element method, on the safe side.

**Example 33.7.2** A fan shaft: Ø{{ex33_7.d_f:.0f}}, simply supported at both ends, span {{ex33_7.L_f:.0f}} mm, with a {{ex33_7.m_imp:.0f}} kg impeller in the middle, driven directly by a 2-pole motor at {{ex33_7.n_fan:.0f}} r/min.

The mid-span stiffness is $k = 48EI/L^3$ = {{ex33_7.k_mid:.0f}} N/mm. Neglecting the mass of the shaft, Eq. (33.7.2) gives $n_\mathrm c$ = {{ex33_7.n_fan_hand:.0f}} r/min. The beam element method, which includes the shaft's own mass of {{ex33_7.m_shaft_f:.1f}} kg, gives {{ex33_7.n_fan_fe:.0f}} r/min, so the hand calculation is {{ex33_7.fan_err:.1f}}% high. The operating speed is {{ex33_7.ratio_fan:.2f}} times the first critical speed. The second critical speed is {{ex33_7.n_fan_fe2:.0f}} r/min, and the operating speed is {{ex33_7.ratio_fan2:.2f}} times it, so both conditions for a flexible shaft are satisfied. This shaft is a flexible shaft: in normal running the deflection is small, but at every start-up and shutdown it must pass through the critical speed. If the motor has a soft start and the speed rises very slowly, the shaft stays in the resonance region for a long time, and the vibration and the bearing loads are large. In design one must either thicken the shaft until it is a rigid shaft (Task 2 of Lab 33.7) or make sure it passes through quickly and is dynamically balanced (Chapter 22).

### 33.7.5 Which shafts must be checked for critical speed

Low-speed reducer shafts and conveyor roller shafts generally need no check. The shafts that must be checked are motor shafts, shafts of fans and pumps, machine-tool spindles (especially high-speed motorized spindles), rotors of steam turbines and compressors, centrifuges, and high-speed motor rotors in robots and drones. In the design of these shafts the critical speed often decides the dimensions as much as strength does.

**Everyday example** The few seconds of a washing machine's spin cycle are in fact the drum–spring–damper system "passing through the critical speed". The laundry is piled to one side, so the eccentricity $e$ is large and the shaking is violent. The machine first turns slowly for a few revolutions before spinning in order to shake the laundry loose, which reduces $e$.

**Engineer's notes**

- The critical speed does not depend on whether the shaft is horizontal or on gravity. Gravity only makes the static deflection larger, and the static deflection can be used to estimate the critical speed: $n_\mathrm c \approx 946/\sqrt{\delta}$ ($\delta$ is the static deflection at the disc, in mm).
- The elasticity of the bearings and of the housing both make the actual critical speed lower than the value calculated with rigid supports. Calculations for high-speed shafts must include them (rotor dynamics, Chapter 38).
- The gyroscopic effect makes the critical speed of an overhung disc change with speed (higher for forward whirl, lower for backward whirl). High-speed spindles and turbines need a special analysis.
