### 33.6.1 The problem

A shaft can be strong enough and still be unacceptable if it bends too much. If the shaft bends where a gear sits, the two tooth faces no longer touch along the whole face width and the load crowds onto one end of the tooth (the face load distribution factor of Chapter 29). If the shaft turns through an angle at a bearing, the raceway of a ball bearing is loaded unevenly and its life falls. If a machine-tool spindle bends a little, the parts it cuts are inaccurate. Stiffness is therefore the second design criterion for a shaft. How much does SH-301 bend, and is that acceptable?

### 33.6.2 Calculating the bending deformation

The deflection $y$ of a shaft satisfies the differential equation of the deflection curve of a beam (Chapter 9):

$$
EI(z)\,\frac{\mathrm d^2 y}{\mathrm dz^2} = M(z) . \tag{33.6.1}
$$

In a stepped shaft $I(z)$ differs from one segment to the next. By hand one can use superposition as an approximation, or draw the $M/(EI)$ diagram of each segment and integrate it by the moment-area method. Today the most convenient approach is numerical: divide the shaft into many short beam elements, each of constant diameter, assemble the global stiffness matrix, apply the constraints at the bearings and solve (Chapter 13 and Chapter 40 of *Linear Algebra*). Program 33.6.1 uses beam elements 1 mm long, with nodes at every step, bearing and gear. The two planes are calculated separately and then combined as vectors, just as the bending moments were:

$$
y = \sqrt{y_H^2 + y_V^2}, \qquad \theta = \sqrt{\theta_H^2 + \theta_V^2} . \tag{33.6.2}
$$

### 33.6.3 Allowable values

The allowable values of stiffness come from the requirements of the mating parts, not from the shaft itself. The typical maximum slopes given in the shaft chapter of [SHI] are: deep-groove ball bearings 0.001–0.003 rad, cylindrical roller bearings 0.0008–0.0012 rad, tapered roller bearings 0.0005–0.0012 rad, and uncrowned spur gears 0.0005 rad. Domestic textbooks often take the deflection at a gear as $(0.01 \sim 0.03)\,m$, where $m$ is the module. This example takes the stricter values: a slope at the bearings of no more than {{ex33_6.th_brg_allow:.1f}} mrad, a slope at the gear of no more than {{ex33_6.th_gear_allow:.1f}} mrad, and a deflection of no more than {{ex33_6.y_gear_allow_um:.0f}} μm (0.01 m).

**Example 33.6.1** Calculate the deflection and slope of SH-301 at rated torque.

The results are shown in Figure 33.6.1. The combined deflection at the gear is {{ex33_6.y_gear_um:.1f}} μm, only 1/{{ex33_6.margin_y:.0f}} of the allowable value. The slopes at the two bearings are {{ex33_6.th_A:.3f}} mrad and {{ex33_6.th_B:.3f}} mrad, and even the larger is only 1/{{ex33_6.margin_th:.0f}} of the allowable value. The slope at the gear is {{ex33_6.th_gear:.3f}} mrad: the gear is almost at mid-span, where the slope is close to zero.

The deflection curve of the overhang is a straight line. There is no transverse load on the overhang (this example ignores the extra force from the coupling), so it simply tilts as a whole with the slope at the right bearing.

The stiffness of SH-301 is more than adequate. This is the usual situation for a short shaft carrying a heavy load at low speed: the diameter is set by strength (especially torque), and the short span leaves a large margin in stiffness. Conversely, slender shafts, such as the lead screw of a machine tool, the long roller shaft of a conveyor or the long drive shaft in a robot arm, often have their diameter set by stiffness.

**Example 33.6.2** The effect of diameter and material on stiffness.

Deflection is inversely proportional to $EI$, and $I \propto d^4$. If the diameters of all shaft segments are reduced by 10%, the deflection at the gear becomes {{ex33_6.ratio_d90:.2f}} times its former value (≈ 1/0.9⁴). Changing to 40Cr makes the deflection {{ex33_6.ratio_40:.2f}} times its former value, that is, no change. Changing to an aluminum alloy (E = {{ex33_6.E_al:.0f}} GPa) makes it {{ex33_6.ratio_al:.2f}} times its former value. This confirms once more the conclusion of Section 33.1: stiffness is raised by size and structure, not by changing the grade of steel.

### 33.6.5 Torsional stiffness

Torsional deformation is measured by the angle of twist per unit length:

$$
\varphi = \frac{T}{G I_\mathrm p} \quad (\mathrm{rad/mm}) , \qquad I_\mathrm p = \frac{\pi d^4}{32} . \tag{33.6.3}
$$

An ordinary transmission shaft is generally allowed 0.25–1 (°)/m, and precision drives and servo systems demand more. For a robot joint, torsional stiffness directly determines how far the end-effector moves under load and the control bandwidth, and it is often more important than strength. The torsional stiffnesses of the reducer, the shaft and the coupling act in series, and the weakest link determines the whole (Chapter 9 and Chapter 45).

### 33.6.6 Engineering practice

One of the fifth-step items of Task Sheet TS-33-1: write the stiffness check in the calculation report (deflection in the two planes, combined deflection and slope, compared with the allowable values), and state the source of the allowable values. If the bearings are chosen as tapered roller bearings (an exercise in Chapter 34), the allowable slope is much stricter and the check must be done again.

**Everyday example** Lay a bamboo pole across two chairs and hang a bucket of water in the middle: the pole sags noticeably. Replace it with a solid steel bar of the same thickness and it sags much less, because of the difference in elastic modulus (steel is more than ten times as stiff as bamboo). But if the steel bar is only half as thick as the bamboo pole, its second moment of area is only 1/16, and it sags about as much as the bamboo. That is the power of $d^4$.

**Engineer's notes**

- In stiffness calculations the bearings are often treated as simple supports, but the bearings themselves are elastic. The stiffness calculation of a precision spindle must include the bearing stiffness in series, which often accounts for more than half of the total deformation.
- The slope at a gear matters more than the deflection: deflection only changes the center distance slightly, whereas slope makes the tooth faces touch at one end.
- When there is an interference fit between hub and shaft, the hub "reinforces" that part of the shaft, and the real stiffness is higher than that calculated from the shaft diameter. This chapter does not take this into account, which errs on the safe side.
