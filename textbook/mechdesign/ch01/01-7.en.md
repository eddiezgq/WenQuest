### 1.7.1 The problem

Today's methods of machine design (standards, tolerances, strength calculation, the theory of mechanisms, finite elements) were not established overnight. Knowing where they came from and which problems they were meant to solve helps us understand why we do things as we do today, and also to see which practices will be replaced by future methods.

### 1.7.2 Ancient technical rules and machines

The *Kaogong Ji* (Records of the Trades), compiled in the Warring States period and later added to the *Zhou Li* (Rites of Zhou) as its "Winter Offices" section, records the crafts of some thirty trades such as cart-making, bow-making and casting. It lays down the proportions of cart wheels and draft poles and the methods of inspecting them, for example: "apply the compass to see that it is round", "apply the frame to see that it is not warped", "hang a plumb line to see that the spokes are straight", "float it in water to see that it sinks evenly". That is, a compass checks that the wheel is round, a truing frame checks that it is not warped, a plumb line checks that the spokes are straight, and floating the wheel in water checks that its weight is evenly distributed. This is one of the earliest surviving systematic sets of technical rules, and an early form of the idea of a "standard": dimensions in prescribed proportions, products inspected by prescribed methods.

Ancient China also had many ingenious machines. There was the **south-pointing chariot**, whose wooden figure points south however the chariot turns (in the Song dynasty both Yan Su and Wu Deren built one, and the histories record its gears; two reconstructions of its transmission principle have been proposed, an automatic clutched gear train and a differential gear train). There was the **Water-driven Astronomical Clock Tower**, built around 1088, in the Yuanyou reign of the Northern Song, under the direction of Su Song and Han Gonglian. Driven by water power, it combined astronomical observation, demonstration of the heavens and automatic timekeeping, and the device in it that made the water wheel turn intermittently is regarded by historians of science such as Joseph Needham as a forerunner of the **escapement**. Su Song drew its structure and parts and wrote them up in the *Xin Yixiang Fayao* (New Design for an Armillary Clock, 1094), a machine manual with a great many structural drawings. In the Ming dynasty, Song Yingxing's *Tiangong Kaiwu* (The Exploitation of the Works of Nature, 1637) systematically recorded the tools and techniques of agriculture and the crafts, including drawings and descriptions of many machines (water-powered trip hammers, norias, draw looms).

### 1.7.3 The Industrial Revolution and the discipline of mechanical engineering

In 1769 James Watt patented the separate condenser; the efficiency of the steam engine rose greatly, and in the second half of the 18th century machines began to replace human and animal power on a large scale. With more machines, parts had to be interchangeable: in 1841 J. Whitworth proposed a uniform screw-thread standard, and thereafter countries gradually built up systems of standards for threads, tolerances and part sizes.

In the 19th century machine design became a discipline grounded in theory. A series of railway axle failures led A. Wöhler (who published his results around 1870) to study fatigue systematically and to put forward the concept of the fatigue limit (Chapter 11). F. Reuleaux's *Theoretical Kinematics* (1875) abstracted mechanisms into kinematic pairs and links, laying the foundation of the theory of mechanisms (Chapter 14). In 1886 O. Reynolds established the theory of fluid-film lubrication, explaining why a plain bearing can "float" (Chapter 35). In the first half of the 20th century, the theory of elasticity, strength of materials, vibration theory and the strength calculation of gears and bearings gradually matured, and in 1947 the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) was founded.

### 1.7.4 Computers and digitization

In the 1950s the aircraft industry developed the finite element method to analyze aircraft structures (the 1956 paper by M. J. Turner et al. is often regarded as its starting point; Chapter 13). In 1963 I. Sutherland's Sketchpad allowed people for the first time to draw interactively on a computer screen, and CAD followed. Today 3D modeling, finite elements, multibody dynamics and fatigue-life calculation are everyday tools for engineers, and design data flow from CAD all the way to the shop floor and the quality system (Section 1.3).

The tools have changed, but the questions have not: will the part break, will it wear out, can it be made, is it worth the price? The question Wöhler answered with testing machines is answered today with finite elements and fatigue software; the *Kaogong Ji* inspected wheels with compasses and plumb lines, and today we inspect shafts with coordinate measuring machines. Understanding the questions behind the methods matters more than memorizing the methods themselves.

**Engineer's notes**

- Reading the classic originals is good training: you see how earlier engineers thought problems through without today's tools. Both Su Song's *Xin Yixiang Fayao* and Reuleaux's *Theoretical Kinematics* are available in modern editions.
- Behind much "empirical data" lies a history of accidents: shoulders must be given fillets because early axles broke at sharp corners.
