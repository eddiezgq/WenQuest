### 1.3.1 The problem

More than half a century ago, a mechanical engineer's tools were the drawing board, the slide rule and the handbook. When a drawing was finished it was printed as a blueprint and sent to the shop, where the parts were machined to it and inspectors checked them against it. Today's engineer models and simulates on a computer, and drawings flow as data between the company's systems. When an engineer changes the length of a keyway from 45 mm to 40 mm in CAD, how does that change reach the CNC machine on the shop floor, the inspector's inspection sheet and the buyer's purchase order?

- **CAD.** Computer-aided design: building 3D models and producing engineering drawings. This book uses the open-source FreeCAD and the Python parametric modeling library build123d (Chapter 3); the digital factory also has a web design desk that needs no software installed.
- **CAE.** Computer-aided engineering: simulation of strength and stiffness by finite elements, fatigue life, multibody dynamics, temperature fields and so on. The digital factory's "Simulation & Analysis" and "Motion & Dynamics" use open-source solvers such as Gmsh, CalculiX, pyLife and MuJoCo (Chapters 13 and 23).
- **PLM.** **Product lifecycle management** looks after design data and revisions: who changed what and when, who approved it, and which revision is current. The digital factory's "Design Release & Approval" is a simplified PLM.
- **ERP.** **Enterprise resource planning** looks after items, bills of materials, routings, orders, stock and cost. The digital factory uses the open-source ERPNext.
- **MES.** The **manufacturing execution system** looks after the shop floor: which machine a work order goes to, which revision of the CNC program is used, what each machine is doing and how many pieces have been finished.
- **QMS.** The **quality management** system covers inspection plans, inspection records, coordinate measuring machine (CMM) data, disposition of nonconforming product and statistical process control.

### 1.3.3 A single data flow

These systems are linked through data. In the digital factory they exchange messages over a unified data bus, and the labels on the arrows of Figure 1.3.1 are the message types. Take the keyway change of Section 1.3.1 as an example:

1. the engineer changes the keyway length to 40 mm on the web design desk and submits it (`design.submit`);
2. the reviewer approves it in "Design Release & Approval", and the system sends `design.release`: the design revision of SH-301 in ERPNext goes up by one, and a new STEP model, engineering drawing and CNC program for the keyway (`design.gcode`) are generated;
3. when the next work order reaches the shop floor, the keyway-milling operation automatically uses the new program;
4. the CMM inspects to the dimensions of the new drawing, and the results return to the quality system as `quality.measurement`; if the part fails inspection, a nonconformance report (`quality.ncr`) is opened and goes back to ERP for disposition.

This whole chain is often called the **digital thread** (Chapter 44): all the data on a part, from design through manufacture to inspection, are linked together, so that from an inspection record one can trace which revision of the drawing, which revision of the program and on which machine tool the part was made.

### 1.3.4 The parts library

As Section 1.1 noted, most of the parts in a machine are purchased standard parts. Engineers do not draw bearings, bolts and parallel keys themselves; they choose them from a **parts library**. The WenQuest parts library contains bearings, keys, threaded fasteners, seals, couplings, reducers, motors, robot arms and more, each with a table of dimensions, the standard it comes from and a 3D model that can be inserted straight into an assembly. Where this book mentions a standard part, it often gives a parts-library link, for example the output-shaft bearing [[part:A-BRG-DG/6207]]; clicking it opens the page of that bearing in the parts library.

Choosing from a parts library has three advantages: the dimensions come from the standard and cannot be miscopied; the same part number refers to the same item in the bill of materials, the purchase order and the warehouse; and interference and fits can be checked directly in the assembly model.

**Everyday example** A parcel bought online leaves a record in a system at every step, from order, dispatch and transport through the sorting center to signature on delivery, and you can see where it is on your phone at any moment. The digital thread does the same for parts: for every shaft one can find out which revision of the drawing, which batch of material, which machine tool and which inspection it came from.

**Engineer's notes**

- Software only "stores" and "passes on"; it does not judge whether something is "right". A wrong dimension will be faithfully passed to the shop floor, so the review before each release still has to be done by people.
- Data are traceable only if they are under revision control. Telling the shop by word of mouth to "make the keyway 3 mm shorter" without going through release is the most common and most dangerous practice in a factory.
- Open-source tools (FreeCAD, CalculiX, ERPNext, ...) work on the same principles as commercial ones; once you have learned one set, moving to another is only a matter of a new interface.
