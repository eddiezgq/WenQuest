### 1.8.1 The problem

A drawing bears the designer's signature. Parts made to that drawing go into thousands of machines, in other people's factories and homes, right beside them. If a part fails and someone is hurt, is the designer responsible? Besides getting the calculations right, what else is an engineer answerable for?

### 1.8.2 Three cases

**The Comet airliner (1954)** Not long after the world's first jet airliner, the de Havilland Comet, entered service, two of them broke up in flight over the Mediterranean within months of each other in 1954. Investigators put the fuselage of an aircraft of the same type into a water tank and pressurized and depressurized it again and again to simulate takeoffs and landings, until the fuselage cracked. The cracking started near the corners of the cabin windows and of the antenna windows in the cabin roof: the stress concentration at the window corners, combined with one cabin pressurization cycle per flight, caused a fatigue failure. The original verification test had also been flawed: the fuselage section used for the fatigue test had previously been through a high-pressure static test, and the favorable residual stresses left by local yielding made the test life unrealistically long. Afterwards the Comet was given windows with rounded corners, full-scale fatigue testing and "fail-safe" design became basic requirements for aircraft structures, and from the 1970s onward these developed further into "damage-tolerant" design (Chapter 11).

**The Hyatt Regency walkways (1981)** In the lobby of the Hyatt Regency hotel in Kansas City, USA, two skywalks on the second and fourth floors hung one above the other, suspended from the roof by hanger rods. In the original design each hanging point had one continuous rod passing through the box beam of the fourth-floor walkway down to the second floor, with the fourth-floor walkway supported by a nut on the rod. During construction, to make erection easier, this was changed to two rods: an upper one carrying the fourth-floor walkway, and a lower one suspending the second-floor walkway from the fourth-floor box beam. The change looked trivial, but it meant that the nut under the fourth-floor box beam now had to support both walkways: its load became {{fig1_8.ratio:.0f}} times the original (Figure 1.8.1). At a dance in July 1981, with the walkways full of people, the connection pulled through the box beam, both walkways fell, and 114 people died. The investigation afterwards (the report of the US National Bureau of Standards) found that even as originally designed, the capacity of this connection was below what the local building code required, and that the change to the shop drawings had neither been carefully checked by the structural engineers nor prompted anyone to ask "how does the load get through now?". The engineers responsible lost their licenses.

**The Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940)** Four months after it opened, this suspension bridge went into violent torsional oscillation in a wind of moderate strength and finally collapsed. Its deck was very slender and had very little torsional stiffness; the aerodynamic forces of the wind flowing around the deck and the torsional oscillation of the deck excited each other, so that the oscillation grew larger and larger (what is now called flutter). This accident made people realize that a structure cannot be designed for static loads alone: its dynamic behavior governs its safety just as much (Chapter 38). It is often misrepresented as simple resonance, "the frequency of the wind matched the natural frequency of the bridge"; for a rigorous analysis see the references for this chapter.

### 1.8.3 What we learn from the cases

The three cases have features in common:

- **Failure occurred where the design had not looked.** The pressurization fatigue of the Comet, the connection of the Hyatt walkways, the aerodynamic torsion of the Tacoma bridge. The design calculations themselves may not have been wrong; what was wrong was what had "not been calculated".
- **A small change may alter the load path.** The change to the Hyatt hanger rods was a mere stroke of the pen on a drawing, yet it doubled the load. That is why every change must be reviewed (Section 1.2), and the review must ask "how does the load get through?".
- **Something new needs new verification.** The jet airliner and the ultra-slender suspension bridge were both new things in their day; past experience was not enough, and dedicated analysis and testing were needed.

### 1.8.4 The designer's responsibility

The engineering societies of every country have codes of ethics, such as those of the National Society of Professional Engineers (NSPE) and the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) in the United States. Their first canon is almost the same: in the performance of their professional duties, engineers shall hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public. For a machine designer, **engineering ethics** in daily work means:

1. working within your competence, and asking others to check what lies beyond it;
2. being answerable for the documents you sign: for a signed calculation sheet or drawing, you must be able to explain where every number came from (Section 1.6);
3. not letting suspicious results pass: when calculations, tests or field data indicate a problem, report it, and do not conceal it under pressure of schedule or cost;
4. designing for the safety of machinery according to the standards: ISO 12100 (GB/T 15706) sets out the order for eliminating hazards and reducing risk: first eliminate the hazard by design, then add safeguards, and only last rely on warning signs and instructions for use;
5. stating assumptions and limitations honestly: write in the calculation sheet "what was not considered", so that those who come later know where the boundaries are.

**Engineer's notes**

- The investigation reports of engineering accidents are the best textbooks: they lay out the full consequences of a design decision.
- When you find a problem in someone else's design, raise it in writing (a review comment, a change request) and follow it up until it is closed.
- Robots and automated equipment work alongside people, and safety design is becoming ever more important: collaborative robots have their own safety standards (ISO 10218 and its associated technical specifications), and design methods for machinery safety are covered in Section 49.6.
