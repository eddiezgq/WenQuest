### 1.5.1 The problem

Example 1.4.1 required the extension to have a safety factor of at least 1.5 at the starting peak. Why 1.5, and not 1.2 or 3? A large safety factor makes a part heavy and expensive; a small one risks an accident. What exactly is a safety factor guarding against?

### 1.5.2 What the safety factor guards against

The **factor of safety** is defined as the ratio of the strength of a part (its limiting stress) to the stress it carries:

$$
S = \frac{\sigma_\mathrm{lim}}{\sigma} . \tag{1.5.1}
$$

Throughout this book the safety factor is written $S$ (the $S_\mathrm{ca}$ of Chapter 33 is the same quantity); English-language textbooks mostly write $n$ or $n_\mathrm d$. Depending on the problem, the strength and the stress may be the yield strength and the shear stress, the fatigue limit and the stress amplitude, and so on, but the two must correspond. If strength and stress were both exactly known numbers, any $S$ greater than 1 would do. The safety factor has to be well above 1 because both sides are uncertain:

- **Scatter of strength.** The yield strength of one grade of steel varies by anywhere from a few percent to over ten percent between heats and between blanks; heat treatment, size and surface condition add further differences.
- **Scatter of load.** The customer's duty, the shock at starting and the occasional jam are all more complicated than was assumed in design.
- **Model error.** Design formulas are simplified, and stress concentration, residual stress and assembly stress are often left out.
- **Severity of the consequences.** Would a failure injure people, stop production or cause a huge loss?

The first three are uncertainties; the last decides how much risk is acceptable. The traditional approach "packages" all of these into one empirical safety factor: for general machine parts 1.3–2 on the yield strength; for products whose failure has serious consequences, such as cranes, elevators and pressure vessels, the value is laid down by the codes of each industry, which also say which strength the safety factor applies to (for example 12 or more on the breaking force for elevator ropes, and about 1.5 on the yield strength for pressure vessels).

### 1.5.3 Stress-strength interference and reliability

A clearer view treats both the strength $\sigma_\mathrm{lim}$ and the stress $\sigma$ as mutually independent random variables (Figure 1.5.1). Where the two distribution curves overlap, the stress may exceed the strength and the part may fail. If both are normally distributed, with means $\mu_\mathrm{lim}$ and $\mu_\sigma$ and coefficients of variation (standard deviation divided by mean) $C_\mathrm{lim}$ and $C_\sigma$, there is a definite relation between the safety factor defined on the means, $S = \mu_\mathrm{lim}/\mu_\sigma$, and the **reliability**, as follows:

$$
z = \frac{\mu_\mathrm{lim} - \mu_\sigma}{\sqrt{s_\mathrm{lim}^2 + s_\sigma^2}} = \frac{S - 1}{\sqrt{S^2 C_\mathrm{lim}^2 + C_\sigma^2}}, \qquad R = \Phi(z), \tag{1.5.2}
$$

where $s_\mathrm{lim}$ and $s_\sigma$ are the standard deviations, $z$ is called the reliability index, $R$ is the reliability (the probability of not failing) and $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function; for now we simply treat it as a function looked up in a table, and Chapter 40 treats it systematically. Eq. (1.5.2) shows that for the same safety factor, the larger the scatter, the lower the reliability.

So the choice of an empirical safety factor is, at bottom, an estimate of "how large the scatter is and how severe the consequences are". The more reliable the load and material data (for example a measured load spectrum, or test data for the actual batch of material), the smaller the safety factor can be; the scarcer the data and the more severe the consequences, the larger it must be. This is also why Chapter 33 uses the torque measured on the run-in test bench instead of an assumed empirical shock factor.

### 1.5.4 Standards and codes

**Standardization** spares engineers from starting from scratch every time: the size series of parts, the properties of materials, methods of calculation and methods of inspection are all laid down uniformly by standards. The common systems of standards are:

| System | Issued by | Typical content |
|---|---|---|
| GB, GB/T | Standardization Administration of China | National standards: GB is mandatory, GB/T is recommended; dimensions of machine parts (parallel keys GB/T 1096, bearings GB/T 276), tolerances (GB/T 1800), materials (GB/T 699) |
| ISO | International Organization for Standardization | International standards: most GB/T standards are identical to or modified adoptions of ISO standards; e.g. ISO 286 (tolerances), ISO 6336 (gear strength), ISO 281 (bearing life), ISO 12100 (safety of machinery) |
| ASME, AGMA, ANSI | American Society of Mechanical Engineers, American Gear Manufacturers Association, etc. | American design codes: the ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code, ASME Y14.5 (geometric tolerancing), AGMA 2001 (gears) |
| DIN, VDI | German Institute for Standardization, Association of German Engineers | DIN 743 (load capacity of shafts), VDI 2230 (high-strength bolted joints); many ISO standards originated as DIN standards |
| JB, industry standards | The authorities of each industry | Machinery industry standards JB/T, and the safety codes of industries such as lifting equipment and pressure vessels |

Standards are used in two ways. One is "make it like this": the dimensions of parallel keys and the tolerances of bearings, for example; if you do not follow them, you cannot buy the parts and they will not fit. The other is "calculate it like this": gear strength and shaft fatigue, for example, where the standard gives calculation methods and factors validated by a large body of tests. Products that affect personal safety (cranes, elevators, pressure vessels, the safeguarding of robots) must also satisfy regulations, being designed, inspected and certified to the standards the regulations specify.

The design data in this book follow the current Chinese national standards, and the corresponding ISO standards are given alongside; the programs in the book read digitized standard tables, which are updated centrally whenever a standard is revised.

**Engineer's notes**

- A safety factor is not "the bigger the better": it is paid for in weight and cost. Robot arms and aircraft structures rely on accurate load and material data, and can use very small safety factors.
- When you state a safety factor, say clearly "on which strength and under which load": on yield or on fatigue, at rated or at peak load, with or without stress concentration. Safety factors defined differently cannot be compared directly.
- Standards have editions. When citing a standard, give its year (e.g. GB/T 1800.1-2020); the old and new editions may give different values.
