Machine design is the work of turning requirements into a machine that can be made, stands up to use and is worth its price. A machine is made up of assemblies and parts, and the bill of materials records this hierarchy; a mechanism is concerned only with motion, while a machine must also transmit energy and carry loads. The WQR-105 reducer brings the motor speed down to about one tenth and multiplies the torque nearly tenfold, losing a little power at each gear stage and each bearing; the torque on each shaft follows from $T = 9550P/n$ and is the starting point of all later strength calculations.

A design passes through seven stages (requirements, concept, detail design, verification, release, manufacture, service), each ending with a design review; changes after release go through engineering change. Today design data flow in a single stream between CAD, CAE, PLM, ERP, MES and QMS, so that all the data on a part, from design to inspection, are linked together. Standard parts are chosen from the parts library.

A part is subject to many constraints at once: function, strength, stiffness, life, cost, manufacture, safety and regulations. Constraints that must be met mark out the feasible range, and "better is better" constraints compare merits within it. When constraints conflict, one either relaxes a constraint, changes the material or the structure, or goes back to the requirements; the price of each path must be worked out and compared.

The safety factor guards against the scatter of strength and load, model error and the consequences of failure. If strength and stress are both treated as random variables, the safety factor is linked to reliability: for the same safety factor, the larger the scatter, the lower the reliability. Standards unify dimensions, methods and inspection: GB/T standards mostly agree with ISO, and ASME, DIN and others are important national codes.

A calculation sheet must let others check it: given, find, formula, substitution, result, units, source, check, conclusion and assumptions, with nothing left out; units must be consistent, and there must be no more significant figures than the accuracy of the inputs allows. Mechanical engineering runs from the technical rules of the *Kaogong Ji* and Su Song's water-driven clock tower, through the standards, fatigue research, theory of mechanisms and lubrication theory that followed the Industrial Revolution, to finite elements and CAD. Accidents such as the Comet airliner, the Hyatt Regency walkways and the Tacoma Narrows Bridge teach us that failure often occurs where the design did not look, and that designers must hold the safety of the public paramount.

### Historical notes

"Machine design" took shape as a course in the German technical universities of the 19th century. F. Reuleaux taught machine construction at the Berlin Gewerbeakademie, and his *Theoretical Kinematics* (1875) and *Der Konstrukteur* (a designer's handbook) organized the design of mechanisms and machine elements into a systematic body of knowledge; C. von Bach in Stuttgart, in his *Die Maschinenelemente* (many editions from the end of the 19th century), taught the strength of machine elements on the basis of tests. The American textbooks of the 20th century, of which J. E. Shigley's *Mechanical Engineering Design* (first edition 1963, now in its 12th edition) is the most widely used, brought strength of materials, fatigue and the design of machine elements together and gradually added computer methods. In China the two courses "Theory of Machines" and "Machine Design" were set up in the 1950s on the model of the Soviet curriculum, and the textbooks of the various universities have been revised again and again since. Today textbooks outside China place more and more weight on the design process, computer tools and project training, and this book aims in the same direction.
