### 1.1.1 The problem

The WenQuest digital factory makes one product: the WQR-105 two-stage cylindrical gear reducer. It is fitted to belt conveyors, mixers, cranes and similar equipment, where it reduces the motor speed of somewhat over a thousand revolutions per minute and multiplies the torque at the same time. A newly hired engineer who is handed its drawings and bill of materials must first work out: what is this machine made of? How does the power pass from stage to stage? What is the torque on each shaft? These numbers are the starting point of every design calculation that follows.

### 1.1.2 Machines, mechanisms, assemblies and parts

First, let us distinguish a few terms.

A **machine** is a device, made up of a number of links with definite relative motions, that transmits energy or does useful work. A motor plus a reducer plus a conveyor belt is a conveyor; the reducer on its own is also a machine, converting the rotation that the motor produces from electrical energy into a different speed and torque.

A **mechanism** is concerned only with motion: it is a system of links connected in a definite way that converts one motion into another. The WQR-105 contains two gear mechanisms. In the first stage the input gear shaft meshes with a large gear; in the second a pinion meshes with the large output gear. When we study a mechanism we care only about geometry and motion (Part III), not about the material it is made of or the forces it can carry; when we study a machine we must care about all of these.

A **link** (member) is a unit that moves as one body in a mechanism; a **part** is a unit of manufacture. One link may consist of several parts: the intermediate shaft, the two gears on it and the two parallel keys are each a part, but fitted together they turn as one body and form one link.

An **assembly** is a unit in which a number of parts are grouped for assembly and management. The bill of materials of the WQR-105 divides it into four assemblies: the housing assembly, the input shaft assembly, the intermediate shaft assembly and the output shaft assembly (Figure 1.1.1). Each assembly can be put together and inspected on its own and then joined to the others in final assembly.

The **bill of materials** (BOM) writes this hierarchy out as a table: what each item is, how many are used, and whether it is made in-house or purchased. According to the digital factory's BOM, the WQR-105 has {{ex1_1.n_sub}} assemblies, {{ex1_1.n_make}} kinds of in-house parts (three shafts, three gears, the housing base and the cover) and {{ex1_1.n_buy}} kinds of purchased parts (bearings, parallel keys, end caps, oil seals, bolts, pins, oil gauge, and so on); counting in-house and purchased parts by the piece, there are {{ex1_1.n_pieces}} parts in all. Purchased parts far outnumber in-house parts, and this is normal for mechanical products: the designer draws and machines only a few key parts, and all the rest are chosen from standard parts and the products of specialized manufacturers (the parts library, Section 1.3).

### 1.1.3 How the power is transmitted

The motor drives the input gear shaft at $n_1$ = {{ex1_1.n1:.0f}} r/min (number of teeth $z_1$ = {{ex1_1.z1}}, module {{ex1_1.m1}} mm), which drives the large gear on the intermediate shaft ($z_2$ = {{ex1_1.z2}}); the pinion on the intermediate shaft ($z_3$ = {{ex1_1.z3}}, module {{ex1_1.m2}} mm) in turn drives the large gear on the output shaft ($z_4$ = {{ex1_1.z4}}). The speeds of a gear pair are inversely proportional to their numbers of teeth, so the ratios of the two stages and the overall ratio are

$$
i_1 = \frac{z_2}{z_1}, \qquad i_2 = \frac{z_4}{z_3}, \qquad i = i_1 i_2 . \tag{1.1.1}
$$

Some power is lost on the way: about 3% in each gear mesh and about 1% in each pair of rolling bearings. Let the input power from the motor be $P$; the torque on each shaft follows from its power and speed:

$$
T = 9550\,\frac{P}{n} \qquad (T\ \text{in N·m},\ P\ \text{in kW},\ n\ \text{in r/min}) . \tag{1.1.2}
$$

The 9550 comes from converting $T = P/\omega$ with $\omega = 2\pi n/60$: $60 \times 1000 / (2\pi) \approx 9549$.

**Example 1.1.1** The standard operating condition in the digital factory's parameter configurator is an input of {{ex1_1.P_in:.0f}} kW at {{ex1_1.n1:.0f}} r/min with an application factor $K_\mathrm A$ = {{ex1_1.KA:.2f}} (moderate shock). Find the speed, power and torque of each shaft.

From Eq. (1.1.1), $i_1$ = {{ex1_1.z2}}/{{ex1_1.z1}} = {{ex1_1.i1:.1f}} and $i_2$ = {{ex1_1.z4}}/{{ex1_1.z3}} = {{ex1_1.i2:.1f}}, so the overall ratio is $i$ = {{ex1_1.i:.1f}}. The intermediate shaft turns at {{ex1_1.n2:.1f}} r/min and the output shaft at {{ex1_1.n3:.1f}} r/min.

The input shaft delivers {{ex1_1.P1:.2f}} kW through one pair of bearings; each further gear stage with its pair of bearings multiplies the power once more by 0.97 × 0.99, giving {{ex1_1.P2:.2f}} kW at the intermediate shaft and {{ex1_1.P3:.2f}} kW at the output shaft. The overall efficiency is {{ex1_1.eta:.3f}}, so {{ex1_1.loss_w:.0f}} W turns into heat (which the housing has to dissipate; for the principle of heat balance see Section 30.5 on worm drives, and for the check of the reducer see Chapter 50). By Eq. (1.1.2), the torques on the three shafts are {{ex1_1.T1:.1f}}, {{ex1_1.T2:.1f}} and {{ex1_1.T3:.1f}} N·m (Figure 1.1.2).

The speed falls to about one tenth and the torque is multiplied nearly tenfold, which is exactly what a reducer is for. Including the application factor, the output torque is {{ex1_1.T3:.0f}} × {{ex1_1.KA:.2f}} = {{ex1_1.T3ka:.0f}} N·m, less than the rated value of {{ex1_1.T_rated:.0f}} N·m. How was the rating set? It is limited by the bearing (crushing) strength of the parallel key on the output shaft (Chapter 25), and Chapter 33 checks it again against the fatigue strength of the shaft.

### 1.1.4 Why the parts look the way they do

Open the reducer and you will see that no part has its shape by accident:

- the input gear is very small (24 teeth, module 2), so it is made in one piece with its shaft (a gear shaft); otherwise there would be no room for a bore and a keyway;
- the output shaft is a stepped shaft, thick in the middle and thin at both ends, because the gear, bearings and coupling have to be slid on in turn from the two ends (Chapter 33);
- the housing is split into a base and a cover along the plane of the shaft axes, and the bearing bores must be bored with the two halves together to keep the three shafts parallel;
- the large gears are forgings, the pinions are of carburizing steel and the housing is of gray cast iron: the material is decided by the loads, the machining and the cost together (Chapter 5).

Most of the chapters in this book are an answer to the question "why does this part look the way it does?".

**Everyday example** A multi-speed bicycle is a "reducer" too, only working the other way round. With 44 teeth on the chainring and 11 on the rear sprocket the ratio is 11/44, so one turn of the pedals turns the rear wheel four times: speed is multiplied and torque reduced, which is why you ride fast on the flat. On a climb you shift to 22 teeth in front and 32 at the rear; the rear wheel turns more slowly and pedaling is easier. Eqs. (1.1.1) and (1.1.2) apply here just the same.

**Engineer's notes**

- To understand an unfamiliar machine, read the bill of materials first and the assembly drawing second: the bill of materials tells you "what is there", the assembly drawing tells you "how it is connected".
- Eq. (1.1.2) is the formula a mechanical engineer uses most often, and you should be able to use it in your head: 1 kW at 1000 r/min is about 9.5 N·m.
- The overall efficiency is the product of the stage efficiencies, not their sum. The more stages, the lower the efficiency and the more heat.
