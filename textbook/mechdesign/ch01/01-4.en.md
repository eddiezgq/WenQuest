### 1.4.1 The problem

If strength were the only concern, the thicker a shaft the better; if cost were the only concern, the thinner the better; if assembly were the only concern, its diameter would just have to match the other parts. Design is the search for a solution that satisfies many requirements at once. Where do these requirements come from? What do we do when they conflict?

### 1.4.2 Types of constraints

The design constraints on a part fall roughly into eight types:

| Constraint | The question it asks | Where this book covers it |
|---|---|---|
| Function | What action must it perform; what motion and force must it transmit? | Chapter 2, Part III |
| Strength | Will it break, or deform permanently? | Chapters 10, 11 |
| Stiffness | Is the deformation large enough to impair its work (meshing, accuracy, vibration)? | Chapter 9 |
| Life | How long will it last? Fatigue, wear, corrosion | Chapters 11, 12, 40 |
| Cost | What do material, machining, purchased parts, assembly and inspection cost in total? | Chapters 5, 6 |
| Manufacture | Can it be made? Can it be assembled? Can it be inspected? | Chapters 4, 6 |
| Safety | Will its failure injure anyone? Is there guarding? | Sections 1.5, 1.8 |
| Regulations | Which standards and regulations must it comply with? | Section 1.5 |

Constraints are of two kinds. Some must be satisfied (enough strength, it fits, it complies with the regulations), and a design that does not satisfy them is not a feasible design. Others are open-ended, where better is always better (low cost, light weight, long life), and are used to compare the merits of the feasible designs. The first kind marks out the feasible range; the second kind chooses within it.

### 1.4.3 The feasible range of a shaft

**Example 1.4.1** The extension at the right-hand end of the SH-301 output shaft carries the coupling that passes the torque of the reducer to the driven machine. What constraints act on its diameter d?

**Strength.** When the motor starts, the torque surges above its rated value. The torque records of the digital factory's run-in test bench show a starting peak of {{ex1_4.K_peak:.2f}} times the rated torque of {{ex1_4.T_R:.0f}} N·m, that is, {{ex1_4.T_peak:.0f}} N·m. The extension has a keyway and is calculated on the section with the keyway; it must not yield under the peak, with a safety factor of at least {{ex1_4.S_req:.1f}}. The shear yield strength of quenched and tempered 45 steel is {{ex1_4.tau_s:.0f}} MPa, which gives $d \ge$ {{ex1_4.d_strength:.1f}} mm.

**Coupling.** We choose [[part:A-CPL-JAW]] from the parts library; selecting by a nominal torque of not less than {{ex1_4.T_R:.0f}} N·m gives size {{ex1_4.cpl}} (the nominal torque with the type a elastomer is {{ex1_4.cpl_T:.0f}} N·m, exactly what is required, with no margin; the starting peak must still be checked against the maximum torque in the catalog, Chapter 37). Its bore comes only in a few sizes between {{ex1_4.cpl_lo:.0f}} and {{ex1_4.cpl_hi:.0f}} mm (25, 28, 30, 32, ...).

**Geometry.** To the left of the extension is the bearing seat carrying a 6207 bearing ([[part:A-BRG-DG/6207]]) with a bore of {{ex1_4.brg_d:.0f}} mm. The extension is thinner than the bearing seat, and a locating shoulder must be left between them, with a shoulder height of at least 0.07d (Chapter 33), so $d + 2 \times 0.07d \le$ {{ex1_4.brg_d:.0f}} and $d \le$ {{ex1_4.d_geom:.1f}} mm.

**Standard diameters.** Shaft diameters are preferably taken from the standard size series (the R40 series of GB/T 2822; cylindrical shaft ends carrying couplings follow GB/T 1569 in addition), for which cutting tools, gauges and coupling bores are all prepared.

When these constraints are plotted on a number line (Figure 1.4.1), strength gives a lower limit, the bearing and shoulder give an upper limit, and the coupling gives a range. The feasible region is only {{ex1_4.lo:.1f}}–{{ex1_4.hi:.1f}} mm, {{ex1_4.width:.1f}} mm wide, and contains just one standard diameter, Ø{{ex1_4.d_pick}}, with a safety factor of {{ex1_4.S_pick:.2f}}.

### 1.4.4 When constraints conflict

So narrow a feasible region means the design is "tight": make any one condition a little stricter (for example, take fatigue into account, since the stress changes once every revolution of the shaft, or let the customer ask for more torque) and the feasible region vanishes. That is exactly what happens in the calculation of Chapter 33: a Ø30 extension in 45 steel does not reach the required fatigue safety factor.

When constraints conflict, the engineer has several ways out:

1. **Relax a constraint.** Choose a bearing one size larger (6208, bore 40) and the upper limit moves up; the price is that the bearing bores of the housing and the end caps must change.
2. **Change the material or process.** Using quenched and tempered 40Cr raises the strength without changing the size; the price is a somewhat more expensive material (the path finally chosen in Chapter 33).
3. **Change the structure.** Replace the parallel key with a spline or a keyless locking assembly, removing the keyway as a weak point; the price is the extra cost of machining and purchased parts.
4. **Go back to the requirements.** Talk to the customer about lowering the rated torque or changing the duty.

Every path has a price, and the choice is made by working out the prices and comparing them.

**Everyday example** Buying a pair of running shoes: the size must fit and the budget must not be exceeded. These are constraints that must be met, and they mark out the range you can choose from. Within that range you compare which pair is lighter, more durable or better looking; these are "better is better" constraints. If every pair that fits is over budget, you must relax a constraint: raise the budget, or try another brand. This is the engineering "trade-off"; Chapter 2 covers the systematic methods, and Chapter 39 shows how optimization algorithms find the best solution automatically.

**Engineer's notes**

- First find the constraints that "must be met" and draw the feasible region, then compare merits within it. Do it the other way around, and you often end up optimizing a design that cannot be made.
- Many constraints come from others: the bore of the bearing, the bore of the coupling, the sizes of cutting tools, the equipment in the shop. To design one part, you must first look carefully at the parts around it and at the conditions of the factory.
- Take special care with a design whose feasible region is narrow: a slight change in material or load may push it outside the constraints. It is then often worth changing the structure to gain a larger margin.
