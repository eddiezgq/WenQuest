### 1.2.1 The problem

When a student does homework, the problem is given, and once the answer is worked out the job is done. An engineer's design is not like that: the problem has to be distilled from what the customer says, and the answer has to be something that can be made, sold and stand up to use; and when problems show up in service, the engineer must go back and change it. What stages does a design pass through from beginning to end? What does each stage deliver? Who decides whether it may go on to the next stage?

### 1.2.2 Seven stages

Textbooks and companies divide the process differently ([UE] has six stages, [PB] four). This book divides it into seven stages according to what is actually produced in engineering practice (Figure 1.2.1):

1. **Requirements.** Turn what the customer says into specifications that can be checked, and write them into a **design specification**. "A sturdy reducer" is not a specification; "output torque 350 N·m (application factor 1.25 included), output speed about 138 r/min, bearing life 10 000 h, overall dimensions no more than ..., unit price no more than ..." is. The digital factory's configurator is a design specification written as a program: the customer chooses the ratio, power, speed and duty, the configurator at once checks the torque, the bearing life and the strength of the key, and refuses to quote if any of them fails.
2. **Concept.** Decide by what principle and with what structure the requirements will be met. A reducer may use cylindrical gears, a worm, planetary gears or a harmonic drive; it may have one stage or two, in an expanded (offset) or a coaxial layout. The concept stage puts forward several concepts, compares them against the specifications and chooses one (Chapter 2).
3. **Detail design.** Fix the shape, dimensions, material, tolerances and technical requirements of every part, produce the drawings (3D models and engineering drawings) and write the calculation sheets. This is the largest part of this book.
4. **Verification.** Show by calculation, simulation and test that the design meets the specification. Computing strength by finite elements, computing life with fatigue software and running in a prototype on a test bench are all forms of verification.
5. **Release.** After review and approval the design is formally handed over to production. Once released, the drawings and the bill of materials carry a revision number, and purchasing, process planning and the shop floor all work to that revision.
6. **Manufacture.** Machine, assemble and inspect according to the released drawings and process plans.
7. **Service.** Installation, maintenance, spare parts and field data once the product is in the customer's hands. Problems exposed in the field return to design.

### 1.2.3 Stage reviews and engineering changes

Every stage ends with a **design review**: people other than the designer (colleagues, process planning, quality, purchasing, customer representatives) check the work item by item against the specification; if it passes it goes on to the next stage, and if not it is sent back. The point of reviews is that the earlier a problem is found, the cheaper it is to fix. Changing a fillet on a drawing takes a few minutes; changing it after the part is made means scrapping a batch of blanks; changing it after the product is sold means a recall.

What if a problem is found after the product has been released? It goes through an **engineering change**: a change request states what is to be changed, why, and which items and documents are affected; after review and approval a new revision is released. The SH-301 output shaft of Chapter 33 is a complete example. Revision A, currently in use in the digital factory, is a teaching model, and the review finds that the left bearing seat is shorter than the bearing, that the steps have no fillets, and that the key on the extension cannot carry the load. The engineer redesigns it as revision B by the methods of shaft design and at the same time lists the affected items (the gear becomes a forging with a hub, the key and the oil seal change size, the inspection template is revised), and after review it is released.

### 1.2.4 Design is iterative

Figure 1.2.1 is drawn as a line from left to right, but a real design seldom goes straight through. Failed verification sends it back to detail design; a concept found unworkable in detail design sends it back to the concept stage, or even back to the requirements to renegotiate the specifications with the customer. A good design process does not avoid iteration; it makes iteration happen early and cheaply: think of several structures at the concept stage, estimate with simple formulas before computing precisely with finite elements in detail design, and make a sample part before manufacture.

**Everyday example** Renovating an apartment goes through the same stages: say how many rooms you want and what the budget is (requirements), compare a few layouts (concept), draw the working drawings and list the materials (detail design), look at the renderings and revise a few times (verification), sign off on the final version (release), carry out the work (manufacture), and do repairs after moving in (service). Moving an outlet halfway through the work is an engineering change, and the later the change, the more it costs.

**Engineer's notes**

- Every specification in a design specification must be checkable: it needs a value, a unit and a test method. A specification for which no test method can be written is as good as none.
- The most useful question in a review is "what happens if this assumption does not hold?", not "did you calculate it right?".
- An engineering change is more than changing a drawing: when one part changes, its items, process, inspection, stock and the work in progress already made must all be dealt with too.
