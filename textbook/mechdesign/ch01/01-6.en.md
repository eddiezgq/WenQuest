### 1.6.1 The problem

An engineer finishes the calculation of a shaft and writes on a scrap of paper "S = {{ex1_6.S_long}}, OK". Three months later a customer complains that the shaft has broken, and the quality department pulls out his calculation: what was T? Was it the rated torque or the peak? Where was the material strength taken from? Was the keyway included? Is the 1.7 based on yield or on fatigue? Nobody can say, not even the engineer himself. A design calculation must be written as a document that others can check and that its author can still understand later. That document is the **engineering calculation sheet**.

### 1.6.2 What goes into a calculation sheet

A proper calculation sheet contains, in order:

1. **Heading.** Part number, revision, the task sheet or change order it belongs to, author and date: this says "which revision of the design this calculation is for".
2. **Given.** The value, unit and source of every input quantity (specification, configurator, standard, test record, previous calculation step).
3. **Find.** The question to be answered.
4. **Calculation.** For every step, the formula, the substituted values, the result and its unit; for every factor looked up or quoted, its source (standard number and table number, or book title and page).
5. **Check.** Compare the result with the requirement, stating the criterion (for example $S \ge 1.5$) and its source.
6. **Conclusion.** Satisfied or not; if not, what to do.
7. **Assumptions and remarks.** What the calculation leaves out (for example "static strength only; for fatigue see separate sheet"), and under what conditions these assumptions would no longer hold.

The programs of this book record their calculations with a single calculation-sheet module (`conventions/calcsheet.py`): it supplies the numbers in the worked examples to the text, and the same record can be exported as a Word calculation sheet. The blank calculation sheets handed out with the engineering task sheets are generated by it too, with the formulas and sources already filled in and the results left for the student.

### 1.6.3 Units

Unit errors are among the most common mistakes in machine design and among those with the most serious consequences. This book uses the International System of Units (ISO 80000) throughout, following the usual conventions of mechanical engineering: lengths in mm, forces in N, stresses in MPa (= N/mm²) and moments in N·m, with N·m converted to N·mm when stresses are calculated. The step "stress = moment / section modulus" is then the easiest place to go wrong: forgetting to multiply the moment by 1000.

**Example 1.6.2** If the peak torque of {{ex1_6.T_max:.1f}} N·m in Example 1.6.1 is substituted directly as if it were in N·mm, the shear stress comes out as {{ex1_6.tau_wrong:.3f}} MPa, a thousand times too small; conversely, if the diameter were set by this mistaken calculation, the minimum diameter required for strength would go from {{ex1_6.d_strength:.1f}} mm to {{ex1_6.d_wrong:.2f}} mm, a "shaft" as thin as a pencil lead. A result as absurd as this is obvious at a glance; the dangerous errors are those of a factor of 2, π or √3, which look very much like the right answer.

**Everyday example** Following a recipe, if you misread "5 g of salt" as "5 spoonfuls of salt", the dish is inedible. A unit error in an engineering calculation is the same, only with far more serious consequences.

How to prevent unit errors: state the units of every formula; write the units along with the numbers when substituting, and check that the dimensions of both sides agree; and use common sense to estimate the order of magnitude of the result (for a Ø30 steel shaft under a few hundred N·m of torque, the stress should be anywhere from a few tens to a little over a hundred MPa). The history of spaceflight has one very costly example: in 1999 NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter flew too low and was destroyed on entering the Martian atmosphere, because ground software output thruster impulse in US customary units (pound-force seconds) while the navigation software read it in SI units (newton seconds).

### 1.6.4 Significant figures and conclusions

The program computes the safety factor as {{ex1_6.S_long}}..., but the calculation sheet needs only {{ex1_6.S:.2f}}. The reason is that the inputs themselves are not that accurate: the yield strength has only two or three significant figures, and the load scatters by ten percent. The result should not have more significant figures than the least accurate input; extra digits do not make it more accurate, they only mislead the reader into thinking that it is. In general design calculations keep three significant figures, and give safety factors to two decimal places.

The conclusion must be explicit: write "satisfied" or "not satisfied", and if not satisfied, state the measures to be taken. Do not just write a number and leave the reader to judge.

---

**Engineer's notes**

- A calculation sheet is written for others to read: assume your reader is a colleague who takes over this part six months from now and was not in on the discussions.
- Give the source of every number you look up. In a review, a number without a source is taken to be "a guess".
- When a program does the calculation, the program itself is part of the calculation sheet: its version, input files and output results must be archived together with the sheet.
