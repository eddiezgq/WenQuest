Picture a reducer on the bench in front of you: a cast-iron housing, a few shafts, a few gears, a few pairs of bearings, some screws and oil seals. It looks ordinary, yet between the moment a customer says "I need a reducer with an output torque of 350 N·m" and the moment it turns on the customer's conveyor lie the work of dozens of engineers and over a thousand decisions. Why each shaft has this diameter, why each fillet has this radius, why each material was chosen: every one of these must have a reason, must be written down so that others can check it, and must be something the factory can make and inspect. That is machine design.

This chapter answers three questions: what machine design does, how engineers work, and how this book teaches. Section 1.1 starts from the WQR-105 reducer of the digital factory, looking at the assemblies and parts a machine is made of and at how power passes from the motor to the output shaft. Section 1.2 describes the whole course of a design from requirements to service. Section 1.3 covers the tools engineers use every day (CAD, CAE, PLM, ERP, MES, QMS) and how a single flow of data ties them together. Section 1.4 deals with the constraints on a design and the trade-offs between them. Section 1.5 explains where the safety factor comes from, how it relates to reliability, and what the standards of different countries cover. Section 1.6 shows how to write an engineering calculation sheet. Section 1.7 looks back at the history of mechanical engineering, Section 1.8 discusses engineering ethics and the designer's responsibility, and Section 1.9 is a roadmap of the whole book.

The engineering task sheet of this chapter, TS-1-1, takes you into the class factory for the first time: you pick up a task sheet for "SH-301 keyway revision", change the design, submit it for review, release it, and watch it being machined and inspected. Every later chapter works the same way.

After studying this chapter the reader should be able to:

1. tell the difference between a machine, a mechanism, an assembly and a part, read a bill of materials, and compute the speed, power and torque of each shaft in a multistage drive;
2. name the seven stages of design and the output of each, and explain the role of stage reviews and engineering changes;
3. state what CAD, CAE, PLM, ERP, MES and QMS each look after, and how a design change passes between them;
4. list the design constraints on a part, find the feasible range, and explain how to proceed when constraints conflict;
5. explain the relation between safety factor and reliability, estimate the safety factor needed from the scatter of load and strength, and describe how the common systems of standards divide the work;
6. write a one-page engineering calculation sheet in the standard form: given, find, formula, substitution, result, units, source, conclusion;
7. cite several milestones in the history of mechanical engineering and several typical accidents, and explain the designer's responsibility.
