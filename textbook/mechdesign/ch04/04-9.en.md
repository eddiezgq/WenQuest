### 4.9.1 Measuring instruments and methods

Every requirement on a drawing must be inspectable. The common means of inspection are:

- **General-purpose measuring instruments**: vernier calipers (scale interval 0.02 mm), external micrometers (0.001 mm), bore gauges, dial indicators (0.01 mm and 0.001 mm), height gauges. They give actual values and are flexible, but slow and dependent on the inspector.
- **Special gauges**: plain limit gauges (GO and NOT GO gauges, Taylor's principle of Section 4.6), thread gauges, functional gauges (Section 4.6). They give no reading, only a decision of conforming or not; they are fast and suit large batches, but they do not show how far a part is from the tolerance limit and cannot be used to analyze a process.
- **Coordinate measuring machine** (CMM): the probe takes points on the surface of the part, the software sets up the coordinate system from the datums on the drawing, fits circles, planes and cylinders, and calculates sizes and geometrical errors (the least-squares and minimum zone evaluations of Section 4.4 are calculated here). All characteristics are measured in one setup, and the data go straight into the quality system. The inspection station qc-01 of the digital factory is a coordinate measuring machine, which measures {{ex4_9.n_chars}} characteristics on every SH-301.
- **Special instruments**: roundness testers, surface roughness testers (Section 4.7), gear measuring centers (gear accuracy is covered in Chapter 29).

Coordinate measuring machines are accepted according to ISO 10360 (Chinese GB/T 16857). The main specification is the **maximum permissible error** of length measurement $MPE_E$, written in the form $(A + L/K)$ μm, where L is the measured length (mm). An ordinary workshop machine is of the order of (1.8 + L/300) μm (the specification this book sets for the measuring machine of the digital factory).

### 4.9.2 Measurement uncertainty and conformity decisions

If a measured diameter is exactly 1 μm below the upper limit, does the shaft conform? Measurement has errors of its own: the indication error of the measuring machine, the scatter of repeated measurements, temperature (a workpiece not at 20 °C expands or contracts), the probe, the fixturing, and so on. Together these are expressed as the **measurement uncertainty**, the "credible range" of a measurement result (according to the GUM, that is, ISO/IEC Guide 98-3).

**Example 4.9.1** Diameter measurement of the Ø35 bearing seat, with three main components:

| Source | Method of estimation | Standard uncertainty / μm |
|---|---|---|
| Indication error of the measuring machine | $MPE_E$ = {{ex4_9.mpe:.2f}} μm, divided by √3 for a uniform distribution | {{ex4_9.u_mpe:.2f}} |
| Repeatability | Standard deviation of repeated measurements (measurement system analysis) | {{ex4_9.u_rep:.2f}} |
| Temperature | Workpiece at 22 °C corrected to 20 °C; standard uncertainty of the thermometer taken as 0.5 K, uncertainty of the expansion coefficient 1×10⁻⁶/K | {{ex4_9.u_temp:.2f}} |
| Combined | Root sum of squares | {{ex4_9.u_c:.2f}} |

The expanded uncertainty is $U = k u_c$ = 2 × {{ex4_9.u_c:.2f}} = {{ex4_9.U:.2f}} μm (k = 2, a confidence level of about 95%), rounded up to {{ex4_9.U_um:.1f}} μm for the decision. The ratio of the tolerance, {{ex4_9.tol35_um:.0f}} μm, to U is {{ex4_9.TU:.1f}}. The usual rule of thumb is that U should not exceed a tenth to a fifth of the tolerance, so this machine is only "just adequate" for measuring an IT6 journal.

ISO 14253-1 (GB/T 18779.1) specifies that **to prove conformity, the supplier must show a measured value within the "acceptance zone", the tolerance zone reduced by U at each side;** to prove nonconformity, the customer must show a measured value more than U outside the tolerance zone. The acceptance zone of Ø35k6 is {{ex4_9.acc_lo:.4f}}–{{ex4_9.acc_hi:.4f}} mm. A measured value within U on either side of a tolerance limit lies in the uncertainty range, and neither party can prove anything: either the part is remeasured by a more accurate method, or the rule is agreed beforehand in the contract.

### 4.9.3 Process capability

Inspection is not only about picking out nonconforming parts; more important is whether the process is "capable" of making conforming parts consistently. The **process capability indices** are:

$$C_p = \frac{USL - LSL}{6\hat\sigma}, \qquad C_{pk} = \min\left(\frac{USL - \bar x}{3\hat\sigma},\ \frac{\bar x - LSL}{3\hat\sigma}\right)$$

$C_p$ looks only at the spread; $C_{pk}$ also looks at whether the process is centered. $\hat\sigma$ is estimated from the within-subgroup (short-term) standard deviation (calculated from the moving range of successive parts) and reflects the potential of the process itself; calculated with the overall standard deviation of all the data, the indices are called $P_p$ and $P_{pk}$ (process performance) and include the drift of the process over time. $C_{pk}$ ≥ 1.33 is generally required. Characteristics with only an upper limit, such as run-out and symmetry, use the one-sided $C_{pu} = (USL - \bar x)/(3\hat\sigma)$.

**Example 4.9.2** Program 4.9.1 calls the machining error model of the digital factory (the same model as the simulation workshop), simulates the grinding machine grinding {{ex4_9.N}} SH-301 shafts in succession (three wheel-dressing cycles), measures {{ex4_9.n_chars}} characteristics on each, and calculates the process capability with the same function as the quality module of the digital factory:

| Characteristic | Tolerance | $C_p$ | $C_{pk}$ | $P_{pk}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Left bearing seat Ø35 k6 | {{ex4_9.lim_d35}} | {{ex4_9.Cp35:.2f}} | {{ex4_9.Cpk35:.2f}} | {{ex4_9.Ppk35:.2f}} |
| Right bearing seat Ø35 k6 | As above | {{ex4_9.Cp35r:.2f}} | {{ex4_9.Cpk35r:.2f}} | {{ex4_9.Ppk35r:.2f}} |
| Gear seat Ø40 k6 (the inspection template of the digital factory is still version A; it changes with the engineering change when version B goes to r6) | {{ex4_9.lim_d40}} | {{ex4_9.Cp40:.2f}} | {{ex4_9.Cpk40:.2f}} | {{ex4_9.Ppk40:.2f}} |
| Keyway width 12 N9 | {{ex4_9.lim_kw}} | {{ex4_9.Cpw:.2f}} | {{ex4_9.Cpkw:.2f}} | {{ex4_9.Ppkw:.2f}} |
| Radial circular run-out of bearing seat | ≤ {{ex4_9.lim_ro}} | — | {{ex4_9.Cpk_ro:.2f}} (one-sided) | {{ex4_9.Ppk_ro:.2f}} |
| Symmetry of keyway | ≤ {{ex4_9.lim_sy}} | — | {{ex4_9.Cpk_sy:.2f}} (one-sided) | {{ex4_9.Ppk_sy:.2f}} |

Look at the bearing seats: $C_{pk}$ = {{ex4_9.Cpk35:.2f}}, not far from 1.33, but $P_{pk}$ is only {{ex4_9.Ppk35:.2f}}, and the two bearing seats together have {{ex4_9.n_out35}} values above the upper limit (on the left seat they are parts {{ex4_9.out_pos}} of each dressing cycle). The reason is in Figure 4.9.1a: the wheel wears a little with every part, the diameter grows part by part and drifts by {{ex4_9.drift_um:.1f}} μm in one dressing cycle; adding ±3σ of random error, the total is {{ex4_9.spread_um:.1f}} μm, wider than the tolerance of {{ex4_9.tol35_um:.0f}} μm. The within-subgroup σ estimated from the moving range is only {{ex4_9.sw35:.1f}} μm, less than half the overall standard deviation of {{ex4_9.s35:.1f}} μm: successive parts differ very little, so the moving range cannot see a slow trend, and only the overall standard deviation reflects the true situation. **In a process with a systematic trend such as tool wear, looking only at $C_{pk}$ is misleading.** Strictly speaking, a process with a clear trend is not in a state of statistical control (the control-chart rule "8 consecutive points on the same side of the center line" would signal), and $C_{pk}$ should not be reported for it at all: bring the process under control first, then talk about capability. Run-out and symmetry are absolute values whose distributions are not normal, so $C_{pu}$ is only an approximation for them; the formal approach is the non-normal methods of ISO 22514.

The remedy has to deal with both ends. The mean diameter of the first part after dressing, {{ex4_9.base_now:.4f}}, minus 3σ is {{ex4_9.first_low:.4f}}, already below the lower acceptance limit. The starting point after dressing must therefore be raised to about {{ex4_9.base_new:.4f}}, and the drift in one cycle plus 6σ must not exceed the width of the acceptance zone, which allows at most {{ex4_9.n_dress}} parts per cycle (at present it is {{ex4_9.n_dress_now}}). Alternatively, the grinding machine can measure in process and compensate for wheel wear automatically. The digital factory treats the present state as its "normal state", which leaves the improvement to the reader.

By the rule of Section 4.9.2, of the {{ex4_9.n_meas}} measured values of the two bearing seats, {{ex4_9.n_conform}} lie in the acceptance zone and can be proved to conform, {{ex4_9.n_inzone}} lie in the uncertainty range, and {{ex4_9.n_out35}} exceed the tolerance.

**Finding the cause from the data**: in teaching mode, the teacher injects "dressing interval too long" into the class factory, and the data from the same grinding machine become those of Figure 4.9.1b. The slope of the drift becomes about 2.5 times as large (in the model the dressing cycle does not change; the effect appears as larger grinding forces and more deflection once the wheel is dull, so the diameter grows faster). From part {{ex4_9.first_out}} on the parts are out of tolerance; of the {{ex4_9.N}} parts, {{ex4_9.n_out_bad}} exceed the upper limit, and $C_{pk}$ = {{ex4_9.Cpk_bad:.2f}}. The features of the data (a sawtooth pattern, a drop back after each dressing, a steeper slope) point directly to wheel wear, not to tailstock offset (a systematic difference between the left and right bearing seats) or lack of warm-up (parts getting smaller one by one after start-up). ### 4.9.4 Inspection plans

An inspection plan (control plan) specifies for each characteristic at which operation it is checked, with what instrument, how many parts are sampled, how often, and what to do when it goes out of control. For SH-301:

| Characteristic | Class | Instrument | Frequency | When out of control |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bearing seats Ø35 k6, circular run-out | Critical | Coordinate measuring machine; micrometer at the machine | Every part (CMM); 1 in 5 (at the machine) | Stop the machine, check the wheel and tailstock; quarantine the parts made since the last good check |
| Gear seat Ø40 r6 (version B) | Critical | Coordinate measuring machine | Every part | As above |
| Keyway width 12 N9, symmetry | Major | Keyway gauge, symmetry fixture | First part + every 20 parts | Check the locating elements of the fixture |
| Other dimensions | Minor | Caliper | First part + sampling of each batch | Rework or scrap |

The data of critical characteristics go into the quality system and are plotted on control charts (see the chapters on statistical process control in the companion volume *Manufacturing Technology*). For a part out of tolerance a **nonconformance report** is raised, and the quality engineer decides the disposition: **rework** (an oversize bearing seat can be ground again), **scrap** (an undersize bearing seat cannot be saved) or **concession** (the deviation is very small and design has confirmed that it does not affect the function; an approval record is required). When the same cause occurs repeatedly, a quality problem report is raised, the root cause is found by the 8D method, and corrective action is taken and verified by data (the "Quality" page of the digital factory).

**Engineer's notes**

- Choose the measuring instrument from the tolerance: its uncertainty should not exceed a tenth to a fifth of the tolerance. If it does, change the instrument, or use the tolerance zone reduced by the uncertainty as the acceptance limits.
- Data are more useful than a pass/fail decision: a gauge tells only "goes" or "does not go", whereas readings show trends, so that problems can be found before any scrap is made.
- Inspectors and measuring systems must be "inspected" too: measurement system analysis (GRR) shows what fraction of the tolerance is taken up by repeatability and reproducibility, and the quality module of the digital factory has a ready-made calculation.
