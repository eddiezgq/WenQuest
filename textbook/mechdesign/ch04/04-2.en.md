### 4.2.1 Size, deviation and tolerance

The size written on a part drawing is the **nominal size**, such as Ø35 for the bearing seat of SH-301. The size actually made and measured is the actual size. The largest and smallest permitted sizes are the upper and lower limits of size; subtracting the nominal size from them gives the **upper limit deviation** (es for a shaft, ES for a hole) and the **lower limit deviation** (ei, EI), together called the **limit deviations**. The difference between the two limits of size is the **size tolerance**:

$$T = \text{upper limit of size} - \text{lower limit of size} = es - ei$$

A tolerance is always positive; a deviation may be positive or negative. If the nominal size is drawn as a horizontal line (the **zero line**) and the deviations are drawn to scale above and below it, the region between the two limit deviations is the **tolerance zone** (Figure 4.2.1, right). A tolerance zone has two attributes: its size (the tolerance T) and its position (how far it is from the zero line). The national standard GB/T 1800.1 (corresponding to ISO 286-1) standardizes the two separately: the size by the **standard tolerance**, and the position by the **fundamental deviation**.

### 4.2.2 Standard tolerances and tolerance grades

The **standard tolerances** are tabulated by size range and **tolerance grade**. The grades run from IT01 and IT0 through IT1 up to IT18; the larger the number, the larger the tolerance. Mating surfaces of machine parts commonly use IT5–IT8, and non-mating dimensions IT11–IT18 (coarser). Within one tolerance grade, all sizes are regarded as "equally hard to make": the tolerance of a large size is larger than that of a small size, because machine, tool and temperature errors are all larger when a large part is made.

Where does the table of standard tolerances come from? It is obtained by multiplying the **standard tolerance factor** $i$ by a grade coefficient. For sizes up to 500 mm:

$$i = 0.45\sqrt[3]{D} + 0.001D \quad(\mu\text{m}), \qquad \text{IT5} = 7i,\ \text{IT6} = 10i,\ \text{IT7} = 16i,\ \text{IT8} = 25i,\ \dots,\ \text{IT11} = 100i$$

where $D$ is the geometric mean of the two ends of the size range (mm). The first term reflects the growth of machining error with size, the second the growth of measurement error (mainly from temperature) with size. From IT6 on, the tolerance grows tenfold every 5 grades, and the grade coefficients follow the R5 preferred number series (1, 1.6, 2.5, 4, 6.3).

**Example 4.2.1** Ø35 belongs to the size range 30–50 mm, so $D = \sqrt{30 \times 50} = {{ex4_2.D35:.2f}}$ mm and $i = {{ex4_2.i35:.3f}}$ μm. By the formula IT6 is {{ex4_2.it6_35_f:.1f}} μm, and the standard table gives {{ex4_2.it6_35}} μm; IT7 is {{ex4_2.it7_35_f:.1f}} μm by the formula and {{ex4_2.it7_35}} μm in the table. The values in the table were calculated by the formula and then rounded, and a few retain historical values. **Design and inspection always use the table; the formula serves only to understand the pattern of the table.**

### 4.2.3 Fundamental deviations

The **fundamental deviation** is the limit deviation of the tolerance zone nearer the zero line, and it determines the position of the zone. The standard denotes it by letters: capitals for holes (A ... ZC), lowercase for shafts (a ... zc). For shafts, the tolerance zones of a–h lie below the zero line (the fundamental deviation is the upper limit deviation es), and h has es = 0; js is distributed symmetrically about the zero line, and j straddles it; from k on, the fundamental deviation is the lower limit deviation ei, the zones mostly lie above the zero line, and the later the letter, the farther from the zero line. Holes are roughly symmetrical to shafts of the same letter: H has EI = 0, and F and G lie above the zero line; K and M mostly straddle or lie close to the zero line, and from N on the zones lie below it.

A fundamental deviation combined with a tolerance grade gives a **tolerance class**, such as k6, H7 or f9. To look one up, first find the fundamental deviation, then calculate the other deviation with the standard tolerance.

### 4.2.4 Indication on drawings and general tolerances

A drawing may give the tolerance class only (Ø35k6), the limit deviations only (Ø35 +0.018/+0.002), or both (Ø35k6(+0.018/+0.002)). In series production with inspection by gauges, the class alone is often given; in one-off production with inspection by general-purpose measuring instruments, giving the deviations is more convenient, since the workshop need not look up the table. Dimensions without an individual tolerance are inspected to the general tolerances of GB/T 1804 (Section 3.3), with the class stated in the technical requirements, for example "general tolerances for linear dimensions to GB/T 1804-m".

**Engineer's notes**

- When looking up a table, watch the boundaries of the size ranges: 35 mm belongs to "over 30 up to and including 50", and so does 50 mm, which does not belong to 50–80.
- The later the letter (for shafts), the "fatter" the shaft: k, m and n may give interference, and p, r and s always give interference (with an H hole, Section 4.3).
- Upper and lower case in a tolerance class must not be confused: Ø35K6 is a hole and Ø35k6 a shaft, two entirely different things (the AI design reviewer of Chapter 3 checks this).
