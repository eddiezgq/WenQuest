### 4.6.1 How are size tolerances and geometrical tolerances related?

When a feature has both a size tolerance and a geometrical tolerance, does each control its own aspect independently, or are they related? Take the bearing seat Ø35k6: if its diameter is made at the lower limit {{ex4_2.k6_min:.3f}}, may it be allowed to bend a little? The rules that settle this relation are called **tolerancing principles**.

### 4.6.2 The independency principle

**Independency principle** (GB/T 4249, ISO 8015): the size tolerance and the geometrical tolerances given on the drawing are independent of each other and must be met separately. The size controls only the local size (the diameter measured between two points), and the form is controlled by geometrical tolerances. This is the default rule of the ISO system and of the Chinese standards: when no symbol is added on the drawing, the independency principle applies.

Its advantage is clarity: each requirement is inspected separately. The problem is that the form of a feature with only a size tolerance is almost unrestricted: a shaft whose diameter conforms everywhere may be bent so much that it will not go into its hole. Under the independency principle, therefore, wherever form must be controlled, a geometrical tolerance must be added, or the technical requirements must state that unspecified geometrical tolerances follow GB/T 1184. That standard specifies that the unspecified roundness tolerance equals the size tolerance of the diameter, but is not greater than the unspecified radial circular run-out tolerance.

### 4.6.3 The envelope requirement

For mating features, what matters most is that they "go together and keep the character of the fit". The **envelope requirement** (Ⓔ added after the size tolerance) specifies that the actual surface of the feature must not violate the **maximum material boundary**, a boundary of perfect form whose diameter equals the maximum material size.

The **maximum material condition** (MMC) is the condition in which the feature contains the most material: the upper limit of size for a shaft, the lower limit for a hole. Take Ø35k6 Ⓔ as an example: the actual surface of the shaft must lie wholly within a perfect cylinder of Ø{{ex4_2.k6_max:.3f}}, and at the same time the local diameter must nowhere be less than {{ex4_2.k6_min:.3f}}. If the diameter is made at the upper limit, the form must be perfect; if it is made at the lower limit, a form error (bending, taper) of up to one size tolerance ({{ex4_2.it6_35}} μm) is permitted. Whatever the combination, the shaft will then always enter a perfect hole of Ø{{ex4_2.k6_max:.3f}}, and the character of the fit is assured. It is inspected with a full-form GO gauge (a plain ring gauge whose bore equals the upper limit) passed over the whole journal, and a two-point NOT GO gauge (a snap gauge) that checks that the local diameter is not below the lower limit. This is the gauging principle put forward by W. Taylor in England in 1905 (Taylor's principle).

The default rule of the American standard ASME Y14.5 is exactly the opposite: features of size obey the envelope requirement by default ("Rule #1"), and no Ⓔ is needed. This point needs particular care when reading American and Chinese drawings.

### 4.6.4 The maximum material requirement

Many holes need only to "let the screw through"; they are not fits. The pillow block of the palletizing conveyor (Chapter 3, PB-6207) is fixed by two {{ex4_6.F:.0f}} mm screws (M10) threaded into tapped holes in the cross-member of the frame. The anchor-bolt holes in the base are Ø{{ex4_6.dh:.0f}} (GB/T 5277, medium series), with a size tolerance of H13 (+{{ex4_6.it13:.2f}}/0). The distance between the two hole centers, {{ex4_6.Lh:.0f}} mm, is a theoretically exact dimension, and each hole has a position tolerance.

Whether a screw will pass depends on two things, the size of the hole and its position: if the hole is made larger, it can be slightly out of position and the screw will still pass. By the usual formula for dividing the tolerance between the two parts, for a fixed fastener (a screw threaded into a tapped hole in one part and passing through a clearance hole in the other) the position tolerance of each part is $t = (H - F)/2 = (11 - 10)/2 = {{ex4_6.tpos:.1f}}$ mm (H being the maximum material size of the hole and F the diameter of the screw). Written in the frame as "position | Ø0.5 Ⓜ | A | B | C" (with Ⓜ after the tolerance value), this is the **maximum material requirement**:

- when the hole is at maximum material condition (Ø11.0), the position tolerance is Ø{{ex4_6.tpos:.1f}};
- when the hole is made larger, the position tolerance increases by as much as the hole departs from maximum material; this increase is called the **bonus tolerance**. When the hole is made at least material condition, Ø{{ex4_6.LMC:.2f}}, the position tolerance grows to Ø{{ex4_6.t_lmc:.2f}};
- however large the hole, its surface must not violate the **virtual condition boundary**: a perfect cylinder at the theoretically exact position whose diameter equals the **virtual condition size** (called the maximum material virtual size in the standard) = maximum material size − position tolerance = 11 − 0.5 = {{ex4_6.vc:.1f}} mm.

So long as the holes all clear the virtual condition boundary of Ø{{ex4_6.vc:.1f}}, and the position error of the tapped holes in the frame is within their own tolerance of Ø{{ex4_6.tpos:.1f}} (the largest "outer envelope" of the screw being 10 + 0.5 = {{ex4_6.vc:.1f}}), the screws will always pass. This is the starting point of design with the maximum material requirement: **the tolerances are derived directly from the condition for assembly, so that even the worst case can be assembled.** There is one precondition: the position tolerance of the tapped holes must control the length of the screw that projects out and passes through the base of the pillow block. The tapped holes in the frame should therefore carry a **projected tolerance zone** Ⓟ (with a projected length equal to the thickness of the base of the pillow block); otherwise, if a tapped hole is slightly tilted, the screw will be out of position at the height of the base.

**Functional gauge**: the virtual condition boundary can be made directly into a gauge. A base plate simulates datum A, a locating stop on each side simulates datums B and C, and two gauge pins of Ø{{ex4_6.vc:.1f}} are mounted on the plate at the theoretically exact positions ({{ex4_6.Lh:.0f}} mm apart). If the part, laid on A and pushed against B and C, fits over the pins, the position conforms (the size of the holes is checked separately with plug gauges). A single check judges the combined effect of the size and position of both holes at once, which is a simulation of assembly itself. If the function requires only that the screws can be fitted through the two holes, and does not care about their position relative to B and C, the frame can read just "Ø0.5 Ⓜ | A" (a pattern of features); the gauge then loses its B and C stops, the part may be shifted and turned on the plate to find a fit, and even more parts will be accepted.

### 4.6.5 Example: how many more parts are accepted

**Example 4.6.1** Program 4.6.1 simulates a batch of {{ex4_6.N}} pillow blocks. The hole diameters are normally distributed (mean Ø{{ex4_6.mu_d:.2f}}, since twist drills cut slightly oversize; holes outside H13 are first rejected by the size check), and the position errors of the holes are normally distributed in each of the two directions with a standard deviation of {{ex4_6.sd_pos:.2f}} mm (holes drilled on a machining center, a process capability assumed in this book). A part conforms only if both holes conform:

- by the independency principle (no Ⓜ, position tolerance always Ø0.5): {{ex4_6.p_rfs:.1f}}% conform;
- by the maximum material requirement: {{ex4_6.p_mmr:.1f}}% conform, {{ex4_6.n_extra}} more parts are accepted;
- the decision by the functional gauge and the calculation by the maximum material requirement are the same inequality (the surface of the hole clears the virtual condition boundary), so naturally the results are the same: the gauge is this inequality in physical form.

Can the {{ex4_6.n_extra}} extra parts be used? The program then assembles every part with a conforming frame (position error of the tapped holes within Ø0.5). In this assembly the pillow block is placed at its theoretical position without any adjustment, which is stricter than in practice, and {{ex4_6.asm_extra:.0f}}% of them assemble. Of all the parts accepted by the maximum material requirement, {{ex4_6.asm_mmr:.0f}}% assemble as well. Figure 4.6.1a is a "dynamic tolerance diagram": the horizontal axis is the actual diameter of the hole and the vertical axis the position error. The region accepted by the independency principle lies below the horizontal line, that accepted by the maximum material requirement below the sloping line, and the region between the two lines (orange) holds the extra parts accepted.

One more number deserves attention: the {{ex4_6.n_rej}} parts rejected by the maximum material requirement could in fact also be assembled with this batch of frames, because the errors of the frames did not happen to fall in the worst direction this time. A tolerance guarantees the worst case. That a rejected part "happens to fit" does not show that the tolerance is too tight; it shows only that the errors of this batch of frames are small.

### 4.6.6 Which to choose

| Principle | Indication | Where used |
|---|---|---|
| Independency principle | No symbol | Where size is unrelated to form and position requirements: sealing, appearance, accuracy of motion (the straightness of a guideway is unrelated to its width) |
| Envelope requirement | Ⓔ after the size tolerance | Holes and shafts whose character of fit must be assured: bearing seats, gear bores, locating pins |
| Maximum material requirement | Ⓜ after the geometrical tolerance value | Features that need only to assemble: bolt holes, cable holes, symmetry of a keyway (where only the key must go in) |

The maximum material requirement (GB/T 16671 / ISO 2692) must not be used where centering, load bearing or accuracy of motion is required: if a gear bore is made larger and out of position, the gear runs unevenly, and nothing compensates for it. There is also the **least material requirement** (Ⓛ), used to ensure a minimum wall thickness or edge distance; its principle is the mirror image of the maximum material requirement.

---

**Engineer's notes**

- For holes, pins and keyways that need only assemble, prefer to add Ⓜ to the geometrical tolerance: the workshop gets more parts accepted, assembly is unaffected, and functional gauges allow quick inspection.
- When Ⓜ is used, the virtual condition size must be written into the inspection documents, and functional gauges are designed to the virtual condition size (the manufacturing tolerance of the gauge itself is considered separately according to GB/T 8069).
- For a foreign drawing, first look at whether the title block refers to ISO 8015 or ASME Y14.5; their default rules differ.
