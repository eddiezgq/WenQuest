### 4.1.1 The problem

A palletizing robot changes its gripper by means of a tool changer at the end of the arm. On the robot flange side there are two locating pins, on the gripper side two locating bushes; the pins enter the bushes and the two halves are then locked. Every time the robot changes a gripper, the gripper must sit exactly where it did last time: the repeatability must be within a few micrometers. The design requirement is that the clearance between the Ø12 locating pin and the locating bush lie between {{ex4_1.req_lo:.0f}} and {{ex4_1.req_hi:.0f}} μm. If the clearance is too small, the pin cannot be inserted or it jams; if it is too large, the gripper sits in a different position each time it is mounted.

The factory makes several hundred tool changers a month and must also supply spare parts to users. When a user's locating pin is damaged, any new pin taken from the warehouse must meet the requirement when fitted. This is **interchangeability**: parts of the same specification can be fitted and used without selection or fitting work. Interchangeability is the basis of series production and of maintenance. But parts can never be made exactly; the pins ground on one grinding machine differ in diameter from one to the next. For any pin and any bush to give a clearance of {{ex4_1.req_lo:.0f}}–{{ex4_1.req_hi:.0f}} μm, how much may the diameters of the pin and the bush each vary?

### 4.1.2 Ways of achieving interchangeability

Let the range of variation of the pin diameter be $T_s$ and that of the bush $T_h$. For a pin and a bush taken at random, the clearance varies over a range of $T_s + T_h$, which must not exceed the required range of variation of the clearance, {{ex4_1.T_need:.0f}} μm:

$$T_s + T_h \le X_{\max} - X_{\min}$$

The range over which the size of a part is allowed to vary is the **size tolerance**. The inequality shows that tolerances are not set arbitrarily: they are allocated from the functional requirement (here, the clearance). There are several ways of meeting it:

- **Full interchangeability**: the tolerances of the parts are allocated according to the inequality, and any pairing is acceptable. In this example each part can be given only about 5 μm, tighter than grade IT5 (which is {{ex4_1.it5_12}} μm for Ø12) and equivalent to IT4. This calls for fine grinding or even lapping, and the cost is high.
- **Selective assembly** (matched assembly): the parts are made to an economical accuracy; before assembly the pins and bushes are measured one by one and sorted by size into several groups, large pins going with large bushes and small pins with small bushes. The variation of size within each group is small, so the clearance meets the requirement. The price is that every part must be measured and the groups stored separately, and spare parts must also be supplied by group.
- **Fitting**: during assembly one part is ground or scraped a little more until the requirement is met. This suits one-off and small-batch production and very high accuracy, and depends on the skill of the fitter.
- **Adjustment**: an adjustable part is provided (an adjusting shim, an adjusting screw, an eccentric sleeve) and set to the requirement during assembly. The bearing clearance of Section 4.8 uses this method.

The last three are all forms of "incomplete interchangeability". Which one to use depends on batch size, accuracy and cost.

### 4.1.3 Example: full interchangeability or selective assembly

**Example 4.1.1** The locating pins of the tool changer are ground on a grinding machine. The distribution of pin diameters is taken directly from the machining error model of the grinding machine of the digital factory, the same machine that grinds the bearing seats of SH-301. With every part ground, the wheel wears a little and the diameter grows part by part, drifting by about {{ex4_1.drift_pin:.0f}} μm in one dressing cycle and dropping back when the wheel is dressed; a random error is added for each part. The locating bushes are honed, and wear of the honing stones makes the bore smaller part by part (a distribution assumed in this book). Program 4.1.1 makes {{ex4_1.N}} of each part, with nominal sizes chosen so that the mean clearance falls in the middle of the requirement, and compares two ways of assembly (Figure 4.1.1):

- The spread of pin diameters (the width corresponding to ±3σ) is about {{ex4_1.spread_pin:.0f}} μm and that of the bushes about {{ex4_1.spread_hole:.0f}} μm, and their sum is far larger than the permitted {{ex4_1.T_need:.0f}} μm. With random pairing (the order of the bushes shuffled, and each assembled with a pin in turn), only {{ex4_1.p_random:.0f}}% are acceptable.
- Selective assembly: the pins and the bushes are each sorted by measured size into n groups (measurement error σ = {{ex4_1.meas:.1f}} μm), and parts with the same group number are paired. With 2 groups, {{ex4_1.p2:.1f}}% of the pairs made are acceptable; with 3 groups, {{ex4_1.p3:.1f}}%; with 4 groups, {{ex4_1.p4:.1f}}%.

Selective assembly has a cost that is easily overlooked: the number on top of each bar in the right of Figure 4.1.1 is the number of parts left over. The pin distribution is "wear drift + random error" and nearly flat-topped; the bush distribution has a different shape, so within one group number the numbers of pins and bushes are not equal, and the surplus cannot be matched and must wait for the next batch. With 3 groups, {{ex4_1.left3}} parts are left over; counted over all {{ex4_1.N}} pairs of parts, only {{ex4_1.y3:.0f}}% are assembled into acceptable pairs ({{ex4_1.y4:.0f}}% with 4 groups). The number left over does not change monotonically with the number of groups; it depends on whether the distributions of the two parts have the same shape. Selective assembly therefore requires the size distributions of the two parts to be similar in shape; in practice the two parts are often given equal tolerances and made by the same process.

### 4.1.4 Tolerance and cost

The smaller the tolerance, the harder the machining: a more precise machine, slower cutting conditions and an extra finishing operation are needed, and inspection needs more precise gauges as well. Roughly speaking, machining cost rises steeply as the tolerance shrinks. Common empirical models make the cost inversely proportional to the tolerance, or let it grow exponentially (the studies of Chase and others compared these models; see reference 6 of this chapter). The tolerance grades that various processes can reach economically are roughly: finish turning IT7–IT8, honing IT6–IT7 (the economic accuracy table of the digital factory, `factory/digital/std/econ_accuracy.yaml`), external cylindrical grinding IT5–IT7, and lapping better still (handbook values, which vary slightly between handbooks).

The principle of tolerance design is therefore: **make tolerances as large as the function allows.** Where the functional requirement is demanding and full interchangeability cannot be achieved, do not simply keep tightening tolerances; look for another way: selective assembly, adjustment, or a change of structure so that the dimension that matters passes through fewer parts (Section 4.8).

**Engineer's notes**

- Tolerances are allocated from the functional requirement: first ask "how far can this dimension deviate before the machine fails?", then see what the workshop can achieve.
- Parts for selective assembly must be measured one by one, and stored and supplied as spares by group; this must be written into the process documents and the service manual. The user cannot simply fit any pin.
- Robots use selective assembly extensively: ball screw assemblies, precision bearings, and the flexspline and circular spline of harmonic reducers are all matched by the maker and supplied as sets.
