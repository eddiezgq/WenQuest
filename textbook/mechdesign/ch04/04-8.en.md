### 4.8.1 The problem

The output shaft SH-301 of WQR-105 is carried by two 6207 deep-groove ball bearings "fixed at both ends": the outer ring of each bearing is held by the spigot of an end cover, a blind cover on the left and a through cover on the right (through which the overhang passes and in which the oil seal sits). The two end covers fix the axial position of the shaft. But it must not be fixed rigidly: in service the shaft is warmer than the housing and expands, and the housing, end covers and parts on the shaft all have manufacturing errors. A support fixed at both ends must therefore have a little **axial clearance**; for deep-groove ball bearings it is generally {{ex4_8.req_lo:.1f}}–{{ex4_8.req_hi:.1f}} mm (the usual recommendation of machine design handbooks). If the clearance is too small, the bearings are clamped solid when the shaft expands; if it is too large, the gear moves axially and the mesh suffers.

This clearance is not made directly by any one part; it is "made up" by eight dimensions: the housing, the two end covers, the two bearings, the sleeve, the gear hub and one step on the shaft. Each of the eight dimensions has a tolerance. Can the clearance be held within {{ex4_8.req_lo:.1f}}–{{ex4_8.req_hi:.1f}} mm?

### 4.8.2 Basic concepts of dimension chains

A set of dimensions in an assembly or in machining that are connected end to end to form a closed loop is called a **dimension chain** (already met in Section 3.3: with chain dimensioning, the error accumulates along the chain). In it:

- **Closing link**: the dimension that results naturally after assembly or machining, in this example the axial clearance. A dimension chain has only one closing link.
- **Component links**: all the other links, which are held directly by the parts. A component link whose increase makes the closing link larger is an **increasing link**; one whose increase makes the closing link smaller is a **decreasing link**.
- **Compensating link**: a component link chosen in advance whose size is changed during assembly (by fitting shims of different thickness, or by an adjusting screw) to bring the closing link within the requirement.

To draw the dimension chain diagram (Figure 4.8.1a), start from the left end face of the housing and pass through the spigot of the left end cover ($c_1$), the width of the left bearing ($B$), the sleeve, the gear hub, the part of the shaft from the gear locating face to the right bearing shoulder ({{ex4_8.C_sh:.0f}} mm, the dimension given directly by function in Section 3.3), the width of the right bearing, the clearance and the spigot of the right end cover ($c_2$), arriving back at the right end face of the housing; the total equals the distance $W$ between the two end faces of the housing. Hence

$$\text{clearance} = W - c_1 - c_2 - (B + \text{sleeve} + \text{gear hub} + {{ex4_8.C_sh:.0f}} + B)$$

$W$ is an increasing link and the other 7 are decreasing links. The sum of the nominal lengths of the parts on the shaft (the distance between the outer faces of the two bearing outer rings) is {{ex4_8.S_nom:.0f}} mm.

The tolerances of the links (read by Program 4.8.1 from the standard tables) are: distance between the housing end faces $W$ = {{ex4_8.W:.0f}} js11 (±{{ex4_8.tol_W:.3f}}), end-cover spigots js11 (±{{ex4_8.tol_c:.3f}}), bearing width 0/{{ex4_8.B_lo:.2f}} (GB/T 307.1, normal class), sleeve {{ex4_8.sleeve:.0f}} h11 (0/−{{ex4_8.tol_sl:.2f}}), gear hub {{ex4_8.hub:.0f}} h11 (0/−{{ex4_8.tol_hub:.2f}}), and {{ex4_8.C_sh:.0f}} js11 on the shaft (±{{ex4_8.tol_C:.3f}}). The housing width $W$ and the lengths of the end-cover spigots are assumed in this book; the housing HSG-001 of the digital factory does not yet give these dimensions.

### 4.8.3 The worst-case method

The **worst-case method** assumes that every component link may be at its most unfavorable limit at the same time:

$$A_{0\max} = \sum \vec{A}_{i\max} - \sum \overleftarrow{A}_{i\min}, \qquad A_{0\min} = \sum \vec{A}_{i\min} - \sum \overleftarrow{A}_{i\max}, \qquad T_0 = \sum T_i$$

($\vec A$ denotes increasing links and $\overleftarrow A$ decreasing links.) The tolerance of the closing link equals the sum of the tolerances of the component links, whether they are increasing or decreasing.

It is more convenient to calculate with mean deviations. The **mean deviation** of each link is $\Delta_i$ = (upper deviation + lower deviation)/2; the mean deviation of the closing link is $\Delta_0 = \sum \xi_i \Delta_i$ ($\xi$ = +1 for increasing links, −1 for decreasing links), and the closing link = nominal value + $\Delta_0$ ± $T_0/2$. This matters a great deal: most of the decreasing links in this example have "unilateral" tolerances (h11, the bearing's 0/−0.12), whose mean sizes are all smaller than their nominal sizes, so the mean clearance is larger than its nominal value.

**Example 4.8.1** First, "make up the nominal sizes": with a spigot length of {{ex4_8.c_naive:.2f}} mm, the nominal clearance is exactly 0.3 mm. But calculated from the mean sizes, the mean clearance is {{ex4_8.mu_naive:.2f}} mm, outside the required range. The spigot length must therefore be set from the mean sizes: taking {{ex4_8.c1:.2f}} mm (rounded to 0.05 mm), the nominal clearance becomes {{ex4_8.nom1:.1f}} and the mean clearance {{ex4_8.mu1:.2f}} mm.

Now by the worst-case method: the clearance varies from {{ex4_8.wmin1:.2f}} to {{ex4_8.wmax1:.2f}} mm, and $T_0$ = {{ex4_8.T_wc:.2f}} mm, more than five times the required {{ex4_8.T_req:.1f}} mm. The lower limit is negative: in the worst case the bearings are clamped solid by the end covers and cannot even be assembled.

Should the tolerances of all the links be tightened? A quick calculation shows that it would not help: the sum of the tolerances of the two bearing widths alone is {{ex4_8.T_brg:.2f}} mm, which already exceeds {{ex4_8.T_req:.1f}} mm. The bearings are purchased standard parts, and we cannot change their tolerances. **This dimension chain cannot be made fully interchangeable by the worst-case method.**

### 4.8.4 The statistical method

The worst-case method assumes that all the links are at their worst limits at once, and in reality the chance of this is minute: the errors of the eight parts are some positive and some negative, and most of them cancel. If the component links are independent, all normally distributed, centered in their tolerance zones, and the width of each tolerance zone equals ±3σ, then the closing link is also normally distributed with standard deviation $\sigma_0 = \sqrt{\sum \sigma_i^2}$, that is,

$$T_0 = \sqrt{\sum T_i^2}$$

This is called **statistical tolerancing** (the root-sum-square method). In this example $T_0$ = {{ex4_8.T_rss:.3f}} mm, much smaller than the {{ex4_8.T_wc:.2f}} of the worst-case method but still twice the requirement. By the normal distribution, the proportion of clearances within {{ex4_8.req_lo:.1f}}–{{ex4_8.req_hi:.1f}} mm is {{ex4_8.p_rss:.0f}}%.

The assumptions of the statistical method must be checked one by one: the parts are produced in large batches and independently; the distributions are close to normal and centered in their tolerance zones. When the assumptions do not hold, the method is too optimistic.

### 4.8.5 The Monte Carlo method

The **Monte Carlo method** needs none of these assumptions: a set of dimensions is drawn at random from the actual distribution of each link and the clearance is calculated; this is repeated several hundred thousand times, and the proportion that conforms is counted directly. The distributions may be skewed, truncated or of any shape, and the relations between the links may be nonlinear (involving angles or radii, for example).

**Example 4.8.2** Program 4.8.1 draws {{ex4_8.n_mc}} samples. Machined dimensions are normally distributed (centered in the tolerance zone, σ = T/6); bearing widths are normal N(−0.05, 0.015) mm, since the actual distribution from the bearing maker is narrower than the tolerance zone and its center slightly larger than the middle of the zone (−0.06) (assumed in this book; in practice it should be obtained from the bearing maker). The clearance has a mean of {{ex4_8.mc_mean:.3f}} mm and a standard deviation of {{ex4_8.mc_sd:.3f}} mm, and {{ex4_8.p_mc:.0f}}% conform (Figure 4.8.1b). This is lower than the statistical result, because the actual center of the bearing width is not in the middle of its tolerance zone, which shifts the mean clearance away from the design center. The statistical method is most vulnerable to off-center distributions.

### 4.8.6 A compensating link: adjusting shims

Since full interchangeability is impossible, the "adjustment" method of Section 4.1 is used: a set of **adjusting shims** is placed between the through cover and the housing as the compensating link. During assembly, the through cover is first pressed lightly against the bearing outer ring without shims (not tightened), and the gap δ between the flange of the through cover and the end face of the housing is measured with feeler gauges; the shim thickness = δ + target clearance (rounded to the shim step). (Alternatively, the depth from the end face of the housing to the bearing outer ring and the length of the end-cover spigot can be measured and δ calculated.)

Shims can only increase the clearance, never reduce it, so the clearance without shims must under no circumstances exceed the upper limit. **Example 4.8.3** The spigot is lengthened to {{ex4_8.c2:.2f}} mm (moving into the 10–18 mm size range, where js11 becomes ±{{ex4_8.tol_c2:.3f}}), and without shims the worst-case range of the clearance is {{ex4_8.wmin2:.2f}} to {{ex4_8.wmax2:.2f}} mm. The shims are combinations of 0.05, 0.1, 0.2 and 0.5 mm thicknesses, with a smallest step of {{ex4_8.step:.2f}} mm; the measurement error is σ = {{ex4_8.meas_sd:.2f}} mm. By the worst-case method, the thickest shim pack needed is about {{ex4_8.shim_wc:.1f}} mm. In the sampling of the program, the shims average {{ex4_8.shim_mean:.2f}} mm with a maximum of {{ex4_8.shim_max:.2f}} mm, and after assembly every clearance is within the requirement ({{ex4_8.p_shim:.0f}}% conform, Figure 4.8.1c).

Without tightening the tolerance of any part, the problem is solved by one measurement and a choice of shims during assembly. The price is one more assembly operation, several sizes of shim to keep in stock, and an assembly instruction sheet that must state how to measure and choose the shims.

There are dimension chains like this in everyday life too. An inline-skate wheel contains two 608 bearings whose inner rings clamp a spacer between them (the axle bolt presses them together), while their outer rings rest on either side of a boss in the middle of the hub. The spacer length minus the boss width is the axial clearance of the bearings, a chain of only two component links. If the spacer is shorter than the boss, the bearings are clamped solid when the bolt is tightened and the wheel will not turn; if it is too long, the wheel wobbles. The boss of an injection-molded hub has a large tolerance, and the cheap solution is to make spacers in several lengths and select them during assembly, which is also a kind of compensation. This is the second scene of Lab 4.8.

Finally, check the requirement itself: when the shaft is {{ex4_8.dT:.0f}} K warmer than the housing, the distance between the two bearing outer rings grows by $\alpha \cdot {{ex4_8.S_nom:.0f}} \cdot {{ex4_8.dT:.0f}}$ = {{ex4_8.d_thermal:.3f}} mm, far less than the lower limit of {{ex4_8.req_lo:.1f}} mm. The recommended value is generous because the deformation of the housing under load, the internal clearance of the bearings and assembly errors must also be allowed for.

### 4.8.7 Choosing a method

| Method | Guarantees | Suitable for |
|---|---|---|
| Worst-case, full interchangeability | 100% assembly | Few links (≤ 4), one-off and small batches, safety-critical dimensions |
| Statistical, partial interchangeability | The great majority (about three in a thousand outside when designed to ±3σ); a few need rework | Many links, large batches, processes that are stable and can be verified statistically |
| Monte Carlo | Yield estimated from the actual distributions | Chains with non-normal, off-center or nonlinear behavior; design verification |
| Selective assembly, fitting, adjustment | 100% assembly with relaxed part tolerances | Demanding requirements where full interchangeability is impossible |

Dimension chains are also used in machining: when a dimension cannot be machined directly, it has to be held indirectly through several successive operations. This is the process dimension chain, to which *Manufacturing Technology* devotes a chapter (process dimension chains) (the digital factory's `hub/tolerance.py` has a ready-made calculation library).

**Engineer's notes**

- Draw the dimension chain diagram first, then write the equation. To find the closing link, ask "which part makes this dimension directly?"; the one that no part makes is the closing link.
- In design, keep dimension chains as short as possible: the fewer parts an important assembly dimension passes through, the better (the "shortest chain principle").
- For unilateral tolerances (h11, bearing widths), calculate the mean from the mean sizes; do not make up the numbers from nominal sizes.
- A design based on the statistical method must monitor the process capability of each link in the inspection plan; if a process drifts, the assumptions of the statistical method no longer hold.
