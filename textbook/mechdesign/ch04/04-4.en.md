### 4.4.1 Is a size tolerance enough?

If every two-point diameter measured in every section of the bearing seat Ø35k6 of SH-301 lies between {{ex4_2.k6_min:.3f}} and {{ex4_2.k6_max:.3f}}, is the seat necessarily good? Not necessarily. The journal may be oval or three-lobed (measured at two points with a micrometer, a three-lobed form has the same "diameter" everywhere and yet is not round); it may be thicker at one end than the other; or the two bearing seats may not be coaxial. A bearing inner ring is thin, and when pressed onto a journal that is out of round it deforms with it: the raceway is out of round, and the bearing runs hot and noisy and has a short life. If the two bearing seats are not coaxial, the inner and outer rings of the bearings are tilted relative to each other. Besides size, therefore, the form, orientation and location of features must also be limited. This is what **geometrical tolerances** do (GB/T 1182 / ISO 1101).

### 4.4.2 Kinds of geometrical tolerance

| Category | Characteristics | Datum | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Form tolerance** | Straightness, flatness, roundness, cylindricity, line profile, surface profile | No | Cylindricity of a bearing seat |
| **Orientation tolerance** | Parallelism, perpendicularity, angularity (and line and surface profile) | Yes | Parallelism of the pillow-block bore axis to the base |
| **Location tolerance** | Position, coaxiality (concentricity), symmetry (and line and surface profile) | Yes | Position of anchor-bolt holes, symmetry of a keyway |
| **Run-out tolerance** | Circular run-out, total run-out | Yes | Radial circular run-out of the gear seat relative to A–B |

A form tolerance controls only the shape of the feature itself; an orientation tolerance controls its orientation relative to a datum; a location tolerance controls its orientation and location relative to a datum. A run-out tolerance is a composite characteristic defined by a method of measurement: the maximum variation of the indicator reading while the part turns once about the datum axis. It reflects form and location errors at the same time, is simple to measure, and is the one most used on shafts.

On drawings a geometrical tolerance is written in a **tolerance frame**. The first compartment holds the characteristic symbol, the second the tolerance value (preceded by Ø if the tolerance zone is a circle or cylinder), and the third and following ones the datum letters. The frame is connected to the toleranced feature by a leader line with an arrow: an arrow on the outline controls the surface, and an arrow aligned with a dimension line controls the axis or median plane. For example, "↗ 0.015 A–B" on SH-301 (task sheet TS-3-1 of Chapter 3) means that when the gear seat turns once about the common axis of the two bearing seats, the radial circular run-out must not exceed 0.015 mm.

### 4.4.3 Tolerance zones

Every geometrical tolerance defines a **tolerance zone**, the region within which the whole toleranced feature must lie. The shape of the zone is determined by the characteristic and by the way it is indicated:

- **Roundness**: between two concentric circles in the same cross-section whose radii differ by t.
- **Cylindricity**: between two coaxial cylinders whose radii differ by t; this limits roundness, straightness of the generatrix and taper at the same time.
- **Parallelism** (surface to surface): between two parallel planes a distance t apart and parallel to the datum plane.
- **Position** (axis of a hole): within a cylinder of diameter t whose axis is the theoretically exact position. The theoretically exact position is given by framed dimensions (theoretically exact dimensions), which have no tolerance of their own.
- **Radial circular run-out**: in every cross-section perpendicular to the datum axis, between two concentric circles centered on the datum axis whose radii differ by t.

The tolerance zone of a form tolerance can "float": the two concentric circles may be centered anywhere, so long as they enclose the actual profile. The zone of an orientation tolerance has its orientation fixed by the datum but its location may still float. The zones of position and run-out tolerances have both orientation and location fixed by the datum.

### 4.4.4 Evaluating form errors: the minimum zone

A coordinate measuring machine or a roundness tester measures several hundred points in one section. How is the "roundness error" obtained from these points? The key is how the reference circle is chosen. Two are in common use:

- **Least-squares circle**: minimizes the sum of the squares of the distances from the points to the circle. It is simple to calculate (a system of linear equations), the result is stable, and measuring-machine software often uses it by default.
- **Minimum zone circles**: two concentric circles that enclose all the points with the smallest difference in radius. This is exactly how GB/T 1182 / ISO 1101 define the tolerance zone (the zone must "enclose" the actual feature), so it is the basis for deciding conformity.

The minimum zone result can never be larger than the least-squares result: the center of the least-squares circle is not chosen to make the difference in radius smallest, so enclosing circles drawn about it can only have a larger difference in radius.

**Example 4.4.1** One section of a bearing seat of SH-301 is scanned by a coordinate measuring machine at {{ex4_4.N}} points. The points contain an eccentricity of about {{ex4_4.ecc:.0f}} μm (the origin of the measuring coordinate system does not coincide with the center of this section, and this must be removed in the evaluation), two- and three-lobed form errors (vibration in grinding, center-hole errors) and random error. Program 4.4.1 evaluates them by both methods (Figure 4.4.1). When the eccentricity is much smaller than the radius, the distance from a point to the center can be linearized as $r_i \approx R + a\cos\theta_i + b\sin\theta_i$ ($a$ and $b$ being the offsets of the center), so that:

- least squares: solve the linear least-squares problem for $R$, $a$ and $b$; the largest residual minus the smallest is the roundness, {{ex4_4.rnd_ls:.2f}} μm;
- minimum zone: find $a$, $b$, $R_{\text{in}}$ and the width $w$ such that $R_{\text{in}} \le r_i - a\cos\theta_i - b\sin\theta_i \le R_{\text{in}} + w$ holds for all points with $w$ as small as possible. This is a linear programming problem, and it gives {{ex4_4.rnd_mz:.2f}} μm.

The two differ by {{ex4_4.diff_pct:.0f}}%. With a roundness tolerance of {{ex4_4.tol:.0f}} μm (the value GB/T 275 recommends for the cylindricity of seats of normal-class bearings; only one section is evaluated here), the section evaluated by least squares {{ex4_4.ok_ls}}, while evaluated by the minimum zone it {{ex4_4.ok_mz}}. The two minimum zone circles are "held" by {{ex4_4.n_contact}} points (two on the inner circle and two on the outer, alternating), which is the criterion for the minimum zone (the crossing criterion). A glance ahead to Section 4.9: the minimum zone result is only a few tenths of a micrometer from the tolerance, less than the measurement uncertainty, so strictly speaking it falls in the "uncertainty range"; for such parts the decision rule must be agreed in advance.

At the boundary of conformity, the method of evaluation can change the conclusion. An inspection report must therefore state the method of evaluation, and in a dispute the minimum zone governs. As far as the method of evaluation goes, the least-squares result errs on the conservative side and may be used for routine inspection; if it exceeds the tolerance, the result is rechecked by the minimum zone method. But when the points are too sparse, both methods may miss the highest and lowest points of the profile and underestimate the error: there must be enough points.

**Engineer's notes**

- Two-point measurement of diameter (micrometer, caliper) cannot detect out-of-roundness with an odd number of lobes; watch for this especially on centerless-ground workpieces. It shows up only with a V-block and indicator or a roundness tester.
- Run-out is the easiest tolerance to measure (support the shaft between two centers and turn it once), and it is the first choice for shafts; when run-out is under control, coaxiality and roundness generally are too.
- Geometrical tolerance values are usually smaller than size tolerances: the cylindricity of a bearing seat is about a quarter to a half of its size tolerance.
