On the part drawing of Chapter 3, the bearing seat of SH-301 is marked Ø35k6, the gear bore and shaft Ø40 H7/r6, and there are symbols such as "↗ 0.015 A–B" and Ra 0.8. They all answer the same question. A part can never be made exactly: **how much may it deviate, in which direction, how is it measured, and how is the result judged?** If the permitted deviation is too large, the machine cannot be assembled or does not last; if it is too small, the workshop cannot make the part, or the cost goes up several times over. Tolerances are an agreement between the designer, the workshop and inspection, and they are part of the quality of a design.

The chapter proceeds in the order "size, form and position, surface, assembly, inspection". Section 4.1 explains why tolerances are needed and the ways in which interchangeability can be achieved. Sections 4.2 and 4.3 cover size tolerances and fits: how standard tolerances and fundamental deviations make up tolerance zones, and how fits are chosen. Sections 4.4 and 4.5 cover geometrical tolerances and datums: the tolerance zones for form, orientation, location and run-out, and how errors are evaluated from measured points. Section 4.6 covers the relation between size tolerances and geometrical tolerances (tolerancing principles), especially how the maximum material requirement relaxes tolerances where all that is required is that the parts "go together". Section 4.7 covers surface roughness. Section 4.8 covers dimension chains: how the tolerances of several parts accumulate into an assembly requirement. Section 4.9 covers measurement and inspection: coordinate measuring machines, measurement uncertainty and conformity decisions, and process capability.

The examples are mostly from robotics: the locating pins of the tool changer at the end of a palletizing robot (interchangeability and selective assembly), the bearings of a collaborative-robot shoulder joint mounted in an aluminum-alloy housing (fits and temperature), and the anchor-bolt holes of the pillow block of the palletizing conveyor (maximum material requirement). The output shaft SH-301 version B of WQR-105 runs through the whole chapter: its fits, roundness, datums and roughness, the dimension chain of the axial clearance of the output shaft system, and the CMM data of a batch of SH-301 ground on the grinding machine of the digital factory. The everyday example is the wheels of inline skates (Lab 4.8). All the numbers in this chapter are calculated by programs: standard tolerances and fundamental deviations come from the digitized standard tables of this book, the distribution of ground sizes from the machining error model of the digital factory, and process capability is calculated with the same function as the quality module of the digital factory. The task sheet of this chapter, TS-4-1, puts the reader in the role of a quality engineer in the class factory, who judges a batch of SH-301, disposes of the nonconforming parts and finds in the data the process problem that the teacher has injected.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. state the differences between full interchangeability, selective assembly, fitting and adjustment, and the relation between tolerance and cost;
2. find the limit deviations from a tolerance class and draw the tolerance zone diagram; state the meaning of the standard tolerance factor and the tolerance grade;
3. calculate the limits of clearance or interference of a fit, and choose a fit according to the working conditions (including fits of rolling bearings and the effect of temperature);
4. read a geometrical tolerance frame, state the tolerance zones of the various geometrical tolerances, and evaluate form errors by the minimum zone method;
5. state the meaning of datums, common datums and the three-plane datum system;
6. state the differences between the independency principle, the envelope requirement and the maximum material requirement, calculate the bonus tolerance and the virtual condition size under the maximum material requirement, and state the principle of functional gauges;
7. state the definitions of Ra and Rz and the roles of the sampling length and filtering, and estimate the roughness of turning from the feed and the tool nose radius;
8. set up a dimension chain, calculate the closing link by the worst-case, statistical and Monte Carlo methods, and use a compensating link where full interchangeability cannot be achieved;
9. state the acceptance specifications of a coordinate measuring machine, draw up a simple measurement uncertainty budget, decide conformity according to ISO 14253-1, and calculate and interpret Cp, Cpk, Pp and Ppk.
