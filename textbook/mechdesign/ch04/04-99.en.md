Parts can never be made exactly, and tolerances state how much they may deviate. Tolerances are allocated from function: full interchangeability requires the sum of the part tolerances not to exceed the variation the function allows; where that is impossible, selective assembly, fitting or adjustment is used. With random pairing, {{ex4_1.p_random:.0f}}% of the locating pins of the tool changer conform; with selective assembly in 3 groups, {{ex4_1.p3:.1f}}% of the pairs made conform, but some parts cannot be matched, and counted over all the parts the figure is {{ex4_1.y3:.0f}}%. A size tolerance zone is formed by a standard tolerance (its size, the IT grade) and a fundamental deviation (its position, the letter). The tolerance zones of a hole and a shaft form one of three kinds of fit, clearance, transition or interference, generally in the hole-basis system. The tolerance zone of a rolling-bearing inner ring lies below the zero line, so a k6 shaft fitted into it gives an interference. Fits must be checked at the working temperature: a bearing outer ring in an aluminum-alloy housing is loosened by {{ex4_3.loosen:.0f}} μm by a temperature rise of {{ex4_3.dT:.0f}} K.

Geometrical tolerances control form, orientation, location and run-out, and the tolerance zone must enclose the actual feature. Form errors are evaluated by the minimum zone method, and the least-squares result is larger: for the roundness of the bearing seat, {{ex4_4.rnd_ls:.1f}} μm against {{ex4_4.rnd_mz:.1f}} μm, which leads to opposite conclusions. Datums are chosen on the surfaces that locate the part in assembly: for SH-301 the common axis A–B of the two bearing seats, for housing-type parts the 3–2–1 three-plane datum system. The relation between size and geometrical tolerances is set by the tolerancing principles: the Chinese default is the independency principle, mating surfaces take the envelope requirement Ⓔ, and features that need only assemble take the maximum material requirement Ⓜ. For the anchor-bolt holes of the pillow block, this raises the yield from {{ex4_6.p_rfs:.1f}}% to {{ex4_6.p_mmr:.1f}}%, and all the extra parts accepted can be assembled. Surface roughness is evaluated by Ra and Rz after Gaussian filtering; the theoretical Ra of turning is proportional to the square of the feed and inversely proportional to the tool nose radius.

A dimension chain accumulates the tolerances of several parts into an assembly requirement. The axial clearance of the output shaft system of WQR-105 must be 0.2–0.4 mm, while the tolerances of the two bearing widths alone add up to {{ex4_8.T_brg:.2f}} mm, so full interchangeability is impossible. The statistical method gives {{ex4_8.p_rss:.0f}}% conforming, and Monte Carlo with the actual distributions only {{ex4_8.p_mc:.0f}}%; with adjusting shims as the compensating link and the part tolerances unchanged, {{ex4_8.p_shim:.0f}}% conform. Inspection must take measurement uncertainty into account (ISO 14253-1: to conform, a value must lie in the acceptance zone), and processes must be judged by their capability: the bearing seats ground on the grinding machine of the digital factory have $C_{pk}$ = {{ex4_9.Cpk35:.2f}} but $P_{pk}$ of only {{ex4_9.Ppk35:.2f}}, because the short-term index cannot see the trend of wheel wear.

### Historical notes

Interchangeable manufacture originated in the making of firearms from the end of the 18th century into the 19th. In France, Honoré Blanc demonstrated interchangeable flintlocks in the 1780s; in the United States, the Springfield and Harpers Ferry armories gradually achieved interchangeability of firearm parts in the first half of the 19th century, in what came to be called the "American system of manufactures". Interchangeability depends on gauges: parts are not made "the same as the sample" but checked with GO and NOT GO gauges to lie between two limits. In 1902 the Newall Engineering Company in England published a standard of limits for holes and shafts, regarded as one of the earliest standards of limits and fits; in 1905 W. Taylor put forward the principle for designing limit gauges. In the 1930s the International Federation of the National Standardizing Associations (ISA) drew up a unified system of limits and fits, which in 1962 became ISO Recommendation R286, the forerunner of today's ISO 286. China issued its first standards for limits and fits in 1959 and revised them according to the ISO system as the GB 1800 series in 1979.

The modern form of geometrical tolerancing came from Britain around the Second World War. Stanley Parker of the Royal Torpedo Factory in Scotland noticed that hole positions dimensioned by coordinates have square tolerance zones, whereas what the function allows is in fact a circular region; he therefore proposed "true position" with a cylindrical tolerance zone and the functional gauges that go with it. The US military issued MIL-STD-8 in 1949, which later developed into ASME Y14.5; internationally, ISO Recommendation ISO/R 1101 was issued in 1969, the forerunner of today's ISO 1101. The measurement of surface roughness appeared in the 1930s with the stylus profilometer, and E. J. Abbott and others used it to build the first commercial instruments. The coordinate measuring machine was first introduced by the British company Ferranti in 1959. Tolerance analysis developed from the worst-case method to the statistical method and then to Monte Carlo simulation on computers; the survey by Chase and Parkinson in 1991 summarized this development.
