### 4.7.1 The surface under magnification

Magnified several thousand times, the bearing seat of SH-301 is a landscape of peaks and valleys formed by tool marks or grinding marks. These tiny irregularities affect many properties: the peaks of a mating surface are flattened, which reduces the interference; the rougher a sliding surface, the faster it wears; the lip of an oil seal running on a rough shaft soon wears out and leaks; fatigue cracks start at the bottoms of the valleys (the surface factor of Chapter 33). The important surfaces on a part drawing must therefore have their **surface roughness** specified.

The profile of a real surface contains three components (Figure 4.7.1a): roughness with very small spacing (tool marks, scratches from abrasive grains), **waviness** with larger spacing (machine vibration, unbalance of the grinding wheel), and the overall form error (Section 4.4). They are distinguished by wavelength. The current standards call the three together **surface texture** (GB/T 131-2006; internationally the ISO 21920 series has replaced ISO 1302 and ISO 4287).

### 4.7.2 Sampling length, filtering, Ra and Rz

To evaluate roughness, the long-wavelength components are first filtered out. This is done by computing a **mean line** with a Gaussian filter (ISO 16610-21). The mean line is a "moving weighted average" of the profile with a bell-shaped weighting function:

$$s(x) = \frac{1}{\alpha\lambda_c}\exp\!\left[-\pi\left(\frac{x}{\alpha\lambda_c}\right)^2\right], \qquad \alpha = \sqrt{\ln 2/\pi}$$

$\lambda_c$ is called the **cut-off wavelength**: a sinusoidal component of wavelength $\lambda_c$ keeps half its amplitude after the filter; longer components go essentially into the mean line (and count as waviness), and shorter ones stay essentially in the roughness profile. The profile minus the mean line is the roughness profile. The cut-off wavelength is also the **sampling length**, and the evaluation normally takes 5 consecutive sampling lengths as the **evaluation length**. $\lambda_c$ is chosen according to the roughness: 0.8 mm for surfaces with Ra of 0.1–2 μm (GB/T 10610).

The two most common parameters are:

- **Ra**: the arithmetical mean deviation, the mean of the absolute values of the deviations of the roughness profile from the mean line, $Ra = \frac{1}{l}\int_0^l |z(x)|\,dx$. It is stable and easy to measure, and is the most used on Chinese drawings.
- **Rz**: the maximum height, the distance between the highest peak and the deepest valley within each sampling length, averaged over the 5 sampling lengths. It is sensitive to individual deep scratches, and is often added for sealing surfaces and surfaces with demanding fatigue requirements.

Values of Ra are chosen from the preferred number series: 0.4, 0.8, 1.6, 3.2, 6.3, 12.5 μm (GB/T 1031). On drawings they are indicated with the √-shaped symbol (Section 3.4), such as Ra 0.8.

### 4.7.3 Where the roughness of a turned surface comes from

The tip of a turning tool is a circular arc (radius $r_\varepsilon$), and the tool advances by one feed $f$ for each revolution of the workpiece, leaving a residual peak between two adjacent cuts. From the geometry, the residual height is

$$R_{th} = r_\varepsilon - \sqrt{r_\varepsilon^2 - (f/2)^2} \approx \frac{f^2}{8 r_\varepsilon}$$

This is the theoretical minimum; a real surface also shows the effects of tiny chipping of the cutting edge, tearing of the material, built-up edge and vibration.

**Example 4.7.1** SH-301 is finish-turned with a feed of f = {{ex4_7.f1}} mm/r and a tool nose radius $r_\varepsilon$ = {{ex4_7.r1}} mm; the theoretical residual height is {{ex4_7.Rth1:.2f}} μm. Program 4.7.1 synthesizes a measured profile: the residual left by the tool nose radius, plus random micro-irregularities, plus waviness of wavelength {{ex4_7.wave_l}} mm and amplitude {{ex4_7.wave_a:.1f}} μm left by machine vibration (assumed in this book). It then applies the standard Gaussian filter ($\lambda_c$ = {{ex4_7.lc}} mm, evaluation length {{ex4_7.ln:.0f}} mm) and obtains Ra = {{ex4_7.Ra1:.2f}} μm and Rz = {{ex4_7.Rz1:.2f}} μm (Figure 4.7.1). Without filtering, a large part of the total profile height of {{ex4_7.Rt_raw:.1f}} μm is waviness, which must not be counted as roughness; after filtering, Rz is close to the theoretical residual height of {{ex4_7.Rth1:.2f}} μm.

With the geometric residual alone (no noise or waviness), the ratio of Ra to the residual height is {{ex4_7.ratio:.3f}}, so the theoretical Ra of turning can be estimated as

$$Ra\,(\mu\text{m}) \approx {{ex4_7.c_ra_um:.1f}}\,\frac{f^2}{r_\varepsilon} \qquad (f, r_\varepsilon \text{ in mm})$$

(When the residual cross-section is approximated by a parabola, the theoretical value of the ratio is $4/(9\sqrt{3})$ = {{ex4_7.ratio_th:.3f}}.)

**Example 4.7.2** The bearing seats of SH-301 require Ra 0.8. With a tool of $r_\varepsilon$ = 0.8 mm, working back from the theoretical formula, Ra = 0.8 μm corresponds to a feed of about {{ex4_7.f_th:.2f}} mm/r. A real surface is rougher than the theory, so 0.1 mm/r is taken to leave a margin, and the program gives Ra = {{ex4_7.Ra_f01:.2f}} μm (theory {{ex4_7.Ra_th_f01:.2f}} μm, Figure 4.7.1c). At a feed of 0.3 mm/r, a tool nose radius of 0.4 mm gives Ra as high as {{ex4_7.Ra_f03_r04:.1f}} μm, and one of 1.2 mm only {{ex4_7.Ra_f03_r12:.1f}} μm. Ra is proportional to the square of the feed and inversely proportional to the nose radius: halving the feed cuts Ra to a quarter, whereas doubling the nose radius only halves it. As far as roughness alone is concerned, reducing the feed is more effective; but a small feed means low productivity, and in production a larger nose radius with the feed kept up is often preferred. The radius cannot be too large either: the radial cutting force increases, and slender shafts tend to vibrate.

In theory turning can reach Ra 0.8, but real surfaces are often rougher than the theoretical value (tool wear, vibration, material), and it is hard to hold reliably in series production; the Ra 0.4 required at the seal seat is harder still. In the digital factory, therefore, the bearing seats of SH-301 have a grinding operation (grinding machine grd-01). The seal seat of version B must also be ground (plunge grinding), a step that the current process of the digital factory (version A) does not yet include.

### 4.7.4 Choosing roughness

The smaller the roughness, the more expensive the machining. The principle is to make it as large as the function allows. Reference values:

| Surface | Ra / μm | Machining method |
|---|---|---|
| Rolling bearing seat (shaft, normal class) | 0.8 (d ≤ 80 mm) | Grinding |
| Rolling bearing housing bore | 1.6 | Fine boring |
| Journal for a lip seal | 0.2–0.8 | Grinding, without lead (to prevent "pumping") |
| Gear bore, coupling bore (H7) | 1.6 | Fine boring, broaching |
| Keyway sides | 3.2 | Milling |
| Non-mating turned surface | 6.3–12.5 | Turning |

(The values follow GB/T 275, GB/T 13871 and common handbooks; the catalogs of the chosen bearing and seal makers govern.)

**Engineer's notes**

- Roughness must match the size tolerance: Ra 0.8 for an IT6 journal and Ra 1.6 for an IT7 hole are common combinations. With a very small tolerance and a large roughness, the peaks are flattened on assembly and the character of the fit changes.
- Besides Ra, a seal journal requires "lead-free grinding" (plunge grinding); otherwise a helical lead pumps the oil out like a screw thread.
- When roughness is measured, the stylus must travel perpendicular to the lay; if the drawing does not say otherwise, the inspector judges from the direction of the lay.
