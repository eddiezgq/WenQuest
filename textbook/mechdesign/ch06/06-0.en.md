Every dimension, every fillet and every tolerance on a drawing must in the end be made by someone on some machine, and the parts must be assembled by someone. A designer takes a second to draw a line, but the machining and assembly that the line commits to may take tens of minutes and cost tens of dollars. It is generally held that most of the cost of a product is fixed at the design stage: the choice of processes, the number of parts and the tightness of the tolerances are all decided by the designer, and by the time the drawing reaches the shop there is little room left for savings. A designer must therefore understand manufacturing: not how to run a machine tool, but what each process can do and cannot do, and where its cost lies.

This chapter covers the common **manufacturing processes** from the designer's point of view: casting and forging (Section 6.1), machining (Section 6.2), sheet metal, welding and profiles (Section 6.3), injection molding and die casting (Section 6.4) and additive manufacturing (Section 6.5). It then presents two systematic methods, **design for manufacture** (DFM, Section 6.6) and **design for assembly** (DFA, Section 6.7), and finally shows how to estimate cost and how to choose a process by batch size (Section 6.8). The details of the processes themselves (cutting conditions, tools, fixtures, process planning, tolerance charting) belong to the companion volume *Manufacturing Technology*; this chapter covers only what a designer must know, and points to where the rest can be found.

The robot example running through the chapter is a small part: the L-shaped mounting bracket of an XM540 servo (servo outline from the parts library [[part:D-ACT-ROBOTIS-X/XM540-W270]]). The same bracket is designed for milling, for sheet metal and for 3D printing (Sections 6.3, 6.6 and 6.8) and costed (Section 6.8). The reducer examples use data from the digital factory: the routing, operation times and station rates of the output shaft SH-301 (Section 6.2), the bearing-block casting of Chapter 3 (Section 6.1), and the bill of materials of the output-shaft assembly SA-300 (Section 6.7). There is also the rear cover assembly of a robot joint, used to explain design for assembly (Section 6.7); task sheet TS-6-1 of this chapter asks the reader to redesign it. The everyday example is a wall-shelf bracket (Lab 6.8).

All the cost figures in this chapter are illustrative and in US dollars. The rates of the milling and assembly stations are those of the digital factory (consistent with its cost accounting); the rates and material prices for laser cutting, bending, injection molding and 3D printing are teaching values assumed in this book. The values themselves vary with region, factory and year; the methods and the general trends of the conclusions do not.

After studying this chapter the reader should be able to:

1. state the characteristics, accuracy ranges and typical parts of casting, forging, machining, sheet metal, injection molding, die casting and additive manufacturing, and propose a suitable process for a part;
2. check a casting against the casting rules (uniform walls, no hot spots, draft, fillets), and locate hot spots using the solidification modulus;
3. read an economic machining accuracy table and judge whether a tolerance requires grinding; estimate the machining time and cost of a part;
4. calculate the flat length of a sheet-metal part, and check it against the sheet-metal rules on minimum flange length and distance from hole to bend line;
5. estimate the cooling time of an injection-molded part, and explain why the walls must be uniform;
6. state the main additive manufacturing processes and their design rules, and judge when they are worth using;
7. check a part rule by rule against design rules; analyze an assembly by the Boothroyd–Dewhurst method and find the parts that can be eliminated;
8. divide cost into material cost, processing cost and the set-up cost allocated to each part, find the break-even batch size, and choose a process by batch size.
