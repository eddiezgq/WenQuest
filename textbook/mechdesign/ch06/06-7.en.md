### 6.7.1 The fewer parts the better

Even a part that is only a washer has to be designed, drawn, purchased, inspected, stocked, managed in inventory, delivered to the line, picked up and fitted; every step costs money and time, and every step can go wrong. The core of **design for assembly** (DFA) can be summed up in a single sentence: reduce the number of parts; and then make the remaining parts easy to handle and easy to fit.

Beginning in the 1970s, G. Boothroyd and P. Dewhurst turned this idea into a systematic method: three questions to decide whether each part must exist separately, tables of measured times to estimate the assembly time of each part, and one index to measure how good the design is.

### 6.7.2 Three questions: the theoretical minimum number of parts

Consider each part in turn as it is assembled (starting with the base part) and ask three questions:

1. When the product is working, does the part move relative to the parts already assembled? (Only real motion counts, not small deformations of elastic parts.)
2. Must it be of a different material from, or be isolated from, the parts already assembled (electrical insulation, vibration isolation, thermal insulation)?
3. Must it be separate in order to assemble or disassemble other parts (including for maintenance)?

If the answer to any of the three is "yes", the part is essential; if all three are "no", then in principle it can be combined with another part or eliminated. The number of essential parts is the **theoretical minimum number of parts** Nmin. Fasteners (screws, washers, nuts), dowel pins, brackets and labels almost always get three "no"s, and they are the first candidates for elimination.

### 6.7.3 Manual assembly time and assembly efficiency

The manual assembly time of each part = **handling time** (grasping it from the bin and orienting it) + **insertion time** (placing it, aligning it, fastening it). The Boothroyd–Dewhurst manual assembly tables classify parts by size, symmetry, whether they tangle or are fragile, whether they must be held down during insertion, whether there is resistance, whether screws must be driven, and so on, and give an average time for each class. Small parts, asymmetric parts and flexible parts are slow to handle; parts that must be held, aligned with holes or tightened are slow to insert. A small screw takes 7–10 s to pick up and drive, while a snap-fit part with lead-in chamfers that aligns itself takes only four or five seconds.

The quality of the design is measured by the **assembly efficiency**, defined as

$$E = \frac{3\,N_{\min}}{t_{\mathrm{ma}}} \tag{6.7.1}$$

where t_ma is the manual assembly time of all the parts (s), and 3 s is the assembly time of an ideal part (easy to handle, easy to insert, no fastening). The closer E is to 1, the fewer the parts and the easier they are to assemble; for real products E is often 5%–30%.

### 6.7.4 Example: the rear cover assembly of a robot joint

**Example 6.7.1** The rear cover assembly of a joint of a small collaborative robot closes the encoder end. The original design has {{ex6_7.n0}} parts: aluminum cover, O-ring, 6 M3 screws and 6 washers, 2 dowel pins, magnet holder and magnet (for the magnetic encoder, turning with the motor shaft), cable gland and nameplate. Program 6.7.1 answers the three questions part by part:

- cover: must be removed to service the encoder (question 3): essential;
- O-ring: different material (rubber): essential;
- magnet holder: turns with the shaft, moving relative to the cover: essential; magnet: different material: essential;
- screws, washers: fasteners, three "no"s; dowel pins: used only for alignment, which a spigot on the cover can provide; cable gland: can be made a sealing sleeve on the cover; nameplate: can be laser-marked on the cover.

The theoretical minimum number of parts is Nmin = {{ex6_7.nmin0}}. Estimated with typical values from the manual assembly tables (values taken by this book, yet to be checked against the original tables), the total assembly time is {{ex6_7.t0:.0f}} s and the assembly efficiency E = {{ex6_7.e0:.1f}}%. The 6 screws and 6 washers alone take {{ex6_7.t_screws0:.0f}} s, {{ex6_7.share_fast0:.0f}}% of the total time.

Improved design (Figure 6.7.1b): the cover becomes an injection-molded part with snap fits, a spigot replaces the dowel pins, the cable sealing sleeve is integral with the cover, and the nameplate is laser-marked; the magnet is glued into the magnet holder by the supplier and arrives as one part; 2 M3 screws with thread-locking adhesive are kept to prevent the snap fits from loosening under vibration (whether they are essential is discussed in the task sheet). There are {{ex6_7.n1}} parts and Nmin = {{ex6_7.nmin1}} (the magnet assembly now counts as one part: the time to glue the magnet has not disappeared, but has moved to the supplier and is included in the price of the bought-in part). The assembly time is {{ex6_7.t1:.0f}} s, E = {{ex6_7.e1:.1f}}%, and {{ex6_7.t_saved:.0f}}% of the assembly time is saved.

At the rate of the digital factory's assembly station ({{ex6_7.labour}} USD/h, including depreciation of the station and energy), the assembly-station cost of each assembly falls from {{ex6_7.c0:.2f}} USD to {{ex6_7.c1:.2f}} USD; at an annual output of {{ex6_7.q_year}} joints, about {{ex6_7.save_h:.0f}} assembly hours and {{ex6_7.save_year:.0f}} USD are saved a year. This does not yet count the purchasing, inventory and inspection costs of the parts eliminated, which often exceed the assembly cost. The trade-offs are the cost of the injection mold (Section 6.4), the design and verification of the snap fits, and the loss of the aluminum cover's electromagnetic shielding and heat dissipation (a shielding layer must be added, or a thermally conductive plastic used); the larger the batch, the more worthwhile it is.

**Example 6.7.2** The output-shaft assembly SA-300 of the WQR-105: the bill of materials of the digital factory lists {{ex6_7.n_kinds_sa}} part types, {{ex6_7.n_sa}} parts in all: output shaft SH-301, large gear GR-302, two 6207 bearings and two parallel keys. The three questions: the shaft is the base part; the inner and outer rings of the bearings rotate relative to each other: essential. And the large gear? It does not move relative to the shaft; 40Cr and 45 steel are both steels, so it need not be "of a different material"; and it need not be separate for assembly: three "no"s, so in principle it could be made in one piece with the shaft (a gear shaft, as with a pinion of the WQR-105). The two keys also get three "no"s and could be replaced by an interference fit or splines. Nmin = {{ex6_7.nmin_sa}}. But "can be eliminated" does not mean "should be eliminated": the diameter of the large gear is much larger than that of the shaft, so a gear shaft would need a very thick forging with most of the material turned away, and hobbing would be awkward; a keyed joint is cheap, reliable and easy to dismantle, whereas an interference fit needs its own strength calculation and a press for disassembly (keys, splines and interference fits are treated in Chapter 25), and splines need special tools. DFA points out opportunities; whether to take them requires costing and checks of strength and manufacture.

### 6.7.5 Parts that are easy to assemble

Once the number of parts is reduced, the remaining parts must be easy to assemble:

1. have one base part, onto which the other parts are fitted from the same direction (preferably from above), without turning it over;
2. make parts as symmetric as possible (no need to find the orientation when picking them up); where symmetry is impossible, make them obviously asymmetric (the orientation is seen at a glance, which prevents fitting them the wrong way around);
3. provide lead-in chamfers and spigots so that parts align themselves;
4. use snap fits, interference fits and rivets instead of screws; where screws are unavoidable, use one size, with captive washers (screw-and-washer assemblies);
5. avoid parts that are flexible, tangle easily, or are too small or slippery; connect cables with plug connectors rather than wiring them on site;
6. parts should need no holding while being fitted, and be in position once fitted (no adjustment needed).

**Engineer's notes**

- A DFA analysis is best done at the concept stage, when changing the structure is cheapest. By the time the drawings are finished and the molds made, the number of parts is already fixed.
- When eliminating a part, ask: who takes over the function it served (locating, sealing, disassembly, identification, locking)? If nothing takes it over, that is not simplification but a hidden hazard.
- Fewer parts can make maintenance harder: how many times can a snap fit be undone? With the magnet glued into its holder, if one fails the whole set must be replaced. These costs should be written into the review documents.
