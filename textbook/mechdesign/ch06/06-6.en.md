### 6.6.1 A checklist of design rules

In describing each process, the previous sections listed some design rules. Collected together and classified by process, they form a checklist for **design for manufacture** (DFM). Its purpose is not "to make the part makeable" (almost anything can be made) but to have the part made by a suitable process, at low cost and consistently. Some principles apply to all processes:

1. **Simplify the shape**: use flat faces rather than curved ones, standard (purchased) parts rather than custom-made ones, profiles rather than milling from solid.
2. **Use standard features**: hole sizes to standard drill diameters, fillets to standard tool radii, standard threads, one inside radius for a sheet-metal part.
3. **Reduce set-ups and operations**: concentrate features in a few directions; make each part by a single process if possible.
4. **Set tolerances and roughness by function**: do not specify accuracy that is not needed, and do not machine faces that need no machining (Section 6.2).
5. **Respect the physics of the process**: uniform walls in castings and molded parts, holes far enough from bend lines in sheet metal, few overhangs in printed parts.
6. **Consult the manufacturer early in design**: the actual values of the rules vary with the factory's equipment, and only the manufacturer knows them.

DFM rules are a summary of experience: they tell you "where there may be a problem", not "that there is a problem". Wherever a rule is broken, either change the design or have a reason and confirm it with the manufacturer.

### 6.6.2 Checking a part process by process

**Example 6.6.1** Two designs of the XM540 servo bracket: a machined part (milled from 6061 aluminum block, walls 4 mm) and a sheet-metal part (5052 aluminum sheet, 2 mm). The designer first submitted initial designs, which are checked against the rules (Figure 6.6.1, Program 6.6.1).

The machined part, {{ex6_6.n_m}} rules:

- M1 inside corner radius: with the blank lying flat and the tool axis normal to the base plate, the inside corner between the two plates is a "floor-to-wall" corner, whose radius is set by the corner radius of the tool (bull-nose or ball-nose cutter). If the radius is one available in the tool library (assumed in this book at 0.5, 1, 2 and 4 mm), the corner is milled in one pass. The initial design showed R2.5, for which there is no stocked tool, so it could only be profiled layer by layer with a small ball-nose cutter, which is much slower. The revision changes it to R{{ex6_6.r_in_m:.0f}}. (A vertical inside corner in a pocket is another matter: its radius is that of the end mill, and should be at least about 1.2 times the tool radius so that the tool does not engage with its full periphery and chatter at the corner.)
- M2 wall thickness, M3 ratio of hole depth to diameter: both satisfied.
- M4 hole size: screw clearance holes are taken from GB/T 5277 (M2.5: fine 2.7, medium 2.9, coarse 3.1). The holes in the upright of the initial design were Ø2.6, only 0.1 mm larger than the screw, so that the slightest error in hole spacing would prevent the screws from passing through; the revision changes them to Ø2.7.
- M5 number of set-ups: the initial design had an extra counterbore on the side, needing three set-ups; the revision removes it and the part is finished in two.
- M6 tolerances: the initial design gave six dimensions ±0.02; in the revision only the group of hole spacings that locates the servo has a tight tolerance, the rest being to GB/T 1804-m.

The sheet-metal part, {{ex6_6.n_s}} rules:

- S1 inside bend radius: the initial design's R1 is less than the thickness; changed to R{{ex6_3.r:.0f}}.
- S2 minimum flange: not less than 4t = {{ex6_6.flange_min:.0f}} mm (Section 6.3). The initial design had a small 4 mm flange (to retain cables); the revision removes it and fixes the cables with cable ties instead.
- S3 hole to bend line: not less than 2t + r, which is {{ex6_6.hole_bend0:.0f}} mm for the initial design with r = 1 and {{ex6_6.hole_bend1:.0f}} mm for the revision with r = 2. The holes of the initial design were only 4 mm from the bend line; the revision moves them 10 mm away.
- S4 hole diameter not less than thickness: satisfied.
- S5 hole to edge: 3 mm in the initial design, less than 2t; changed to 6 mm.
- S6 one inside radius: satisfied.

Result: the initial machined part fails {{ex6_6.n_fail_m0}} rules and the initial sheet-metal part {{ex6_6.n_fail_s0}}; the revised machined part fails {{ex6_6.n_fail_m1}} and the revised sheet-metal part {{ex6_6.n_fail_s1}}. The revision does not change the function of the bracket at all; it merely turns something that "looks the same on the drawing" into something that "is cheap to make".

Rule checking can be programmed: write each rule as "quantity + criterion", read fillets, hole sizes, hole spacings and wall thicknesses from the three-dimensional model, and judge automatically. Most commercial CAD software includes DFM checking tools, and the AI design reviewer of the digital factory checks part drawings in the same way (Chapter 3). A program can check only what has been "written as a rule"; problems outside the rules, such as whether a part should exist at all or could be merged with another, need the method of the next section.

**Engineer's notes**

- Every factory, for every class of process, should have its own table of DFM rules (with values and sources). Newcomers read it before they draw, reviews check against it rule by rule, and it is updated with the equipment.
- The difference between an initial design and its revision is often the difference between an experienced designer and a beginner. Add every problem found in a review to the rule table; only then does experience stay in the organization.
