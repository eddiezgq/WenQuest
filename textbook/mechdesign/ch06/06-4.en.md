### 6.4.1 Injection molding

**Injection molding** heats plastic pellets until they melt in the barrel and injects the melt under high pressure with a screw into the cavity of a steel mold; when the part has cooled and solidified, the mold opens and the part is ejected. A cycle takes from about ten seconds to a minute, a mold can have several cavities, and the shape of the part is almost unrestricted: housings, gears, snap fits, handles. Robot covers, teach-pendant housings and small plastic gears are injection-molded. The catch is the mold: a steel mold costs from a few thousand to several hundred thousand dollars and takes weeks to months to make, so injection molding pays only when the batch is large enough (Section 6.4.3).

Design rules for injection-molded parts:

1. **Uniform walls.** This is the most important rule. Thick places cool slowly and shrink more, causing depressions on the surface (sink marks), voids inside and warping of the part. Common wall thicknesses are 1.5–3 mm; where reinforcement is needed, use ribs, not thicker walls.
2. **Ribs.** Rib thickness 0.5–0.6 times the wall thickness; if thicker, sink marks appear on the other side. Rib height not more than about 3 times the wall thickness; if higher, the rib does not fill and is hard to eject.
3. **Draft.** Faces parallel to the direction of mold opening are given a draft of 0.5°–2°, more for textured faces.
4. **Fillets.** Inside corner fillets of at least about 0.5 times the wall thickness, to reduce stress concentration and help the melt flow.
5. **Bosses** (screw bosses). Make them hollow and tie them to the side walls with ribs, rather than making them solid lumps.
6. **Snap fits.** One of the greatest strengths of injection-molded parts: the part carries its own fastening, without screws (Section 6.7). The cantilever of a snap fit must be designed for the allowable strain, and must release in the direction of mold opening (otherwise a side core is needed, and the mold becomes expensive).
7. **Avoid undercuts.** Projections and recesses normal to the direction of mold opening (side holes, side recesses) need side cores or lifters, making the mold complex and expensive; change the shape if you can.

### 6.4.2 Cooling time: why walls should be thin and uniform

Most of the injection molding cycle is spent cooling. Treating the wall as a plate cooled by the mold from both faces, the time for the center of the melt to fall to a temperature at which the part can be ejected is given by the solution of one-dimensional heat conduction (taking the first term of the series):

$$t_c = \frac{s^2}{\pi^2 \alpha}\ln\!\left[\frac{4}{\pi}\cdot\frac{T_m - T_w}{T_e - T_w}\right] \tag{6.4.1}$$

where s is the wall thickness, α the thermal diffusivity of the plastic, and Tm, Tw and Te are the melt temperature, the mold temperature and the ejection temperature.

If a part is thick in just one place, the whole part must wait for the cooling time of that place, and the hourly output of the mold drops accordingly. This is the same principle as Chvorinov's rule for castings (Section 6.1): the solidification (cooling) time is proportional to the square of the "thickness".

### 6.4.4 Die casting

Die casting is similar to injection molding, except that the plastic is replaced by molten aluminum, zinc or magnesium alloy and the die is made of hot-work tool steel. The walls of aluminum die castings can be 2–3 mm, with high accuracy and a good surface, which suits housings in large batches: robot joint housings, motor end shields, small reducer housings. The design rules are also close to those for injection molding: uniform walls, draft and fillets, ribs for reinforcement. The differences are that die castings often contain fine porosity, so they cannot be strengthened by heat treatment (the pores would blister) and are not suitable for welding; machining may expose pores on the machined faces, a point to watch on sealing faces. Die-casting dies are more expensive than injection molds and have a shorter life (tens of thousands to a hundred thousand or more shots), so the economic batch size of die casting is larger.

**Engineer's notes**

- The drawing of a plastic part must state the material grade (including color and whether it is glass-filled); the shrinkage allowance is set by the mold maker according to the material. Dimensional tolerances should be specified by the tolerance grades for plastic parts in GB/T 14486, not borrowed from metal parts.
- Run a mold-flow analysis (filling, packing, cooling, warpage) before the mold is made: changing wall thicknesses and gate positions at the design stage is far cheaper than modifying a steel mold after trials.
