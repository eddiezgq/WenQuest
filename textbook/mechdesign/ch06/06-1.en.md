### 6.1.1 Two ways of forming

There are two ways of making a metal part of complex shape "in one go". One is to melt the metal and pour it into a mold, where it solidifies in the shape of the part: **casting**. The other is to heat the metal to a temperature at which it is very plastic and press it into the required shape with a hammer or a press: **forging**. Casting can produce parts of very complex shape (housings, machine bases, pump casings); forged parts are simpler in shape but strong and tough (crankshafts, connecting rods, gear blanks, flanges). Both usually produce a **blank**, whose mating faces and holes must then be machined (Section 6.2).

The housing of the WQR-105 and the bearing blocks of the conveyor roller of Chapter 3 are HT200 gray-iron castings; the blank of the large gear GR-302 is a 40Cr forging (Chapter 5).

### 6.1.2 Casting processes

- **Sand casting**: a mold cavity is formed in molding sand with a wooden pattern (or a metal pattern; alternatively the sand mold is 3D-printed); after pouring, the sand mold is broken up and the casting removed. There is no limit on material or size, the tooling is cheap, and single parts and small batches are feasible; dimensional accuracy and surface quality are low, and machining allowances must be left. Housings, machine bases and bearing blocks are mostly sand-cast.
- **Die casting** (pressure die casting): molten aluminum, zinc or magnesium alloy is forced at high speed into a steel die, taking a few seconds to a few tens of seconds per part. Accuracy is high, the walls can be very thin and the surface is smooth, but the steel dies are expensive, so it suits large batches (Section 6.4).
- **Investment casting** (lost-wax casting): a wax model of the part is coated with refractory material, the wax is melted out by heating, and the one-piece shell left behind is filled with metal. It can make small parts of complex shape and fairly high accuracy, in materials including steels and superalloys, such as turbine blades, valve bodies and the stainless-steel parts of robot grippers.

### 6.1.3 Design rules for castings

Most defects of castings (shrinkage cavities, shrinkage porosity, cracks, misruns) are caused by the design, not by the foundry. The designer should observe several rules:

1. **Keep the walls as uniform as possible.** Thick places solidify later; as the surrounding metal, which solidifies first, shrinks, it "draws" liquid from them, finally leaving a shrinkage cavity at their center. Large differences in thickness also make the shrinkage uneven during cooling, producing internal stress or even cracks.
2. **Avoid hot spots.** Where two walls meet, at bosses, and in the corners where walls join the base plate, the local mass of metal is thicker than the walls on either side: this is a **hot spot**, which solidifies last and is most prone to shrinkage cavities. Remedies are to core it out (cast in a recess or hole), to make walls meet in staggered joints rather than crossing, and to round the outside of corners as well (avoiding accumulation of metal at the inside fillet).
3. **Draft.** Walls parallel to the direction of draw are given a draft of 1°–3°, so that the pattern can be drawn from the sand mold (the same applies to die casting and injection molding).
4. **Fillets.** Round both inside and outside corners, to avoid stress concentration, cracks and sand falling from the mold at sharp corners; but too large an inside fillet creates a new hot spot.
5. **Parting line.** The mold must be split in two to remove the pattern; the parting surface is best a single plane, and important faces should not straddle it (they would be offset by mold shift).
6. **Minimum wall thickness.** Metal cannot reach the end of a wall that is too thin (misrun). For gray iron in sand molds the wall is generally not less than 3–5 mm; the exact value depends on the size of the casting and the material, and should be obtained from a handbook or from the foundry.

There is a simple rule for judging where the hot spots are and how long solidification takes, Chvorinov's rule: the solidification time t is proportional to the square of the **solidification modulus** M (the ratio of the volume of the casting to its cooling surface area):

$$t = B\,M^2 \tag{6.1.1}$$

B depends on the metal, the mold material and the pouring temperature. For a large flat plate of thickness s, M ≈ s/2; for a complex section, the local modulus can be approximated by the radius of the largest inscribed circle at each point of the section, and the place where the inscribed circle is largest is the hot spot.

### 6.1.4 Forging

Forging presses metal into shape at high temperature, and at the same time breaks up and consolidates the coarse as-cast structure, so a forged part is much stronger and tougher than a casting of the same material. More important still is the **grain flow**: the metal forms a fibrous structure along the direction of deformation, and the strength and fatigue strength are higher along the flow lines. A good forging makes the flow lines follow the outline of the part; in a crankshaft, for example, they go around every corner. If the same shape is machined from bar, the flow lines are cut through.

- **Open-die forging**: the metal is shaped by repeated blows between flat anvils or with simple tools, without special dies; it suits single parts and large parts, with large allowances and low accuracy.
- **Closed-die forging**: the metal is pressed into shape in the cavity of a pair of dies, which guarantee shape and dimensions; it suits batch production, but the dies are expensive and their cost must be spread over the batch.

The design rules for die forgings are similar to those for castings: the parting line (best a plane, at the largest cross-section of the part), the forging draft (generally 5°–7° on the outside walls of steel parts, larger than for castings), fillets (if too small the metal will not flow in and the dies crack easily), and avoiding ribs that are too thin and holes that are too deep.

### 6.1.5 How to choose: casting, forging, or profiles and bar

| Requirement | Preferred route |
|---|---|
| Complex shape, internal cavities (housings, pump casings) | Casting |
| Alternating or impact loads, high demands (crankshafts, connecting rods, heavy-duty gear blanks) | Forging |
| Shafts and sleeves of simple shape, small batches | Machining directly from bar |
| Small aluminum or zinc parts of complex shape in large batches | Die casting |
| Single parts, urgent, complex shape | Sand casting (3D-printed sand molds) or additive manufacturing (Section 6.5) |

**Engineer's notes**

- Drawings of castings and forgings must state the dimensional tolerances of unmachined surfaces (GB/T 6414, dimensional tolerances of castings), the machining allowances, the draft and the general (unspecified) fillet radii; if these are not clear, the foundry can only guess.
- The datum of a casting should be chosen on an unmachined surface (for locating in the first machining operation), and transferred to machined surfaces afterward.
- Talk to the foundry before designing a casting: they know how thin a wall and how large a part their equipment can make.
