### 6.2.1 The basic machining operations

**Machining** removes surplus metal from a blank with cutting tools to obtain the required shape, dimensions and surface. It can work almost any metal with high accuracy, and is the main method for mating faces, holes and threads. The basic operations are:

- **Turning**: the workpiece rotates and the turning tool moves axially and radially, producing surfaces of revolution: outside diameters, end faces, bores, threads, tapers. Shaft-type parts (SH-301) are made mainly by turning.
- **Milling**: the tool rotates and the workpiece (or the tool) moves, producing flat faces, steps, slots, pockets and all kinds of three-dimensional surfaces. Keyways, the flat faces of housings, the aluminum-alloy links of robots and the servo bracket (Sections 6.6 and 6.8) are all milled. A modern vertical machining center changes tools automatically and can mill, drill and tap in a single set-up.
- **Drilling**: a drill makes a hole in solid material, with fairly low accuracy; holes with higher requirements are then core-drilled, reamed or bored.
- **Grinding**: a grinding wheel rotating at high speed removes a very thin layer; it is used for surfaces requiring high accuracy and low roughness, and for hard surfaces after hardening. Bearing seats and seal journals are generally ground.

The designer needs to know how high an accuracy each method can reach "economically": the **economic machining accuracy** is the accuracy that can be held consistently under normal conditions (suitable equipment, qualified workers and normal operation times) without spending extra time. If the accuracy required exceeds it, another method or an extra operation is needed, and the cost jumps up a level.

### 6.2.2 Economic accuracy and roughness

The economic accuracy table of the digital factory (`factory/digital/std/econ_accuracy.yaml`, with the source of every row stated) gives the IT grades and roughness ranges of the common methods of machining outside diameters and holes (Figure 6.2.1b): rough turning reaches only IT11–IT13, semi-finish turning IT8–IT10, finish turning IT7–IT8 with Ra 0.8–1.6 μm; drilling IT11–IT13, core drilling IT10–IT11.

Compare the requirements of SH-301 (Chapter 4). General dimensions are to class m of GB/T 1804 (roughly IT14 in this size range), for which rough turning is enough; the seal journal is f9, requiring semi-finish to finish turning; the two bearing seats Ø35k6, the gear seat Ø40r6 and the shaft extension Ø30k6 are all IT6, beyond the range of finish turning, and must be ground. That is why the routing of SH-301 contains "cylindrical grinding". Fine (diamond) turning in the table also reaches IT6, but it is used for non-ferrous metals and cannot machine steel parts. The economic accuracy of grinding is not yet in the digital factory's table (so far only one source has been found, and the rule is that two sources must agree before a value is entered); general handbooks give about IT6 for external cylindrical grinding.

The conclusion for the designer is simple: do not specify accuracy that is not needed. Specifying IT6 for a dimension that needs only IT9 adds a grinding operation and costs money. Conversely, a face to be ground must have a relief groove for the wheel (Chapter 33), and a ground shoulder cannot have a sharp corner.

### 6.2.3 Time and cost: the routing of SH-301

**Example 6.2.1** The routing of the digital factory divides the machining of SH-301 into {{ex6_2.n_ops}} operations: sawing (cutting-off), rough turning, quenching and tempering, finish turning, keyway milling, cylindrical grinding and inspection, each with a standard time (min) and a station. The processing cost of an operation = time × hourly rate of the station (depreciation + labor + energy; CNC lathe {{ex6_2.rate_cnc:.0f}} USD/h, cylindrical grinder {{ex6_2.rate_grd:.0f}} USD/h). Program 6.2.1 calls the same function as the standard costing of the digital factory and finds, per SH-301: material cost {{ex6_2.mat:.2f}} USD, processing cost {{ex6_2.ops:.2f}} USD, total {{ex6_2.total:.2f}} USD; total time {{ex6_2.t_total}} min (Figure 6.2.1a). The processing cost is more than ten times the material cost: for a small shaft like this, saving material means almost nothing, while saving operations and time is what counts. Cylindrical grinding alone costs {{ex6_2.grind:.1f}} USD; it grinds four IT6 surfaces; each surface taken off the list saves part of that time, and if none of the four needed IT6 the whole operation would be saved.

**Example 6.2.2** The routing records {{ex6_2.t_rough}} min for rough turning. How much of that is actual cutting? SH-301 (revision A) is turned from Ø{{ex6_2.D0:.0f}} bar to {{ex6_2.n_seg}} steps, removing a volume of {{ex6_2.V_cm3:.0f}} cm³. Taking usual cutting conditions for rough turning 45 steel, cutting speed {{ex6_2.vc:.0f}} m/min, feed {{ex6_2.f:.1f}} mm/r and depth of cut {{ex6_2.ap:.1f}} mm, the metal removal rate = v·f·ap = {{ex6_2.mrr:.1f}} cm³/min, and the pure cutting time is only {{ex6_2.t_cut:.1f}} min, {{ex6_2.cut_share:.0f}}% of the routing time. The rest is spent on loading, tool setting, tool changing, idle travel and measuring (the routing times are illustrative values for teaching and also include an allocation of set-up time).

This shows two things. First, machining time cannot be estimated from the cutting time alone; the auxiliary time is often the larger part. Second, reducing the number of set-ups in the design (features concentrated in a few directions, so that they can be machined in one set-up) and reducing tool changes (uniform hole sizes and fillets) save more time than reducing the amount of cutting (Section 6.6).

### 6.2.4 What the designer should do for machining

1. Separate machined from unmachined surfaces, and keep machined surfaces as few and as small as possible (for example, only the two end pads of the bearing-block underside are machined, Chapter 3);
2. let the tool reach the machined surfaces and run out of them: threads need an undercut, ground surfaces a relief groove, and the bottom of a drilled blind hole is conical (the drill point angle) and must not be drawn flat (unless a special flat-bottom drill is to be used);
3. make the sizes of holes, slots and fillets as uniform as possible, using standard tools (standard drill diameters, standard cutter radii);
4. arrange for the features of a part to be machined from as few directions as possible, to reduce set-ups;
5. set accuracy and roughness by function, not on the principle "the higher the better" (Chapter 4).

For cutting conditions, tools, fixtures and process planning, see *Manufacturing Technology*.

**Engineer's notes**

- To estimate roughly what a part costs, first count the operations and set-ups, then see whether there are "expensive operations" such as grinding and heat treatment; this is quicker and more accurate than computing cutting times.
- Dimensions whose tolerances are tight enough to need grinding can be spotted at a glance on a drawing (IT6 or finer, Ra below 0.8 μm); go over them specifically in drawing reviews.
