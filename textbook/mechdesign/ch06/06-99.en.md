Design determines which processes a part uses, how many operations it needs and how many parts must be assembled, and so determines most of the cost. Casting can make complex internal cavities, and forging makes parts strong and tough; castings need uniform walls and no hot spots. The inscribed-circle diameter at the hot spot in the corner of the bearing block is {{ex6_1.d_ratio0:.1f}} times the thickness of the base plate, and a cast-in process pocket brings it down to {{ex6_1.d_ratio1:.1f}} times. Machining is accurate, but tolerances must be specified with the economic machining accuracy in mind: the bearing seats, gear seat and shaft extension of SH-301 are all IT6, beyond the range of finish turning, and must be ground; of the {{ex6_2.total:.2f}} USD per part, the processing cost far exceeds the material cost, and the pure cutting time of rough turning is only {{ex6_2.cut_share:.0f}}% of the routing time. Sheet-metal parts are developed along the neutral layer; simply adding the outside lengths of the servo bracket makes the blank {{ex6_3.err_naive:.1f}} mm too long, and holes must be at least 2t + r from the bend line. The cooling time of injection molding is proportional to the square of the wall thickness, and the mold cost must be spread over the batch: the joint cover is cheaper by injection molding than by laser sintering from {{ex6_4.q_im_sls}} parts on. Additive manufacturing needs no molds and complex shapes cost nothing extra; it suits prototypes, small batches and integrated parts.

DFM writes the experience of each process into rules and checks them one by one: the initial design of the servo bracket violated {{ex6_6.n_fail_m0}} rules as a machined part and {{ex6_6.n_fail_s0}} as a sheet-metal part, and the revisions satisfy all of them. DFA uses three questions to find parts that can be eliminated, and measures the design by the assembly efficiency E = 3Nmin/t: the joint rear cover assembly goes from {{ex6_7.n0}} parts to {{ex6_7.n1}}, the assembly time is cut by {{ex6_7.t_saved:.0f}}%, and E rises from {{ex6_7.e0:.0f}}% to {{ex6_7.e1:.0f}}%. Cost = material cost + processing cost + set-up cost / batch size: for one servo bracket {{ex6_8.best1}} is the cheapest, from {{ex6_8.q_sp}} parts on sheet metal is the cheapest, and milling is always the most expensive for this part; lead time must be reckoned separately, including queuing and transport.

### Historical notes

Casting has a long history in China. In the Shang dynasty bronzes were cast by the piece-mold method; the Houmuwu *ding* weighs more than eight hundred kilograms and required several furnaces pouring at once. When the lost-wax method (investment casting) appeared in China is still debated among scholars, but it is generally held to be no later than the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods. The *Tiangong Kaiwu* (The Exploitation of the Works of Nature) of Song Yingxing in the Ming dynasty describes the casting of bells and cauldrons and the forging of metal, and is one of the earliest works in the world to record manufacturing processes systematically.

The hallmark of modern mechanical manufacturing is "interchangeable parts": at the beginning of the 19th century American armories began to produce interchangeable firearm parts with jigs, gauges and special-purpose machine tools, which developed into the "American system of manufactures"; at the beginning of the 20th century Ford's assembly line carried it into mass production. F. W. Taylor published "On the Art of Cutting Metals" in 1906, establishing from years of experiments the relation between cutting speed and tool life, and laying the foundation for cutting conditions and time standards. Around 1940 Chvorinov in Czechoslovakia proposed the rule that solidification time is proportional to the square of the modulus. Injection molding originated in the injection machine invented in the 1870s by the Hyatt brothers (J. W. Hyatt) for celluloid, and after the screw injection molding machine came into general use in the mid-20th century it became the main process for plastic parts.

Design for assembly was first studied by Boothroyd at the University of Massachusetts in the 1970s; working with Dewhurst, he published an assembly design handbook in 1983, which was then turned into software. In the 1980s several large American companies used it to redesign their products and greatly reduce part counts, which made DFA and DFM widely known ([BDK]). Additive manufacturing began in the 1980s: C. Hull's stereolithography patent (granted in 1986), C. Deckard's laser sintering at the University of Texas, and S. Crump's fused deposition modeling (patent filed in 1989); over the last twenty years metal printing has entered structural applications in aircraft, medical devices and robots.
