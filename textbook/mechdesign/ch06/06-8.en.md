### 6.8.1 What makes up the cost of a part

The manufacturing cost of a part can be divided into three components:

- **Material cost**: the mass of the blank × its unit price, plus losses to chips, offcuts and scrap. The blank of a milled part may weigh several times as much as the part, a sheet-metal part leaves offcuts after nesting, and a printed part leaves powder that cannot be recycled.
- **Processing cost**: the processing time per part × the hourly rate of the equipment (including labor). The time includes the cutting (or printing, or injection) time and the auxiliary time per part for loading, unloading, measuring and so on.
- **Set-up cost** (fixed cost): costs incurred per batch or once only, regardless of how many parts are made: programming, tool setting, machine set-up, building fixtures and molds, first-article inspection. It is spread over the parts, and the larger the batch, the less each part bears.

$$C(q) = C_{\mathrm{mat}} + t_{\mathrm{run}}R + \frac{C_{\mathrm{setup}}}{q} \tag{6.8.1}$$

Here q is the **batch size** and R the rate. The proportions of the three components differ greatly between processes: 3D printing has almost no set-up cost, but the printing time and material of each part are expensive; injection molding, die casting and stamping have very high tooling costs but take only seconds per part; milling lies in between. The batch size at which the cost curves of two processes cross is the **break-even batch size**; below it one process is used, above it the other.

The conclusions must be read together with their premises: the three brackets are not exactly the same. The milled part is the stiffest and most accurate, and can take the torque and shocks of the servo directly; the stiffness of the sheet-metal part depends on its bend and thickness, and hole positions across the bend line are less accurate; the printed part is the most flexible and creeps (Chapter 5). Cost is only one aspect of the choice, to be decided together with the materials selection of Chapter 5 and the strength and stiffness calculations of later chapters. At the prototype stage, printing one is quickest and cheapest; for a few dozen to a hundred or so, sheet metal is a good choice for this part; milling is worthwhile only when high accuracy and stiffness are required, or when the shape cannot be bent from sheet.

### 6.8.3 Methods of cost estimation and their limits

The approach above is a bottom-up estimate (summed operation by operation): the time and rate of each operation are worked out and added up, which is how the standard cost of the digital factory (Section 6.2) is calculated. It needs a routing, and suits a design that is already fairly detailed. At the concept stage coarser methods are common: analogy with similar parts (historical costs of parts of similar mass, size and complexity), or empirical formulas (such as cost ∝ mass × material factor × complexity factor).

Whatever the method, its limits must be known:

1. **What the rate includes**: the rates in this chapter are depreciation of equipment + labor + energy, excluding plant, overhead and profit, which must be added for an external quotation.
2. **Lead time is not working time**: Figure 6.8.1b counts only the working time of equipment and labor, without queuing, transport, inspection or rework. Actual delivery times are often several times the working time.
3. **How one-off costs are spread**: whether programming and molds are charged to the first batch or to the whole life of the product makes a great difference to the conclusion.
4. **Values change**: rates and material prices vary with region and year; the teaching values of this chapter can only illustrate the method and the trends.

**Engineer's notes**

- When comparing two processes, first plot the curves of "cost per part against batch size" and find the crossover, then ask yourself: is the expected batch size far from the crossover? If it is, the conclusion is robust; if it is close, refine the model or ask two suppliers for quotations.
- The purpose of estimating cost at the design stage is to compare several options, not to compute one figure accurately. Relative comparisons are much more reliable than absolute values.
