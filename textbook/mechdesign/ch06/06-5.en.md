### 6.5.1 "Growing" a part layer by layer

**Additive manufacturing** (popularly, 3D printing) slices the three-dimensional model of a part into very thin layers and builds the part directly by depositing, bonding or melting material layer upon layer, with no molds and no special tooling. ISO/ASTM 52900 divides it into seven process categories, of which the following four are common for mechanical parts:

- **Fused deposition modeling** (material extrusion, FDM/FFF): plastic filament is heated and extruded, tracing each layer along a path. The machines are cheap and the range of materials is wide (PLA, ABS, PETG, nylon, carbon-fiber-reinforced nylon); it is the most widely used process in laboratories and for prototypes. The layer lines are visible and the bond between layers is relatively weak.
- **Vat photopolymerization** (SLA, DLP): liquid resin is cured layer by layer with ultraviolet light. The surface is smooth and the detail fine, suiting appearance models and small, delicate parts; the resins are rather brittle, with poor heat resistance and aging resistance.
- **Selective laser sintering** (powder bed fusion, SLS): a laser sinters nylon (PA12, PA11) powder layer by layer. No supports are needed (the surrounding powder holds the part up), and the parts have good strength and toughness, suiting functional parts in small batches: gripper fingers, cable clips, covers, brackets (Section 6.8).
- Metal **powder bed fusion** (SLM, LPBF): a laser fully melts metal powder (stainless steel, aluminum alloys, titanium alloys, tool steels). It can make internal channels, lattice structures and topology-optimized lightweight parts that machining cannot; machine cost and cost per part are high, supports are needed, and after printing the parts must be stress-relieved, have their supports removed and their mating faces machined.

### 6.5.2 Accuracy and surface

The accuracy of printed parts is generally of the order of ±0.1–0.3 mm (depending on process, size and orientation), and the surface is rough, with stair-stepped layer lines most visible on sloping faces. Mating faces, bearing bores and threads are generally machined after printing, or the design leaves machining allowances or uses inserts such as heat-set nuts. The strength of printed parts depends on direction: it differs greatly along and across the layers, especially for fused deposition parts. This was calculated in Section 5.6 (the gripper finger must be printed flat, so that the bending stress runs along the print path).

### 6.5.3 Design rules

1. **Overhangs and supports.** For fused deposition and metal printing, overhanging faces should generally make an angle of not less than about 45° with the horizontal, otherwise supports are needed; supports must be removed and leave a rough surface. The top of a horizontal round hole sags; it can be changed to a teardrop or diamond shape. Laser sintering needs no supports.
2. **Minimum wall thickness and minimum feature size.** These depend on the process: for fused deposition about 2–3 times the nozzle diameter, for laser sintering around 0.8–1 mm. Walls that are too thin cannot be printed, or break at a touch.
3. **Print orientation.** It determines strength (Section 5.6), accuracy (dimensional accuracy and roundness are poorer normal to the layers), the amount of support and the print time (the taller the part, the more layers), all of which must be weighed together.
4. **Powder and support removal.** Powder cannot escape from a closed cavity, so drain holes must be provided; supports inside internal channels cannot be removed, so the channels must be designed at self-supporting angles.
5. **Integration.** The greatest advantage of printing is that complex shapes cost nothing extra: several parts can be combined into one (snap fits, hinges and cable channels printed together), which is exactly what design for assembly asks for (Section 6.7).
6. **Thermal distortion and residual stress.** In metal printing, large flat areas and abrupt changes of thickness tend to warp and crack; the orientation and supports should be decided together with the printing service provider.

### 6.5.4 When to use it

Additive manufacturing needs no molds, has short preparation times, and complex shapes cost nothing extra; but the printing time per part is long, the materials are expensive, and the unit price hardly falls as the batch grows (the cost curves of Sections 6.4 and 6.8). It therefore suits:

- prototypes and design verification: parts in a few days, and another print after a design change;
- functional parts in small batches and many variants: robot end effectors, gripper fingers, jigs and fixtures, inspection gauges;
- shapes that machining and casting cannot make, or make only at great cost: internal channels, lattice structures, topology-optimized parts, integrated assemblies;
- spare parts and parts no longer in production.

It does not suit simple parts in large batches (sheet metal, injection molding and die casting are cheaper), parts that need high accuracy and surface quality but cannot be post-machined, or load-bearing parts whose material properties must be strictly guaranteed while the process is not yet qualified. **Engineer's notes**

- Before handing a part to someone else to print, state in the model (or in an attached note) the printing process, material, orientation, layer thickness, infill and which faces are to be post-machined; if these are not clear, the same model printed at two different shops gives two different parts.
- For hole and shaft fits on printed parts, first print a test piece (one of each of several clearances) and try the fit, then fix the dimensions.
