### 6.3.1 Sheet metal: cut it out, bend it up

A **sheet metal** part is made from thin metal sheet (generally 0.5–6 mm) by blanking and bending (sometimes also drawing, flanging and welding): cabinets, control boxes, brackets, guards. It is light, cheap and quick to make, and many of the electrical boxes of robots, the bodies of mobile robots and small servo brackets are sheet-metal parts.

- **Blanking**: for small batches, laser cutting or CNC turret punching cuts the developed outline and the holes under program control, without tooling; for large batches a blanking die (one press stroke per part) is used, which must be made first.
- **Bending**: on a press brake an upper tool (punch) presses the sheet into the V-groove of the lower die to bend it to an angle. For large batches progressive dies blank and bend in succession on a press.

### 6.3.2 Flat length: bend allowance and K-factor

When sheet is bent, the outside is stretched and the inside compressed, and one layer in between keeps its length: the **neutral layer**. The flat length must be calculated along the neutral layer. The neutral layer is not at mid-thickness but shifted toward the inside; its distance from the inside surface is written Kt, and K is called the **K-factor** (between 0 and 0.5; for soft aluminum with an inside radius about equal to the thickness, 0.33–0.4 is common, and each shop measures it for its own sheet and tooling). For a bend of angle θ (in radians) and inside radius r, the arc length of the neutral layer, the **bend allowance**, is

$$BA = \theta\,(r + K t) \tag{6.3.1}$$

Flat length = the lengths of the straight legs (up to the start of each bend) + the bend allowances.

### 6.3.3 Design rules for sheet metal

1. **Inside bend radius not less than the thickness.** This holds for aluminum and low-carbon steel; high-strength aluminum alloys and stainless steel need more. If the radius is too small the outside cracks. Use the same inside radius for all the bends of a part as far as possible, to reduce tool changes.
2. **Flanges must not be too short.** If a flange is too short, the V-groove of the lower die cannot support the sheet. The exact minimum flange is set by the width of the die opening V (about V/2 + t); common guidelines take at least 4t ({{ex6_3.min_flange:.0f}} mm in this example).
3. **Holes must be far enough from the bend line.** The edge of a hole should be at least about 2t + r from the bend line ({{ex6_3.hole_edge:.0f}} mm in this example), or the hole will be pulled oval; where it must be closer, cut a relief hole or slot on the bend line first.
4. **Hole diameter not less than the thickness, and hole edge to sheet edge not less than about 2t** (so that the sheet does not tear or distort when punched).
5. Cut a **bend relief** on both sides of a bend to prevent tearing; arrange bend lines perpendicular to the rolling direction of the sheet where possible.
6. Dimension from a single datum, be consistent about whether outside or inside dimensions are given, and state which governs.

These are all rules of thumb (from the design guides of sheet-metal shops, and handbooks such as Bralla's *Design for Manufacturability Handbook*); the values vary with the material and the equipment, so ask the shop before using them. Section 6.6 uses them to check the first design of the bracket and its revision rule by rule.

### 6.3.4 Welded structures

Plates or profiles welded together can produce shapes that cannot be bent from a single sheet: frames, bases, the body frames of mobile robots. Welded structures need no tooling and are easy to modify, which makes them very economical for single parts and small batches; but welding distorts, so welded parts often have to be straightened and stress-relieved, and important mating faces are machined after welding. In design, note that the welding torch must be able to reach the weld; welds should be kept away from the regions of highest stress and from faces to be machined; welds should be arranged as symmetrically as possible to reduce distortion; and crossing welds should be avoided. The strength calculation of welds is in Chapter 26, and their fatigue is treated in the same chapter.

### 6.3.5 Profiles and profile frames

Extruded aluminum profiles (square sections with T-slots), with standard corner brackets, bolts and connectors, can be assembled into frames, guards, test benches and the bases of robot workcells without welding or machining (they need only be sawn to length). They are easy to modify and suit prototypes, special-purpose equipment and small batches. The drawbacks are that the joints are less stiff than welds or one-piece castings, and that a large number of corner brackets adds noticeably to the cost; mounting faces requiring high accuracy need additional machined plates. Hot-rolled channels, angles and square tubes are another kind of profile, mostly used for welded frames.

**Engineer's notes**

- The drawing of a sheet-metal part must state the thickness, material and inside bend radius, and which side governs the dimensions. The flat pattern is normally generated by CAD software from the K-factor or bend allowance table supplied by the shop; do not cut blanks from a hand calculation.
- Accuracy of sheet-metal parts: laser-cut hole positions are very accurate, but dimensions across a bend line are less so after bending (a few tenths of a millimeter). Where two holes must line up precisely, put them on the same flat piece if possible.
