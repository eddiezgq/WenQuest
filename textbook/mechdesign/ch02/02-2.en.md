### 2.2.1 The problem

The Unitree Go2 is a quadruped robot (parts library [[part:B-LEG-GO2]]): it weighs {{ex2_2.weight}} kg, has a rated payload of {{ex2_2.payload}} kg, stands about {{ex2_2.height}} mm tall, measures {{ex2_2.footprint}} mm overall, and its battery lasts two to four hours. Users would like it to walk back to its charging dock by itself after a patrol and charge without anyone plugging it in. How should the docking mechanism on the charging dock be designed?

Drawing a particular structure right at the start tends to trap you in the first concept that comes to mind. Engineers first ask not "what does it look like?" but "what does it have to do?".

### 2.2.2 The function structure

**Functional decomposition** breaks the overall function into a number of subfunctions, each stating clearly "what goes in and what comes out", written only as a verb plus a noun and without mentioning any particular structure. The overall function of the docking mechanism is "connect the robot to the dock electrically and reliably", which can be decomposed into {{ex2_2.n_func}} subfunctions:

1. approach the dock (the robot returns from a distance to the vicinity of the dock);
2. absorb the position error (the position and attitude in which the robot stops always deviate somewhat, and it must be "guided" into the exact position);
3. make electrical contact;
4. hold contact (it must not come loose during the hour or two of charging);
5. protect the contacts (an outdoor dock must keep out dust and water).

Strictly speaking, "approach the dock" is a function of the robot and not part of the dock; it is listed here because the dock can help the robot with markers, beacons or guide strips. There is also a signal function that must not be forgotten: confirm that the robot is in position and the contacts are made before energizing the contacts. This is a safety requirement (Section 1.8): otherwise the contacts on the dock would be live all the time.

A diagram of the subfunctions with the flows of energy, material and signals between them is the **function structure** ([PB] Chapter 6). The function structure breaks one large problem into several small ones, each of which can be solved independently.

### 2.2.3 The morphological matrix

Each row of the **morphological matrix** is a subfunction and each column a solution for that subfunction; choosing one solution from each row makes up a concept (Figure 2.2.1). For the five subfunctions of the docking mechanism, {{ex2_2.opts}} solutions are listed respectively, which in principle combine into {{ex2_2.n_all}} concepts.

**Example 2.2.1** Some combinations are incompatible: a plug and socket needs a very small locating error before insertion, which only precise locating such as a taper pin can provide; a V-shaped guide, a floating plate or magnetic self-centering is not good enough. Ruling out these combinations leaves {{ex2_2.n_ok}} concepts. Other combinations work but contain something superfluous; for example, inductive charging needs no contact pressure, so adding a mechanical latch is unnecessary. Such combinations are not ruled out but lose points at evaluation. {{ex2_2.n_ok}} concepts are still far too many to design each in detail, so from them the review team picks, by experience, {{ex2_2.n_concepts}} representative concepts (Figure 2.2.1):

- **A Lie-down with spring-loaded pins.** Navigation by visual marker; a V-shaped guide absorbs the error; the robot lies down and its own weight presses the contacts on its belly onto spring-loaded contact pins (pogo pins) on the dock.
- **B Floating plate with blade contacts.** A floating contact plate absorbs the error; blade contacts slide in to make contact; a permanent magnet holds the contact closed.
- **C Inductive pad.** The robot walks onto an inductive pad and lies down; no exposed contacts are needed.
- **D Taper pin and plug.** A floor guide strip leads the robot into position; after a taper pin has located it precisely, a plug extends into a socket and a mechanical latch locks it.

These four concepts are evaluated in Section 2.4.

The strength of the morphological matrix is that it is systematic: it forces the designer to think of more than one solution for every subfunction and puts all the combinations in view, so good concepts are not easily missed. Its limitations are that the number of combinations grows very fast and that subfunctions are often linked (the compatibility of solutions), so engineering judgment is needed to narrow the field.

---

**Engineer's notes**

- Write functions as "verb + noun", not as structures: "absorb the position error", not "fit a V-groove". Writing a structure amounts to choosing the solution in advance.
- Think of at least three solutions for each subfunction, preferably one of them based on an entirely different principle (mechanical, electromagnetic, pneumatic, software).
- Write down the reasons why combinations are incompatible; in reviews they are often the focus of debate.
