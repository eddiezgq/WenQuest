### 2.3.1 The problem

Where do the solutions in the cells of the morphological matrix come from? If designers can think only of the few they have already seen, then however large the matrix, it merely rearranges old ideas. Is there a way to come up with new solutions systematically?

### 2.3.2 Brainstorming

**Brainstorming** is a group method for generating ideas that the advertising executive A. F. Osborn used in his company from 1939 onward and popularized in 1953 in his book *Applied Imagination*. The rules are simple:

1. aim for quantity, not quality;
2. no criticism: write every idea down first;
3. welcome wild ideas;
4. encourage improving on and combining other people's ideas.

A session lasts 20–30 minutes with 5–8 people, a facilitator and a recorder. The key to brainstorming is to separate "thinking up" from "judging": evaluation is left to Section 2.4. Research has found that thinking alone first and then pooling the ideas ("brainwriting") often produces more ideas than everyone talking together from the start, because it keeps a few people from dominating the discussion.

### 2.3.3 Analogy

Many good solutions come from other fields.

- **Existing products.** The wireless charging pad for a mobile phone, the charging base of a robot vacuum cleaner (two spring contacts plus an infrared beacon), and the inductive charging base of an electric toothbrush are all ready-made solutions for charging docks.
- **Nature (biomimetics).** Cats retract their claws into their paws to protect the tips, which suggests contacts that are "retracted normally and extended for docking"; the fine hairs on a gecko's feet inspired dry-adhesive grippers (Chapter 48).
- **Other fields of engineering.** A railway coupler, with just a knuckle and a set of guide surfaces, couples automatically when two cars are pushed together; the docking mechanisms of spacecraft use "guide petals + latches" to dock from large initial misalignments.

The trick of analogy is first to abstract the function ("align automatically and lock from a large initial misalignment") and then to ask "who else is solving this problem?".

### 2.3.4 An introduction to TRIZ

The **theory of inventive problem solving** (TRIZ) was distilled by the Soviet engineer G. Altshuller from the analysis of a great many patents, beginning in 1946. He found that most inventions resolve a "contradiction": improving one parameter makes another worse. For example, for the docking mechanism to "tolerate a large position error" (good), the guide surfaces must be made large, and then the dock becomes bigger and heavier (bad). TRIZ reduces engineering parameters to 39, arranges the common contradictions of "improving one parameter while worsening another" into a contradiction matrix, and gives 40 inventive principles, for example:

- "Segmentation" (No. 1): divide an object into several independent parts. Split one large contact into several spring-loaded pins, each pressed on separately, and contact becomes more reliable.
- "Dynamics" (No. 15): make something fixed movable or adjustable. A floating contact plate (concept B) lets the contacts on the dock follow the robot's error.
- "Spheroidality, curvature" (No. 14): replace flat surfaces and straight lines with curved ones. A conical guide sleeve (concept D) turns the robot's position error into sliding along the cone.
- "Replacement of mechanical system" (No. 28): replace mechanical action with electric, magnetic, optical or other fields. Inductive charging (concept C) does away with mechanical contact altogether.

TRIZ cannot give the answer directly, but it can point the direction in which to think. Its fuller methods (substance-field analysis, the ideal final result, the laws of evolution of technical systems) are covered in the references for this chapter.

**Engineer's notes**

- The greatest enemy in generating concepts is "the first idea": it makes people stop thinking. Make it a rule to think of at least three concepts based on different principles before you start evaluating.
- Patent databases are the largest library of analogies. Run a patent search before you start designing: you will find ideas and also avoid infringement.
- The inventive principles of TRIZ work well as a checklist: going through all 40 for each subfunction often yields one or two more solutions.

**Exercises**

2.3.1 Hold a 15-minute brainstorming session on "making a robot align itself automatically with its charging dock from a large misalignment", record at least 20 ideas, and then sort them by principle.

2.3.2 Find two everyday devices that "align automatically from a large misalignment" (hint: the USB-C connector, magnetic charging cables, the charging port of an electric car), and explain how they absorb the error.

2.3.3 For the contradiction between "tolerating a large position error" and "a small, light dock" in the Go2 docking mechanism, use at least two TRIZ inventive principles to think of one solution each.
