### 2.4.1 The problem

Section 2.2 picked out four docking concepts for the Go2 charging dock. Which one should be chosen? Everyone has a preference: the electrical engineer likes inductive charging, the mechanical engineer likes the taper pin and plug, finance likes whichever is cheapest. **Concept evaluation** puts these preferences on the table and measures them with the same yardstick.

### 2.4.2 Evaluation criteria

First set the evaluation criteria, and set them before looking at the concepts; otherwise it is easy to pick criteria that let one's favorite concept win. The criteria come from the design specification (Section 2.1); this example takes {{ex2_4.n_crit}}: docking success rate (how large a position error can be tolerated), charging efficiency and heating, cost, reliability and life (contact wear, fouling), safety (whether there are exposed live parts), and the extent of changes to the robot.

### 2.4.3 The Pugh matrix

The **Pugh matrix** was proposed by S. Pugh in the 1980s. One concept is chosen as the datum (A in this example), and each of the others is compared with it criterion by criterion: + if better, − if worse, 0 if about the same (Pugh himself wrote S, for "same"); then the numbers of + and − are counted. A second round with a different concept as the datum often reveals problems the first round missed.

| Criterion | A (datum) | B floating plate, blades | C inductive | D taper pin and plug |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Docking success rate | 0 | + | + | − |
| Charging efficiency and heating | 0 | 0 | − | + |
| Cost | 0 | 0 | − | − |
| Reliability and life | 0 | 0 | + | − |
| Safety | 0 | 0 | + | 0 |
| Changes to the robot | 0 | − | − | − |
| Number of + / − | — | {{ex2_4.p_B}} / {{ex2_4.m_B}} | {{ex2_4.p_C}} / {{ex2_4.m_C}} | {{ex2_4.p_D}} / {{ex2_4.m_D}} |

The Pugh matrix uses no weights and does not aim at precision; its purpose is discussion: every + and − must be justified. In this example D has the most −, so it can be dropped first; B, C and the datum each have strengths and weaknesses and are hard to separate. Pugh's advice at this point is to combine the strengths of the concepts into a new one (for example "the lie-down posture of A + the inductive charging of C") and to run another round of comparison.

### 2.4.4 Weighted scoring

The **weighted scoring method** gives each criterion a weight $w_k$ (summing to 1) and each concept a score $s_{jk}$ from 1 to 5 on each criterion; the total score is

$$
U_j = \sum_k w_k s_{jk} . \tag{2.4.1}
$$

**Example 2.4.1** The review team's weights and scores (1–5) are:

| Criterion | Weight | A | B | C | D |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Docking success rate | 0.25 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 2 |
| Charging efficiency and heating | 0.15 | 4 | 4 | 2 | 5 |
| Cost | 0.20 | 5 | 4 | 2 | 3 |
| Reliability and life | 0.20 | 3 | 3 | 5 | 2 |
| Safety | 0.10 | 3 | 3 | 5 | 3 |
| Changes to the robot | 0.10 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 2 |

By Eq. (2.4.1), the total scores of the four concepts are: A {{ex2_4.t_A:.2f}}, B {{ex2_4.t_B:.2f}}, C {{ex2_4.t_C:.2f}}, D {{ex2_4.t_D:.2f}}. A and C have the same score and B is only slightly behind: weighted scoring has not given a clear answer.

### 2.4.5 Is the conclusion robust?

The weights were agreed by the review team and are themselves imprecise. **Example 2.4.2** Let the weights vary at random around their nominal values 20 000 times (each time the weights still sum to 1; they are sampled from a Dirichlet distribution whose mean equals the nominal weights, the standard deviation of each weight being about a quarter of its value, for example 0.25 ± 0.06), and count how often each concept ranks first: A {{ex2_4.win_A:.0f}}%, B {{ex2_4.win_B:.0f}}%, C {{ex2_4.win_C:.0f}}%, D {{ex2_4.win_D:.0f}}% (Figure 2.4.1, right). A and C each take about half, which shows that "A or C" depends on fine differences in the weights. Among the 20 000 sets of weights near the nominal ones, D never ranks first and can safely be dropped (D would win only if the weight of "charging efficiency" were made exceptionally large).

Now consider one definite change: if what users care about most is "docking successfully on the first attempt", raising the weight of docking success rate from 0.25 to 0.40 changes the totals to A {{ex2_4.t2_A:.2f}}, B {{ex2_4.t2_B:.2f}}, C {{ex2_4.t2_C:.2f}}, with C clearly ahead.

So evaluation methods do not make the decision for the engineer; they lay out the disagreement clearly. The difference between A and C lies in whether "cost and charging efficiency" or "docking success rate, reliability and safety" matter more. That question must be taken back to the customer (Section 2.1), not argued over on the scoring sheet. In practice, prototypes of both A and C are often built, and test data replace the scores.

**Engineer's notes**

- Set the criteria and weights first, then look at the concepts; the criteria must be independent of one another, or the same advantage will be counted twice.
- When scoring, it is best for each person to score independently first and then discuss the cells with the largest differences; the differences are information in themselves.
- When two concepts score close together, do not choose the higher one just because it is 0.05 ahead; do a sensitivity analysis, or build prototypes.
