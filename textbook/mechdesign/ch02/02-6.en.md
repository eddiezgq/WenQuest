### 2.6.1 The problem

The orders a reducer manufacturer receives vary enormously: one wants an output of 250 r/min, another 100 r/min; one has a 1 kW motor, another 7 kW. If every order were designed from scratch, the design, molds, fixtures, material procurement and inspection would all have to be done anew, and the cost and lead time would be unacceptable; if only one model were made, it would not satisfy most customers. How can as many needs as possible be covered with as few kinds of parts as possible?

### 2.6.2 Standardization and preferred numbers

**Standardization** (Section 1.5) lets the dimensions and parameters of parts take only a limited number of values. Which values? At the end of the 19th century the French engineer C. Renard proposed a way of grading the sizes of balloon mooring ropes: take values in geometric progression, so that the ratio of any two neighboring values is the same. This is today's **series of preferred numbers** (GB/T 321, ISO 3): the R5, R10, R20 and R40 series have ratios of $\sqrt[5]{10}$, $\sqrt[10]{10}$, $\sqrt[20]{10}$ and $\sqrt[40]{10}$, that is, about 1.6, 1.25, 1.12 and 1.06. The R10 series, for example, is 1, 1.25, 1.6, 2, 2.5, 3.15, 4, 5, 6.3, 8, 10.

The advantages of geometric grading are that every step covers the same relative range, so small and large sizes are graded equally "finely"; and products, quotients and powers of preferred numbers are still (approximately) preferred numbers, so when one parameter is a preferred number, the parameters proportional to it naturally fall on preferred numbers too. Most dimensions, speeds, powers and reduction ratios in machinery, and the sizes of bearings and motors, are graded by preferred numbers; the standard series of shaft diameters in Section 1.4 is derived from them.

### 2.6.3 Serialization and modularization

**Serialization** divides a product into a number of sizes by one principal parameter (rated torque, for example), the sizes being similar in structure and scaled up in dimensions by preferred numbers. Reducer, motor, coupling and bearing catalogs all present series: once one size has been designed, the others can be derived from it by similarity.

**Modular design** divides a product into modules that are functionally independent and have standard interfaces, and combines different modules into different products. The input shaft assembly, intermediate shaft assembly, output shaft assembly and housing of the WQR-105 are four modules; the joint module of a collaborative robot (Chapter 45) is a still more typical case: the same joint module is used in different joints of a robot arm and in different models.

### 2.6.4 Parametric product families

A **product family** is a group of products that share a platform (common parts, structure and processes) and vary in a number of parameters. The WQR-105 is a parametric product family: the modules of the two stages ({{ex2_6.m1:.0f}} and {{ex2_6.m2:.0f}} mm) and the center distances ({{ex2_6.a1:.0f}} and {{ex2_6.a2:.0f}} mm) stay the same, the total number of teeth is kept at {{ex2_6.sum1}} in the first stage and {{ex2_6.sum2}} in the second, and changing only the tooth-number combinations gives {{ex2_6.n_ratio}} ratios ({{ex2_6.i_min:.2f}}–{{ex2_6.i_max:.2f}}). The housing, bearings, shafts, keys and end caps are all common, and only the four gears have to be made separately for each ratio. These ratios cover the usual range of reduction ratios, but they are not graded by preferred numbers: they deviate from the R20 series (neighboring steps about 12% apart) by up to {{ex2_6.dev_max:.1f}}%, some are crowded together ({{ex2_6.crowd}}), and in places there are larger gaps. This is the limitation that comes from "changing only the tooth numbers with the total number of teeth fixed" (Exercise 2.6.2).

**Example 2.6.1** The digital factory's configurator (Chapter 1) is the selection tool for this product family. Using the same function as the configurator, for an input of 1450 r/min, application factor 1.25 and bearing life 10 000 h, we compute the maximum allowable input power for each ratio (Figure 2.6.1). The larger the ratio, the lower the output speed, and the smaller the power corresponding to the same rated torque of {{ex2_6.T_rated:.0f}} N·m: from {{ex2_6.p_lo:.2f}} kW at i = {{ex2_6.i_min:.2f}} down to {{ex2_6.p_hi:.2f}} kW at i = {{ex2_6.i_max:.2f}}; the standard model, i = {{ex2_6.i_std:.1f}}, allows at most {{ex2_6.p_std:.2f}} kW. For all {{ex2_6.n_ratio}} ratios the rated torque is the limit that is reached first. The rated torque itself was set by the parallel key of the output shaft: at {{ex2_6.T_rated:.0f}} N·m the bearing stress of the key is already {{ex2_6.key_util:.0f}}% of the allowable value, while the bearing life has a large margin. This shows that the "platform" of this product family (output shaft, key) is designed for the maximum torque: the power of the high-ratio models is limited by this platform, while the low-ratio models can transmit much more power; but the configurator checks only the torque, key and bearings on the output side, and whether the input shaft, intermediate shaft and first-stage gears are adequate at high power must be checked separately (Chapters 29 and 33).

The difficulty in designing a product family lies in choosing the "platform": make the platform too strong and the small sizes carry superfluous weight and cost; make it too weak and the performance of the large sizes is limited. The usual practice is to have two or three platforms (for example WQR-105, WQR-125, ...), each covering a range, with the ranges of neighboring platforms joined by preferred numbers.

**Engineer's notes**

- Before designing a new part, look in the parts library and through the company's existing parts: if an existing one will do, do not design a new one. Every additional kind of part means another set of drawings, tooling, stock and inspection.
- When designing a new product family, grade the parameters by preferred numbers, not by "round numbers": steps of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 are too coarse at the small end and too fine at the large end.
- The configurator is the "manual" of the product family: it writes the selection rules as a program, so that sales staff and customers can select models themselves and the engineer need not calculate every order.
