### 2.5.1 The problem

The WQR-105 is made of {{ex1_1.n_make}} kinds of in-house parts and {{ex1_1.n_buy}} kinds of purchased parts. If the designer of each part focused only on that part, every part might "pass" and yet the assembled reducer might fail to meet the customer's requirements: the gears strong enough but the housing unable to shed the heat, the bearing life sufficient but the noise over the limit. How can we make sure that the work done at the part level ends up meeting the requirements for the whole machine?

### 2.5.2 Systems engineering and the V-model

**Systems engineering** is an approach that grew out of the aerospace and defense industries in the 1950s and 1960s (see the NASA and INCOSE handbooks). Its central idea is: first define the requirements for the whole, then decompose them level by level down to subsystems and parts; once the work is done, verify it level by level back up again. Drawn out, the process looks like the letter V and is called the **V-model** (Figure 2.5.1). The V-model was put forward in software engineering in the mid-1980s and brought into systems engineering in the early 1990s (the 1991 paper by Forsberg and Mooz; the V-Modell of the German federal government, 1992):

::: Figure 2.5.1
src: fig2_5_1
caption: The V-model (with the WQR-105 as the example): the left side decomposes requirements level by level down to parts, the right side verifies level by level from parts up to the whole machine; the dashed lines show that verification at each level is against the requirements of the same level
:::

- **Left side (decomposition).** Customer needs (torque, life, price) → machine specifications (ratio 10.5, rated 350 N·m, overall dimensions, efficiency) → assemblies (load and life of the output shaft assembly, stiffness and heat dissipation of the housing) → parts (diameters, fillets and material of SH-301). Every requirement at each level must be traceable to the level above.
- **Bottom.** Manufacture.
- **Right side (verification).** Part verification (finite elements, fatigue calculation, CMM inspection) → assembly verification (assembly check, no-load run-in) → machine test (duty simulation on the run-in test bench, final inspection) → user acceptance (operation at the customer's site).

The most important thing in the V-model is the correspondence between the two sides (the dashed lines in the figure): verification at each level is against the requirements of the same level. Part inspection can only prove that a part conforms to its part drawing; it cannot prove that the machine meets the customer's needs. When the machine test finds a problem, one must trace back up the left side of the V to find at which level the decomposition went wrong. The design of SH-301 in Chapter 33 is an example: the customer's requirement of "frequent starts and stops, 10 000 h" is decomposed down to the shaft, where it becomes a fatigue-life requirement on Section IV, and is finally verified with the torque record measured on the run-in test bench.

The international standard ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288 specifies the processes of the system life cycle; VDI 2206 of the Association of German Engineers (design of mechatronic systems, first published in 2004 and revised in 2021) uses the V-model to organize the joint design of mechanics, electronics and software; VDI 2221 is the general model of the phases and activities of product development.

### 2.5.3 Stage-gates

The **stage-gate** process is a method of managing new product development summarized by R. G. Cooper around 1990: development is divided into stages, and at the end of each stage there is a "gate" at which management and representatives of each department decide, against criteria set in advance, whether to "go", "rework" or "kill". Stage-gates and the design reviews of Section 1.2 are two views of the same thing: a design review looks at the technology, while a stage-gate also looks at the market, cost and risk.

The stage-gate process requires every gate to have definite deliverables and pass criteria. For example, the "concept gate" requires a concept comparison report (Section 2.4), a preliminary cost estimate and a list of the main risks; the "release gate" requires the full set of drawings, calculation sheets and verification reports. The engineering task sheets of the digital factory (TS-1-1 in Chapter 1, TS-33-1 in Chapter 33) are designed on this principle: only when the deliverables are complete and the review comments are closed may the work "pass the gate" into manufacture.

**Engineer's notes**

- Requirements must be traceable: for every dimension of a part you should be able to answer "which higher-level requirement is it there to satisfy?"; a dimension for which there is no answer is either superfluous or a sign of a requirement left unwritten.
- Verification must be carried up to the higher levels: a good part does not mean a good assembly, and a good assembly does not mean a good machine.
- A stage-gate is not bureaucratic red tape: it gives a formal opportunity to "kill a project doomed to fail", and the earlier it is killed the more money is saved.
