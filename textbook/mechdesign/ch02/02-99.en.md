The requirements and concept stages account for little of the spending but determine the overall shape of the product. Requirements analysis turns what the customer says into a design specification with values and test methods; the house of quality multiplies the weights of the needs by the need-characteristic relationships and sums them to find the engineering characteristics that matter most. In the WQR-105 example the importance of tooth-flank hardness is second only to the rated torque, the result of scattered influences adding up.

Concepts start from functions: decompose the overall function into subfunctions, list several solutions based on different principles for each subfunction, arrange them in a morphological matrix, rule out incompatible combinations, and then pick out representative concepts. Solutions can come from brainstorming, from analogies with existing products and with nature, and from the inventive principles of TRIZ. To evaluate concepts, first set the criteria and weights, then discuss with the Pugh matrix and compare with weighted scoring. The weights themselves are imprecise, so one must check whether the conclusion is robust to them: in the Go2 charging-dock example, concepts A and C each have about an even chance of ranking first when the weights are perturbed, and the decision has to be taken back to the customer.

Systems engineering uses the V-model to decompose requirements level by level down to parts and then verify level by level back up to the whole machine, verification at each level being against the requirements of the same level; a stage-gate decides at the end of each stage whether to go, rework or kill. Standardization and preferred numbers let parameters take a limited set of values, and serialization, modularization and parametric product families cover as many needs as possible with as few kinds of parts as possible; the WQR-105 obtains 16 ratios by changing only the tooth numbers, and the configurator is the selection tool for this product family. AI can organize, translate, check and propose candidates, but it cannot be responsible for the results, and every number it gives must be checked.

### Historical notes

"Design methodology" became a field of research only after the Second World War. The German school of engineering design (Hansen, Rodenacker, Pahl and Beitz) systematized the design process from the 1950s to the 1970s. Pahl and Beitz's *Engineering Design* (first edition 1977) set out the phases of "clarification of the task, conceptual design, embodiment design, detail design" and the method of function structures, which later became the basis of the VDI 2221 guideline of the Association of German Engineers. F. Zwicky proposed morphological analysis in the 1940s. In Japan, Yoji Akao proposed the concept of quality function deployment in 1966; in 1972 engineers at the Kobe Shipyard of Mitsubishi Heavy Industries produced the first "quality table"; in 1978 Akao and Shigeru Mizuno published a monograph; and in the 1980s the method passed by way of Toyota to the American automotive industry. S. Pugh, at the University of Strathclyde in Britain, put forward "total design" and the concept selection matrix. Altshuller studied patents in the Soviet Union and founded TRIZ. Systems engineering came from the large projects of the US Department of Defense and NASA in the 1950s and 1960s; the V-model was put forward in software engineering in the mid-1980s and brought into systems engineering in the early 1990s.
