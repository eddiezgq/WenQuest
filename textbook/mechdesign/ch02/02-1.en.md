### 2.1.1 The problem

The purchasing manager of a conveyor manufacturer calls: "We need a reducer: sturdy, durable, not too noisy, not too expensive." With only this sentence, the engineer cannot start drawing. How sturdy is sturdy? How many decibels count as noisy? Expensive compared with what? The first step of design is to turn what the customer says into checkable design specifications; the second is to find out which of these specifications matter most, and where the limited design effort should go.

### 2.1.2 From the customer's words to the design specification

**Requirements analysis** has three steps:

1. **Collect.** Interviews, questionnaires, observation on site, complaint and repair records, comparison with competing products. What customers say is often a solution ("use a bigger bearing"), and the engineer has to probe for the need behind it ("the bearings keep failing").
2. **Organize.** Write the needs as short statements that contain no solutions, group them, and remove duplicates, for example "enough torque, survives occasional overloads", "long life, little maintenance".
3. **Quantify.** Assign to each need one or more measurable engineering characteristics, give target values and test methods, and write them into the design specification. For example, "enough torque" corresponds to "rated output torque 350 N·m (application factor included), tested by duty simulation on the run-in test bench".

The design specification must also state the constraints (overall dimensions, interfaces, standards, regulations, cost ceiling) and the delivery requirements (quantity, delivery date, documents).

### 2.1.3 The house of quality

**Quality function deployment** (QFD) is a method developed in Japan in the 1960s and 1970s (Yoji Akao proposed the concept in 1966, and in 1972 the Kobe Shipyard of Mitsubishi Heavy Industries applied it to ship design). It places customer needs and engineering characteristics in one matrix (shaped like a house, and so called the house of quality) and answers the question "which engineering characteristic matters most for satisfying the customer?".

**Example 2.1.1** The house of quality of the WQR-105 (Figure 2.1.1). From customer interviews the review team distilled {{ex2_1.n_needs}} needs and gave each a weight from 1 to 5 ("enough torque" is the most important, at {{ex2_1.w_torque}}); it chose {{ex2_1.n_chars}} engineering characteristics, all with target values taken from the digital factory: rated output torque {{ex2_1.T_rated:.0f}} N·m, bearing life ≥ {{ex2_1.life}} h, ex-works price {{ex2_1.price:.0f}} USD, pinion tooth-flank hardness, center distances of the two stages {{ex2_1.a1:.0f}} / {{ex2_1.a2:.0f}} mm, and overall efficiency {{ex2_1.eta:.3f}}. Each cell of the matrix holds the strength of the relationship between a need and a characteristic: strong 9, medium 3, weak 1, none 0.

The importance of each engineering characteristic is the sum, over all needs, of the weight of the need times the strength of the relationship:

$$
I_j = \sum_i w_i r_{ij} . \tag{2.1.1}
$$

This is exactly what the house of quality is for: adding up scattered influences to find key characteristics that are easily overlooked. For the WQR-105 it means that the heat treatment and inspection of the gears (Chapter 29) deserve more design and quality effort.

A full house of quality also has a "roof" (correlations between engineering characteristics, for example that raising hardness increases cost), a "right wall" (comparison with competing products) and a "basement" (target values and technical difficulty). The numbers in a house of quality come from team scoring and are subjective; its value lies not in precise numbers but in bringing design, process planning, quality and sales around one table to explain clearly, and write down, "why the specifications were set this way".

**Engineer's notes**

- What customers say they "want" is often "how to do it". Ask "why?" three times to reach the real need.
- Every specification in the design specification needs a test method; a specification without one will cause disputes at acceptance.
- Do not take the percentages from a house of quality as precise conclusions: change one weight or one relationship strength and the ranking may change (Section 2.4 discusses the stability of rankings).
