### 3.1.1 The problem

On the roller conveyor beside a palletizing robot, each roller has a pillow block at either end (Figure 3.1.1, left). The frame designer needs to know its center height and the spacing of its anchor-bolt holes; the foundry needs to know its outer shape; the machine shop needs to know how large and how deep the bearing bore is. Is a single axonometric (pictorial) view enough? It is not. In a pictorial view circles become ellipses, lengths are foreshortened according to their direction, the depth of a hole and the position of a step cannot be seen clearly, and dimensions certainly cannot be placed on it accurately. In engineering, shape is expressed by a set of drawings made by **orthographic projection**, called **views**.

### 3.1.2 Orthographic projection and the three views

Orthographic projection projects an object onto a plane with a set of parallel projectors perpendicular to the plane of projection. Its advantage is that a line or plane parallel to the plane of projection appears in its true length and true shape, so dimensions can be measured directly. One view shows dimensions in only two directions, so several views are used together.

The most common arrangement is the **three-view drawing**: looking from the front gives the front view, looking from above gives the top view, and looking from the left gives the left view. In the arrangement of the national standard, the top view lies directly below the front view and the left view directly to the right of the front view. The three views are related as follows:

- **Length aligned**: the front and top views both show the length of the object (left to right), and corresponding points line up vertically;
- **Height level**: the front and left views both show the height of the object, and corresponding points line up horizontally;
- **Width equal**: the top and left views both show the width of the object (front to back), and corresponding dimensions are equal.

The orientation must also be remembered: in the top view and the left view, the side away from the front view is the front of the object (the side next to the front view is the back). Hidden outlines are drawn as thin dashed lines, and axes and center lines of symmetry as thin long-dashed dotted (chain) lines.

### 3.1.3 First-angle and third-angle projection

Three mutually perpendicular planes of projection divide space into eight "angles" (quadrants). When the object is placed in the first angle, between the observer and the plane of projection, the method is called first-angle projection. When it is placed in the third angle, with the plane of projection between the observer and the object, as if looking through glass, it is called third-angle projection. The views have exactly the same shapes in both; only their positions differ. In first-angle projection the top view lies below the front view and the left view to its right; in third-angle projection the top view lies above it and the right view to its right (Figure 3.1.2).

The Chinese national standard (GB/T 14692) and most European countries use first-angle projection; the United States (ASME Y14.3) and Japan use third-angle projection; the international standard ISO 5456-2 provides for both. When reading a foreign drawing, first look for the projection symbol near the title block. It shows two views of a truncated cone: if the circles are on the right, it is first-angle; if they are on the left, it is third-angle. Misreading the method reverses left and right, top and bottom. This is one of the most common mistakes made in reading drawings of imported equipment.

### 3.1.4 Generating views from the model

Most engineering drawings today are not drawn stroke by stroke but generated by projecting a three-dimensional model. For every edge of the model, the CAD software works out whether it is hidden by other faces in the direction of projection; hidden parts are drawn dashed and visible parts continuous. Program 3.1.1 uses the same algorithm (`project_to_viewport` in build123d, which rests on the hidden-line removal of OpenCascade). The program also removes the "tangent edges", where fillets and arcs meet planes tangentially, since the national standard says no line is drawn there. The advantage is that the drawing always agrees with the model. There is also a limitation: the program does not know which dashed lines are "necessary". The national standard allows dashed lines to be omitted for hidden features already made clear in other views, and it still takes an engineer to decide which lines to keep.

---

**Engineer's notes**

- To read a drawing, find the front view first, then use "length aligned, height level, width equal" to find the corresponding lines in the other views, and work out the shape one feature at a time.
- When you receive a foreign drawing, look at the projection symbol first; if there is none, check whether the arrangement of the views and the dimensions agree.
- Choose as the front view the direction that best shows the characteristic shape, and as far as possible make it agree with the machining position or working position of the part.
