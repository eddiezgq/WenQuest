Engineering drawings and 3D models are the language engineers use with one another. Orthographic views show true shapes and sizes, and the three views are related by "length aligned, height level, width equal". China uses first-angle projection and the United States third-angle projection; when reading a drawing, look at the projection symbol first. Internal structure is shown by sectional views (full, half and local sections), and keyways and holes in shafts by cross-sections. Dimensions are given by function: dimensions that matter are given directly, and dimension chains are never closed. In the SH-301 example, chain dimensioning lets the error of the overall length accumulate to ±{{ex3_3.tot_chain:.1f}} mm, whereas dimensioning by function gives only ±{{ex3_3.tot_func:.1f}} mm.

A part drawing must have views, all dimensions, technical requirements and a title block. An assembly drawing must have views, five kinds of dimension, technical requirements, item numbers and a parts list, and the parts list must agree with the BOM. Drawing an assembly is a check of the assembly: that is how the original structure of RJ-201, which could not be assembled, was discovered and corrected. Threads, gears, rolling bearings and springs are drawn in conventional representation, and standard parts have no part drawings. A parametric model consists of sketches, constraints and features, and a fully constrained sketch has zero degrees of freedom. Design intent decides whether a model remains sound after its parameters are changed: with the arc radius of the pillow block hard-coded, changing to a larger bearing leaves a wall only {{ex3_7.wall_bad:.1f}} mm thick, without any error message. Modeling in code writes the design rules as a program, and one program generates a whole family. Model-based definition places tolerances and requirements directly on the 3D model, while 2D drawings remain useful in the supply chain and on the shop floor. Item codes follow "one code per item": a part that remains interchangeable goes up a revision, one that does not gets a new code. Once a drawing has been released, changes go through the engineering change process and are followed through from the PLM to the ERP, MES and QMS.

All the figures in this chapter are generated by projecting and sectioning 3D models: the model is the source, and the drawing is a view of it.

### Historical notes

China has a long tradition of expressing machines in drawings. Su Song of the Northern Song dynasty directed the building of the water-powered astronomical clock tower, and his *Xin Yixiang Fayao* (New Design for an Armillary Clock, late 11th century) includes drawings of the whole machine and of its parts. Li Jie's *Yingzao Fashi* (Treatise on Architectural Methods, printed 1103) used a large number of drawings to set the form and construction of building members. The theoretical foundation of the modern engineering drawing is the descriptive geometry of the French mathematician Gaspard Monge: at the end of the 18th century he systematized the representation of solids in space by two mutually perpendicular planes of projection, taught it in Paris in 1795, and his lectures were published in 1799 as *Géométrie descriptive*. With industrialization in the 19th century, each country gradually developed its own drafting practice: Europe kept to first-angle projection, while the United States turned to third-angle projection in the late 19th century. China's first national standards for mechanical drawing were issued in 1959, and they have since been revised many times and gradually harmonized with international standards such as ISO 128.

Computers entered design in the 1960s. In 1963 Ivan Sutherland built the interactive drawing system Sketchpad at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, which already had the idea of constraints. Solid modeling (boundary representation, B-rep, and constructive solid geometry, CSG) appeared in the 1970s; parametric, feature-based 3D CAD became commercial in the late 1980s and spread to personal computers in the 1990s. The first edition of STEP (ISO 10303), the international standard for exchanging 3D models, was published in 1994. At the beginning of the 21st century the American Society of Mechanical Engineers issued ASME Y14.41 (2003), and 3D annotation and model-based definition began to enter industries such as aerospace and automotive. Today open-source tools (FreeCAD, and the OpenCascade geometry kernel used by build123d) can carry out the whole process from modeling to drawing.
