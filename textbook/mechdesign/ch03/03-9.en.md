### 3.9.1 The problem

The part drawing of the pillow block (Figure 3.4.1) is generated from the 3D model. NC programming in the workshop uses the 3D model, and so does the inspection program of the coordinate measuring machine, yet requirements such as tolerances and roughness are written on the 2D drawing. The NC programmer and the inspector have to look at the model and leaf through the drawing at the same time, working out which dimension belongs to which face. Could these requirements be placed directly on the model?

### 3.9.2 3D annotation and MBD

**Model-based definition** (MBD) places dimensions, tolerances, datums, surface roughness, material and technical requirements, collectively called product and manufacturing information (PMI), directly on the 3D model and associates them with the geometric faces of the model, so that the 3D model becomes the single, complete definition of the product (Figure 3.9.1). The relevant standards are ASME Y14.41 (Digital Product Definition Data Practices, first edition 2003), ISO 16792 (first edition 2006) and the Chinese GB/T 24734 (Technical product documentation: digital product definition data practices, 2009).

::: Figure 3.9.1
src: fig3_9_1
caption: 3D annotation of the pillow block: tolerance class, center height, datum and parallelism, surface roughness and hole sizes are all attached to the corresponding geometric faces (same content as the part drawing of Figure 3.4.1)
:::

Compared with 2D drawings, MBD has these advantages:

- **A single source**: the model is the definition, so the drawing and the model can never disagree;
- **Machine-readable**: PMI is associated with geometric faces, so NC programming software can read directly which faces need finishing and what their tolerances are, and inspection software can generate measuring programs automatically;
- **Fewer drawings**: parts with complex curved surfaces (such as robot covers or blades) are hard to express in 2D drawings, and MBD saves a great deal of drafting work.

For PMI to pass between different software packages, an exchange format that supports it is needed. STEP AP242 (ISO 10303-242) can carry semantic PMI, that is, "this frame is a circular run-out of 0.01 on this face, with datums on those two faces", not merely a picture of text stuck onto the model.

### 3.9.3 When 2D drawings are still needed

MBD has not completely replaced 2D drawings (Quintana et al., 2010). There are several reasons. Small companies in the supply chain do not necessarily have software that can read PMI; paper drawings are the most convenient on the shop floor; contracts, regulations and archives still require documents that can be signed and kept for a long time; and many engineers read a 2D drawing faster than 3D annotation. A common transitional practice is "model first, drawing second": the 3D model and PMI are the official definition, and the 2D drawing shows only what is awkward to show in 3D, or is generated automatically from the model for reference only.

Design release in the digital factory currently consists of "STEP model + 2D drawing (SVG or PDF)": the model is for "Simulation & Analysis" and NC programming, the drawing for the workshop and inspection. 3D annotation is not yet supported (the STEP file contains geometry only). This is also where MBD stands in many factories.

**Engineer's notes**

- Whether 2D drawings or MBD are used, a product definition can have only one "master". When both exist, state which one governs.
- 3D annotation follows the same rules as 2D annotation: datums, tolerance frames and roughness mean the same as on a 2D drawing; they are simply attached to 3D geometry.
- Before sending out a STEP file, confirm whether the recipient needs AP214 with geometry only or AP242 with PMI.
