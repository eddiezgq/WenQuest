### 3.10.1 The problem

Chapter 33 revised SH-301 from version A to version B. The workshop still has a batch of shafts made to version A, the warehouse still holds 8 × 7 × 36 keys, and next week's work orders have already been scheduled. After the revision, which version of the drawing does the workshop receive? Can the old parts still be used? Should purchasing go on buying the old keys? However well a drawing is made, poor management of it leads to trouble: machining to a voided drawing is one of the most common and costliest mistakes in a factory.

### 3.10.2 Item codes, drawing numbers and revisions

Every part, assembly and raw material has a unique **item code**. It is the drawing number of the part drawing, and it is also the part's "name" in the BOM, on purchase orders, in the warehouse and on work orders. The digital factory codes items with a prefix for each category: raw materials `RM-` (such as RM-45-D50, Ø50 bar of 45 steel); purchased standard parts by kind (BRG-6207, KEY-12x8x45, BOLT-M12x110); parts made in-house by the assembly they belong to (SH-301 is the shaft of assembly 3, GR-302 the gear of assembly 3); assemblies `SA-`; and the finished product `WQR-105`. The coding rules need not be complicated. What matters is one code per item and one item per code: the same part must not have two codes, and different parts must not share a code.

A **revision** records how many times the design of a part has been changed. If a changed part is still interchangeable with the original (mounting dimensions, fits and function unchanged), only the revision is raised and the code stays the same; if it is no longer interchangeable, it must get a new code. This book writes version A, version B; the PLM of the digital factory numbers revisions (revision 1, revision 2, ...), adding 1 each time a design release is approved.

By this rule, the change of SH-301 from version A to version B altered its diameters, lengths, keyways and material, and the oil seal, end cover and gear that go with it changed too. It is no longer interchangeable with version A and, strictly speaking, should be given a new code (SH-311, say), with the old code voided. For teaching purposes the digital factory simplifies this to "same code, higher revision", so that the revision history of one shaft stays under one number; in a real factory, a case like this calls for a new code.

(where-used) gives {{ex3_10.wu_brg}}. Only by a where-used search before changing a part can one know which products the change will affect.

The design department's BOM (the engineering BOM) is organized by product structure. The manufacturing department's BOM (the manufacturing BOM) adds what the process needs, such as blanks, consumables and packaging, and is organized in the order of assembly. In ERPNext of the digital factory, the BOM of SH-301 (the current version A) is "2.8 kg of RM-45-D50 bar", a line that does not exist in the engineering BOM; version B changes to 40Cr bar, and this line must change as well.

### 3.10.4 Engineering changes

Once a drawing has been released it cannot be changed at will; a change must go through the **engineering change** process (Figure 3.10.1a):

1. **Engineering change request** (ECR): who found what problem, what change is proposed, which items and documents are affected;
2. **Review**: design, process, quality and purchasing look at it together: is it technically right, how are work in progress and stock to be dealt with, and what are the effects on cost and delivery;
3. **Approval**: an engineering change order (ECO) is issued, the new revision takes effect in the PLM, and the old revision is voided but its record kept; the document notifying all departments concerned is called the engineering change notice (ECN);
4. **Implementation**: a new BOM version is created in the ERP, stating the effective date and the disposition of old stock; work orders in the MES switch to the new revision of the drawings and NC programs; the inspection templates in the QMS are changed accordingly.

From version A to version B of SA-300, the BOM has {{ex3_10.n_diff}} changes (Figure 3.10.1b): SH-301 and GR-302 go up a revision (the simplified practice of the digital factory, see Section 3.10.2); the coupling key gets a new item code (its length has changed and it is not interchangeable with the old key); a sleeve is added; and the other {{ex3_10.n_same}} items are unchanged. The complete change request also includes changes outside SA-300: the material of SH-301 changed to 40Cr, the oil seal and end cover brought by the change of the seal seat to Ø35, the shaft-end retaining plate, and the inspection template. The digital factory closes this chain into a loop. A new revision (STEP + drawings) is submitted in "Design Release & Approval"; when the reviewer approves it, the PLM sends a "design release" message, ERPNext automatically raises the revision of the item, creates a new BOM version if needed with the drawings attached, and the next work order uses the new revision. This is the path followed by task sheet TS-1-1 of Chapter 1.

**Engineer's notes**

- Drawings that have been issued must be recallable: the workshop keeps only the current revision, and old revisions are stamped "VOID" or withdrawn from the system.
- Before changing a part, find where it is used; a change in the size of one standard part may affect dozens of products.
- A change request must state "why", not only "what": three years later, the change request is the only place where anyone can learn why the change was made.
