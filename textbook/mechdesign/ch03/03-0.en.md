title: "Chapter Overview"
---

The result of a design is handed to other people to make: the shop floor machines the part to the drawing, inspection checks it against the drawing, purchasing buys parts from the parts list, and the next engineer who takes over the work learns the designer's intent from the drawing. Engineering drawings and three-dimensional models are the "language" that engineers use with one another. This language has a strict grammar. National standards prescribe the projection method, the line types, how dimensions are placed, how tolerances and roughness are written and what goes into the title block. If the grammar is wrong, others will misread the drawing and the part will be made wrong.

The chapter proceeds in the order "reading drawings, making drawings, modeling, managing". Sections 3.1 and 3.2 show how views, sectional views and cross-sections express a three-dimensional shape clearly. Section 3.3 explains the reasoning behind dimensioning: dimension by function, and never close a dimension chain. Sections 3.4 and 3.5 cover the content and rules of part drawings and assembly drawings. Section 3.6 gives the conventional representation of standard and common parts such as threads, gears, bearings and springs. Sections 3.7 and 3.8 move from drawings to three-dimensional models: sketches, constraints, features and design intent, and modeling in code (build123d). Section 3.9 covers model-based definition, in which dimensions and tolerances are placed directly on the three-dimensional model. Section 3.10 deals with item codes, revisions, bills of materials and engineering changes, that is, with how drawings are managed.

All the figures in this chapter are generated by projecting and sectioning three-dimensional models; none is drawn by hand. A program first builds the solid of a part with build123d, then computes the visible and hidden lines of each view and the regions cut by a section, and finally draws them with the line types of the national standard. This is the same principle by which modern CAD produces drawings, and it makes one point clear: the model is the source, the drawing is a "view" generated from the model, and the two must agree.

The examples are mostly from robotics. The pillow block of a roller on the conveyor line of a palletizing station runs through the three views, sections, part drawing, parametric modeling and 3D annotation. The hollow shaft RJ-201 of a collaborative-robot shoulder joint (Section 33.10) serves as the example for sections, and also as the example of "drawing an assembly is a check". The output shaft SH-301 of WQR-105, the output shaft assembly SA-300 and the bill of materials of the whole machine run through dimensioning, assembly drawings and drawing management. The task sheet of this chapter, TS-3-1, asks the reader to draw the part drawing of SH-301 version B in FreeCAD and submit it to "Design Release & Approval" in the digital factory, where the AI design reviewer picks out errors on the drawing against the national standards.

After studying this chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. read and draw the three views of a simple part, and state the difference between first-angle and third-angle projection;
2. choose views, sectional views and cross-sections according to the structure of a part;
3. choose dimension datums and dimensioning methods according to the needs of function, machining and inspection, and avoid closed dimension chains;
4. state the content of part drawings and assembly drawings, and write dimensions, tolerance classes and fit designations, roughness, technical requirements and the title block according to the national standards;
5. draw threads, gears, rolling bearings and springs in conventional representation;
6. build a parametric model with sketches, constraints and features, and check its degrees of freedom and design intent;
7. write a build123d program for a parametric part;
8. state what model-based definition and two-dimensional drawings are each good for, and how item codes, revisions, BOMs and engineering changes are used to manage drawings.
