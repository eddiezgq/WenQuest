### 3.6.1 The problem

The thread of an M12 bolt has dozens of turns of helix, a gear has dozens of involute teeth, and a bearing contains a dozen or more balls. Projecting all of them in their true shape would make drawing slow and leave the drawing too cluttered to read. The shapes of these parts are fixed by standards; what the drawing has to convey is only "there is a thread, gear or bearing of such-and-such a size here". The national standards prescribe simplified ways of drawing them, called **conventional representation** (Figure 3.6.1).

### 3.6.2 Threads (GB/T 4459.1)

For an external thread, the major diameter (crest) is drawn as a thick continuous line, the minor diameter (root) as a thin continuous line, and the thread end line as a thick continuous line; in the end view the minor diameter is drawn as a thin continuous circle of about 3/4 of a turn. Take [[part:A-BLT-HEX/M12x50]] as an example: major diameter {{fig3_6.d:.0f}} mm, pitch {{fig3_6.P:.2f}} mm, minor diameter d₁ = d − 1.0825P = {{fig3_6.d1:.2f}} mm (GB/T 196). For an internal thread in section the lines are reversed: the minor diameter (crest) is a thick continuous line and the major diameter a thin continuous line, and the hatching extends up to the thick line (the minor diameter). The cone at the bottom of a drilled hole is drawn at 120°.

The size of a thread is indicated by a designation placed on the major diameter: a coarse metric thread is written "M12", a fine-pitch thread "M12 × 1.25", and a tolerance class may follow (such as M12-6g). Where an internal and an external thread are engaged, the engaged length is drawn as the external thread.

### 3.6.3 Gears (GB/T 4459.2)

The tip circle and tip line are drawn as thick continuous lines, the reference circle and reference line as thin chain lines, and the root circle and root line as thin continuous lines (or omitted). In a section through the axis, the teeth are always drawn unsectioned and the root line is drawn as a thick continuous line. Figure 3.6.1b shows the second-stage pinion GR-203 of WQR-105: m = {{fig3_6.m}}, z = {{fig3_6.z}}, reference diameter Ø{{fig3_6.dp}}, tip diameter Ø{{fig3_6.da}}, root diameter Ø{{fig3_6.df:g}}. For a pair of gears in mesh, the two reference circles are tangent in the meshing zone; in section the teeth of one gear are drawn with thick continuous lines and the hidden part of the other gear is drawn dashed or omitted. The module, number of teeth, pressure angle, accuracy grade and other parameters of a gear are given in a parameter table in the upper right corner of the part drawing.

### 3.6.4 Rolling bearings (GB/T 4459.7)

A rolling bearing can be drawn in three ways: **general representation**, a rectangle with an upright cross, which says only "there is a bearing here"; **characteristic representation**, in which line segments inside the rectangle show the type and arrangement of the rolling elements; and **conventional representation**, in which the inner and outer rings are hatched (in the same direction) and the rolling elements are drawn as circles without hatching. On an assembly drawing, the conventional representation is usually drawn on one side of the axis only, and the general representation on the other (Figures 3.5.1 and 3.6.1c). The boundary dimensions of a bearing (bore d, outside diameter D, width B) are taken from the standard according to the designation; for example, [[part:A-BRG-DG/6207]] is 35 × 72 × 17 mm.

### 3.6.5 Springs (GB/T 4459.4)

In a view of a cylindrical helical compression spring parallel to its axis, the outlines of the coils are drawn as straight lines. When there are more than four active coils, the coils in the middle may be omitted, drawing only one or two coils at each end and joining them by thin chain lines through the centers of the wire. The end coils are drawn in the prescribed way whatever their actual number. Figure 3.6.1d shows [[part:A-SPR-CMP/1.6x14x40]]: wire diameter {{fig3_6.spr_d:g}} mm, mean diameter {{fig3_6.spr_D:.0f}} mm, free length {{fig3_6.spr_H0:.0f}} mm, {{fig3_6.spr_n:g}} active coils. The parameters of the spring (rate, length under working load and so on) are also given in a parameter table on the part drawing; Chapter 27 shows how to calculate them.

### 3.6.6 No part drawings for standard parts

Standard parts such as bolts, nuts, washers, keys, pins and rolling bearings have no part drawings. Only their size and standard number are written in the parts list of the assembly drawing (for example "Bearing 6207 GB/T 276"), and purchasing buys them to the standard. In the digital factory they all have numbers and 3D models in the parts library: a CAD assembly calls them directly from the library, and the parts list and BOM carry the library number, so that the same part has the same "name" in design, purchasing and the warehouse.

---

**Engineer's notes**

- The purpose of conventional representation is to let the reader recognize at a glance what part it is and what size, without drawing the true shape; the size is conveyed by the designation and the parameter table.
- In 3D CAD, standard parts may be modeled in their true shape (the parts library does this), and when a 2D drawing is produced the software switches to conventional representation according to its settings; check the settings before producing the drawing.
- Gears and springs made in-house need part drawings, and the parameter table is indispensable: without it the workshop does not know how to make or inspect the part.
