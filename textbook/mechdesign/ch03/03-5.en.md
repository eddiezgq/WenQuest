### 3.5.1 The problem

The part drawing of SH-301 version B is finished. The workshop has to assemble the shaft, gear, sleeve, two bearings and two keys into the output shaft assembly SA-300. Which part goes on first, and where? Which parts must be clamped, and how is the assembly checked when it is complete? Purchasing needs to know how many of each part to buy. Part drawings cannot answer these questions; for that an **assembly drawing** is needed.

### 3.5.2 Content of an assembly drawing

An assembly drawing shows how the assembly works, how the parts are assembled to one another, and the structure and shape of the main parts. It contains:

1. **A set of views**: usually the front view is a section along the main assembly line (such as the axis);
2. **The necessary dimensions**: not every dimension of every part, only five kinds: specification (performance) dimensions, assembly dimensions (fit dimensions and important relative positions), mounting dimensions, overall dimensions and other important dimensions;
3. **Technical requirements**: requirements for the method of assembly, adjustment, inspection and testing;
4. **Item numbers, parts list and title block**.

Assembly drawings follow several rules of **conventional representation**. Contact faces and mating faces between two parts are drawn as a single line, while faces that do not touch are drawn as two lines, however small the gap. Adjacent parts are hatched in opposite directions or with different spacing. Solid shafts, keys, pins, bolts and the like are drawn unsectioned when cut along their axis. Rolling bearings, gears and the like are drawn in the conventional representation of Section 3.6.

**Example 3.5.1** The assembly drawing of SA-300 (version B) (Figure 3.5.1). The shaft is not sectioned, and the keys in their keyways are drawn in outline only; the gear and sleeve are sectioned and hatched in opposite directions; the upper halves of the two 6207 bearings are in conventional representation and the lower halves in general representation. The dimensions given are:

- Assembly dimensions: the fit between gear bore and shaft, Ø40 H7/r6 (an interference fit; above the fraction line is the tolerance class of the hole, below it that of the shaft), and the fit between the bearing inner rings and the shaft, Ø35k6 (the bearing is a standard part, so only the tolerance class of the shaft is given);
- Mounting dimensions: the distance between the two bearing centers, {{ex3_5.span:.0f}} mm, and the bearing outer diameter Ø72; the positions and diameters of the bearing bores in the housing must correspond to them;
- Overall dimensions: the tip diameter Ø{{ex3_5.d_a}} and the overall length {{ex3_5.L:.0f}} mm.

### 3.5.3 Item numbers and the parts list

Each kind of part is given an item number. A thin continuous leader is drawn from inside the visible outline of the part (starting with a small dot) and ends in a short horizontal line, on which the item number is written. The item numbers run in order clockwise or counterclockwise and are aligned horizontally or vertically. Identical parts receive a single item number, and the quantity is written in the parts list.

The **parts list** is drawn above the title block, with item numbers running from bottom to top. Each row gives the item number, the code (item code or standard number), the name, the quantity, the material and remarks. It and the **bill of materials** (BOM) are two forms of the same thing: the parts list is printed on the drawing for people to read, while the BOM is held in the ERP system for planning, purchasing and the workshop. The two must agree. The digital factory therefore generates the parts list from the BOM rather than writing each separately.

### 3.5.4 An assembly drawing is also a check

Drawing an assembly means assembling it once on paper (or in CAD). The problems found when SH-301 version A was reviewed in Chapter 33 — the left bearing seat narrower than the bearing, the key as long as the hub — are obvious as soon as the assembly is drawn: the bearing overhangs its seat, and the key sticks out of the hub.

Consider also RJ-201 of the collaborative-robot joint. Section 33.10 first fixed its dimensions by strength and stiffness: at the two ends a reducer flange (Ø70) and an upper-arm flange (Ø80), both integral with the shaft, and between the two Ø50 bearing seats a Ø55 shoulder. When the two 6010 bearings were "fitted" in the assembly drawing, it became clear that from the left they are blocked by the Ø70 flange, from the right by the Ø80 flange, and even with one flange removed the inner bearing cannot pass the Ø55 shoulder in the middle. The shaft cannot be assembled. Strength, stiffness and life were all calculated correctly, yet the structure did not work. This is exactly the kind of problem that is easily missed when one calculates but does not draw the assembly.

The remedy is to work out the assembly sequence. The reducer flange remains integral with the shaft and serves as the locating face of the inner bearing. The two bearings sit on a continuous Ø50 segment, and the shoulder between them is replaced by a spacer. The upper-arm flange becomes a separate part, RJ-202, fitted on a spline at the outer end of the shaft to transmit the torque. A round nut at the very end clamps RJ-202, the outer bearing, the spacer and the inner bearing together. Both bearings are fitted from the upper-arm end (Figure 33.10.2; Figure 3.2.1a shows the revised shaft). Section 33.10 recalculated stiffness, strength and life for the new structure; the conclusions did not change, and a check of the spline was added.

The assembly models of modern CAD can also check interference and simulate the assembly sequence automatically, but only if the part models and the BOM are correct, and only if someone asks, "How does it go in?"

**Engineer's notes**

- An assembly drawing gives only the five kinds of dimension; the detailed dimensions of the parts are in the part drawings and should not be repeated on the assembly drawing.
- The "code" column of the parts list holds the item code or standard number, not a name you have made up. The same part must have the same number on drawings, in the BOM, on purchase orders and in the warehouse.
- Assembly requirements must be checkable: "turns freely" is better written as a measurable quantity, for example "no-load starting torque not greater than a stated value".
